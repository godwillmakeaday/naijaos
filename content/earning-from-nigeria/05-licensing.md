# You Can Earn by Letting Others Use What You Create

By Paul A. Magaji

Someone may find my work useful enough to want it inside their own organisation.

They might want to use a guide in a training programme, adapt a worksheet for their staff or reproduce an article for a particular audience.

That creates a commercial possibility I want to understand: earning by giving others permission to use something I have created.

The work could travel further without requiring me to deliver every session or participate in every use. An organisation could pay for an agreed use while I continue developing the original work.

This is one direction I am exploring through my websites. I am not describing licensing income I have already established.

My legal background draws my attention to ownership and permission. The commercial starting point, however, is the usefulness of the work. Someone needs a reason to want it before the terms of using it become valuable.

## Create something another person wants to use

A licence needs something worth licensing.

That could be a publication, a teaching resource, an illustration, a collection of original research materials or software. The possibilities depend on what I create and what another person needs.

Consider a hypothetical example.

I develop a workshop resource that helps participants define a manageable first digital project. It contains explanations, exercises, fictional examples and facilitator notes.

An organisation running an enterprise programme might find the resource relevant to its participants. It could ask to use it in its own sessions.

The organisation’s interest would concern more than the existence of a document. It would need to assess whether the material fits its audience, whether its facilitators can use it and whether it offers something they would otherwise have to prepare.

That is where the commercial conversation begins.

## Identify the particular use

“Can we use your material?” sounds simple until I examine what it could mean.

An organisation might want to distribute a document to a small group. It might want to upload the same document to a public website. It might want to translate it, remove my name, change the examples or incorporate sections into something it sells.

Those requests involve different uses.

Before discussing a price, I need to understand what the other party wants to do.

Who will use the work? How will they receive it? Will it be reproduced, adapted or taught? Is the proposed use limited to one programme, or intended to continue?

These questions help turn a general expression of interest into something both sides can assess.

They also reveal whether I am comfortable with the proposed association and whether additional preparation is needed.

## Distinguish access from wider permission

Someone who obtains a resource may have a particular use in mind that goes beyond the access I intended to provide.

A person downloading a worksheet for their own project is in a different position from an organisation wanting to distribute it across several training centres.

I should make the intended arrangements understandable.

A product description can explain the use included with a purchase and provide a route for enquiries about broader use. The precise terms would need to suit the work and the circumstances.

This is partly a communication task.

If people cannot understand what they may do, some may hesitate to use the resource at all. Others may assume permission that I did not intend to offer.

Clarity helps the work travel with fewer misunderstandings.

## Know what I can authorise

Before offering permission, I need to examine what the work contains.

A resource may combine my original writing with photographs, quotations, illustrations, software components or material obtained elsewhere. My ability to authorise someone else’s use depends on the rights and permissions involved.

That requires care.

I would need to distinguish what I created, what I have permission to include and what may require separate arrangements. Keeping a record of sources and permissions during development would make that examination easier.

My legal training can help me recognise the questions. It does not remove the need to investigate the particular material and proposed use.

I should avoid making an ownership promise broader than I can support.

## Explain why licensing would help the buyer

Permission alone may sound abstract.

The buyer needs to understand what the arrangement allows them to accomplish.

A prepared teaching resource might reduce the work of building a programme from the beginning. A publication might help an organisation communicate a subject to its audience. A software component might support a function within a larger system.

Those benefits need evidence.

A sample, demonstration or trial use could help the organisation assess suitability. For teaching materials, facilitator instructions may matter as much as the participant pages. For a research resource, the dates, sources and limitations may affect whether it can be used responsibly.

The licence makes the agreed use possible. The quality and suitability of the underlying work give that permission its commercial value.

## Decide how much adaptation makes sense

An organisation may need changes before it can use a resource well.

Its audience may speak a different language, work in a different sector or need examples that reflect different circumstances.

Adaptation could improve the work’s usefulness. It could also change its meaning.

I would want to distinguish changes that can be made freely within the arrangement from changes that need discussion or review. If the material carries my name, I need to consider how alterations could affect what readers understand me to have said.

There may also be work for me to perform.

Preparing a new version, reviewing a translation or adjusting a workshop for a particular audience requires time and skill. That contribution should be recognised separately when defining the engagement.

The conversation may therefore involve both permission to use existing work and payment for additional work.

## Think carefully about exclusivity

A prospective buyer might want the resource for its exclusive use within an agreed setting.

That request deserves attention because it may limit what I can offer others.

I would need to understand the extent of the proposed restriction: the relevant material, audience, place, duration and type of use.

The commercial question is what I would be giving up.

An arrangement that prevents further opportunities may require a different assessment from one that allows several organisations to use the same resource.

I do not need to begin with a complex exclusive arrangement simply because it sounds substantial. A clearly defined initial use could provide a better opportunity to learn.

The appropriate choice depends on the work, the buyer and the wider direction I want to pursue.

## Set a price around the actual arrangement

There is no single price suggested by the word “licensing.”

A limited use of a short publication differs from a continuing arrangement involving extensive distribution, adaptation and support.

I would need to consider the scope of permission alongside the work required from me.

Does the buyer receive an existing version or continuing updates? Will I train their staff? Must I review adaptations? Are there reporting or administration requirements? Does the arrangement restrict opportunities elsewhere?

Those questions affect the commitment.

The fee needs to make sense for both the permitted use and the responsibilities attached to it. An attractive amount can become less attractive when the full obligation is understood.

I would want to establish those details before making a promise.

## Preserve an identifiable version

Materials change over time.

If an organisation uses my work, both sides should be able to identify which version the arrangement covers.

That matters when errors are corrected, examples are revised or information becomes outdated. A later edition may differ substantially from the one originally supplied.

A clear version record can help answer practical questions.

Which document did the organisation receive? What changes were made for its programme? Does the arrangement include later editions? Who should be told if a material correction becomes necessary?

These details may appear administrative, but they support dependable use.

A resource that travels beyond my immediate involvement needs enough documentation to remain understandable.

## Begin with a bounded use

I do not need to establish a large licensing operation before testing whether an organisation can use my work.

A first arrangement could cover one resource, one programme and a defined period.

That would allow me to observe what the organisation needs to use the material properly. Perhaps the instructions require improvement. Perhaps facilitators need an introductory session. Perhaps participants respond well to one exercise and struggle with another.

Those findings could improve the resource and the next offer.

I would also learn about the surrounding work: enquiries, preparation, delivery, questions, record-keeping and any agreed review.

The first arrangement should be small enough to understand and useful enough to teach me something.

## Let the work travel with its purpose intact

Licensing interests me because it creates a way for useful work to reach people through other organisations.

A resource developed in Nigeria could contribute to a programme elsewhere. A publication could reach an audience I would struggle to reach directly. An organisation could use a prepared method while I continue improving the underlying work.

For that possibility to acquire substance, I need to create something others can use, establish what I can authorise and define the proposed arrangement clearly.

My next step is to identify one original resource with a plausible organisational use and prepare it well enough for someone else to assess.

The opportunity begins when another person can see a place for my work in what they are trying to accomplish.
