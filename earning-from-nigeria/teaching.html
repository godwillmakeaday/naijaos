What You Know Can Help Someone Else Learn

What should a learner become able to do?

By Paul A. Magaji · 9 min read · Commercial exploration

There are things I understand today because someone took the trouble to explain them.

Sometimes the explanation came through a teacher. Sometimes it came through a book, a conversation or a practical demonstration. In each case, knowledge became more useful because someone found a way to make it accessible.

That creates another possibility for earning through useful work.

Someone may be willing to pay me to help them understand a subject, develop a skill or complete a task they cannot yet manage independently.

My experience as an English teacher gives this possibility a personal connection. My legal training and my attempts to build websites give me further subjects to examine. But knowing something and teaching it effectively are separate accomplishments.

If I want to earn through teaching, I need to become attentive to what happens in the learner.

I am exploring this as a direction for my work. It is not a claim that I have already established a successful international teaching business.

Begin with something the learner wants to do

A subject can be large enough to make a course sound impressive while leaving its purpose unclear.

“Understanding technology” covers too much for a first workshop. Helping someone prepare a clear brief for a first website gives the learning a more manageable destination.

The distinction matters because the learner brings a purpose of their own.

They may want to perform a task, understand a responsibility or make a decision with greater confidence. They may need enough knowledge to work more effectively with someone they have engaged.

I need to identify that purpose before deciding what to teach.

What should the participant be able to explain, assess or do afterwards? What difficulty is preventing them from doing it now?

Those questions give the teaching a direction.

Choose a starting point you can serve responsibly

My interests extend beyond the subjects in which I have the strongest experience.

That is useful for learning. It requires judgment when deciding what to teach.

I can discuss an investigation while still making clear that it is unfinished. I can document an attempt without presenting myself as an expert in every tool involved. A structured course, however, asks learners to rely on my selection and explanation of the material.

I need a sound basis for that responsibility.

An appropriate starting point may be a task I have performed repeatedly, a subject I have studied carefully or a process I can demonstrate and explain accurately.

Where my knowledge is still developing, the offering needs to reflect that. A discussion group examining a question makes a different promise from a workshop teaching an established procedure.

The description should let participants understand what kind of learning they are joining.

Understand what the learner already knows

An explanation can be accurate and still miss its audience.

A beginner may need ordinary terms defined before the main idea becomes accessible. Someone with experience may need help with a particular difficulty rather than an introduction to the entire subject.

People also arrive with different assumptions.

A participant learning to commission a website might believe that paying for the initial build includes every future change. Another might not yet distinguish a domain name from hosting. A third may understand those matters but struggle to describe the site’s purpose.

Those starting points affect the lesson.

Before preparing the material, I would want to understand the intended audience well enough to recognise what requires explanation and what can reasonably be assumed.

Otherwise, I risk teaching the lesson I want to give rather than the lesson they need.

Organise the learning into a workable sequence

When I understand a subject, its parts may appear connected in ways a newcomer cannot yet see.

Teaching requires making those connections available gradually.

For a workshop on preparing a website brief, I might begin with the intended audience and the task the site should support. Pages, content and functions would follow from those decisions. Costs and maintenance would then have a clearer context.

Starting with a list of technical features could leave participants selecting things before understanding why they need them.

Sequence is part of the service.

Each stage should give the learner enough understanding to approach the next one. The material needs room for explanation, examples and questions.

A short workshop can only carry so much. Choosing what to leave for later may be necessary to help participants learn the essentials properly.

Give people an opportunity to attempt the work

Listening to an explanation can produce a feeling of understanding.

Attempting the task reveals more.

A participant might agree that a project needs a clear audience and still struggle to describe their own. Someone may understand an example while being unsure how to apply the method to different circumstances.

That is where an exercise becomes useful.

I could ask learners to prepare a short brief, examine a fictional scenario or explain a distinction in their own words. Their attempts would show where the explanation had become usable and where further help was needed.

The exercise should fit the purpose.

If the promised outcome is the ability to assess a document, recalling definitions alone would provide limited evidence. Participants need an opportunity to examine something and explain their judgment.

Teaching becomes more valuable when it helps people move from recognition to application.

Make feedback specific enough to help

A learner needs to know more than whether an answer is right or wrong.

They may need to understand which part of their reasoning is sound, what they have overlooked and how to improve the next attempt.

That requires attention.

If a participant produces an unclear project brief, I could identify the point at which the intended audience becomes vague or where two different tasks have been combined. A focused comment gives them something they can revise.

This also affects the size and format of the offering.

A recorded course can provide examples and self-check exercises. A small live workshop can allow more individual discussion. A programme promising detailed feedback requires time to examine participants’ work.

The offer needs to explain what kind of support is included and whether I can provide it dependably.

Let the format follow the learning need

Teaching can take several forms.

A short live session may help people work through one question. A workshop can combine explanation with practice. A recorded lesson can allow someone to pause and revisit the material. A longer programme may support skills that require repeated attempts and feedback.

Each form has its own demands.

A live session depends on scheduling and participation. Recorded material needs instructions that remain clear without immediate clarification. Continuing programmes require a level of availability I must be able to sustain.

The learner’s circumstances matter too.

What device will they use? How much uninterrupted time do they have? Can the materials be downloaded? Is the exercise practical within the resources available to them?

Working from Nigeria makes me conscious of those questions, although I should investigate the actual audience rather than assume everyone shares my circumstances.

Explain what the fee covers

A teaching offer should describe the experience clearly enough for someone to assess it.

The participant needs to know the subject, intended level, format, duration and expected preparation. They should understand whether materials, exercises, feedback or continuing access are included.

The outcome also needs careful wording.

I can undertake to teach a method, provide practice and respond within an agreed scope. A participant’s later success will depend on factors that extend beyond the session.

My legal background makes me attentive to the relationship between a promise and what can actually be delivered. That attention can help me describe the engagement responsibly.

The quality of the teaching still has to justify the description.

Test a small lesson before building a large course

Preparing an extensive course can feel productive before I know how learners respond.

A smaller session could provide better information.

I could select one audience, one task and a manageable learning outcome. Afterwards, I would examine what participants were able to do, where they needed help and whether the session addressed a difficulty they considered important.

Their comments would matter. So would their work.

Did they understand the instructions? Could they complete the exercise? Which questions kept returning? Did the material assume too much or spend too long on something they already knew?

Those observations could guide the next version.

They might support a fuller programme. They might also show that a short workshop is sufficient, or that the problem needs a resource people can consult individually.

Allow teaching to deepen the wider work

Teaching could connect naturally with my writing and other projects.

An article may introduce a question. A worksheet may help a reader organise an attempt. A workshop may provide the explanation and feedback needed to use the method more confidently.

The questions participants ask can then improve future articles and resources.

That relationship interests me because it keeps the work in contact with people trying to use it.

It also exposes gaps in my own understanding. A question I struggle to answer may reveal an assumption I have never examined or a subject requiring further study.

Teaching can become part of how I develop my knowledge, provided I remain honest about what I know and willing to correct the material.

Make the learning worth carrying away

The possibility of earning through teaching begins with knowledge, but it asks for more.

I need to understand the learner, define a useful purpose, organise the material and create an opportunity to practise. I need some way to assess whether the explanation has helped.

A participant should leave with something they can carry into their own work: a clearer distinction, a better question, a completed attempt or a skill they can continue developing.

That is the contribution I would want someone to pay for.

My next step is to choose one task I can teach responsibly and prepare a small lesson around it. Then I need to observe whether another person becomes more capable through the experience.

What I know becomes a teaching opportunity when I can help someone else make use of it.
