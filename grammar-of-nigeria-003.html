Federation and Federalism: Why Is Power Divided in Nigeria?

Before demanding that an authority solve a problem, we should know who holds the power, the duty and the resources.

Paul MagajiLong-form civic education3,170 words

The Broken Road and the Wrong Question

A road has become impassable. Traders struggle to move their goods. Children arrive late at school. Motorists complain about the damage to their vehicles. A community asks its elected representatives why nothing has been done.

Almost immediately, arguments begin. Some blame the president. Others blame the governor. Others insist that the local government chairman ought to act. Someone recalls that a legislator promised to intervene. Another person asks whether a government agency has already received money for the road.

It is possible that some of these complaints are justified. It is also possible that the public discussion has begun with a question that should have come later.

Before asking who has failed, have we established who has the power, the duty, the money and the capacity to act?

This is the practical problem at the heart of federalism. Nigeria is not governed by one undivided public authority. It is a federation: a political and constitutional arrangement in which governing powers are distributed across levels of government.

That arrangement influences ordinary life. It shapes how laws are made, how public money is shared, which institutions can act, and whom a citizen should question when public services fail.

Yet the word federalism is frequently used as if it were itself a political programme. Some invoke it to demand more state power. Others invoke national unity to defend stronger federal authority. Still others use restructuring, devolution and true federalism as though the words explained themselves.

They do not. This essay is an attempt to acquire the distinctions that should come before our conclusions.

1. One Country Does Not Mean One Government

The first distinction is between a unitary state and a federal state.

In a unitary system, the central legal order is the ultimate source of authority for subnational institutions; powers may be decentralised, but the arrangement is not necessarily constitutionally protected in the same way as a federation's division of powers.

In a federation, the constitution establishes a distribution of authority between a central government and constituent units. Neither level is supposed to receive every important power merely at the discretion of the other. Each has a legal sphere of action, though their powers may overlap and the constitution may give national laws priority in specified circumstances.

Federalism is therefore not simply the existence of governors or local administrators. A country may have regional offices without being a federation. Nor is federalism merely a cultural recognition that people are different.

It is, at its core, a method of organising political power under a common constitutional order.

Nigeria is one sovereign country. It is not thirty-six separate sovereign countries joined by a convenient agreement. But it also has thirty-six states whose political offices and legislative responsibilities exist within the constitutional structure, not simply as branches of the presidency.

This creates two facts that must be held together: Nigeria is one state in international law, and power within that state is constitutionally divided.

2. Why Divide Power at All?

Why not give all authority to the centre, where national decisions could be made uniformly? Or, conversely, why not allow each constituent state to make almost every decision for itself?

The answer is not purely technical. Division of power involves competing judgments about democracy, diversity, efficiency, solidarity and the prevention of abuse.

One argument for federalism begins with proximity. A government closer to a community may understand its practical problems more readily and may be easier for residents to scrutinise. A state government may discover solutions suited to its geography, population or local economy without waiting for an identical national answer.

Another argument begins with difference. Where communities have distinct histories, languages, economic conditions and priorities, a federal arrangement can create space for variation without requiring the dissolution of a common country.

A third argument concerns the concentration of power. Dividing responsibility can make it more difficult for a single political centre to control every decision that affects citizens.

But each argument has a corresponding difficulty.

A local authority can be corrupt or intolerant just as a national authority can. Proximity does not automatically produce accountability. Variation can become unequal treatment. Dividing powers can create disputes, duplication and a tendency for one government to blame another.

Federalism is therefore neither a guarantee of freedom nor an explanation for every government failure. It is an institutional arrangement with promises, trade-offs and dangers. Whether it works well depends on its rules and on the behaviour of those who operate them.

3. How Nigeria's Constitution Divides Authority

Section 2(2) of the Constitution of the Federal Republic of Nigeria 1999, as amended, establishes Nigeria as a federation consisting of states and a Federal Capital Territory. Section 3 identifies the thirty-six states. The constitutional distribution of lawmaking powers is developed chiefly in section 4 and the Second Schedule. [1]

Three categories are especially useful to understand.

Exclusive matters. The National Assembly has the legislative authority over subjects placed on the Exclusive Legislative List, subject to the constitutional text. These matters are not ordinarily open to independent state legislation. Federalism, then, does not mean that every state can legislate on everything within its borders.

Concurrent matters. The Constitution permits federal and state legislative action in prescribed fields. It is not enough to read the heading of the Concurrent Legislative List; one must also read the particular provision to see the extent of each level's authority.

Residual matters. Where a subject is not constitutionally reserved to the federal legislature and no other constitutional rule changes the result, it generally falls within the states' legislative competence. This category is often described as residual, although it should not be mistaken for an unrestricted power to disregard the Constitution.

Section 4(5) supplies another important principle: when a state law conflicts with a law validly made by the National Assembly, the valid federal law prevails to the extent of the inconsistency. The word validly matters. The existence of a federal statute does not, by itself, settle whether the National Assembly had constitutional authority to enact it.

For the citizen, these rules provide a method of investigation. Do not merely ask which level of government claims authority. Ask what provision creates the power, how far that power extends, and whether another level has a lawful role as well.

4. Federalism Changes: Electricity and Railways as Examples

The constitutional distribution of authority is not frozen for all time.

In March 2023, constitutional alterations changed the legislative landscape. One amendment removed railways from the Exclusive Legislative List and introduced a defined concurrent arrangement. Another removed the limitation that had restricted state electricity legislation in relation to areas covered by the national grid. [2]

The significance is not that each state suddenly gained unlimited control over the entire railway or electricity system. Federal and state powers must still be read according to the amended provisions and any applicable valid legislation.

The more important lesson is conceptual: when powers are constitutionally redistributed, the set of institutions capable of taking initiative changes.

A state that previously faced a constitutional restriction may acquire additional room to plan, legislate, attract investment or regulate aspects of a sector. Whether it makes good use of that room is a separate question.

Devolution of authority creates an opportunity for performance; it does not create performance by itself.

The distinction matters because a proposal to transfer powers is only the beginning of a serious policy argument. We must still ask whether the receiving institutions have technical competence, financial means, safeguards, and the administrative ability to exercise the powers responsibly.

5. Three Questions Hidden Inside Every Public Problem

Return to the damaged road.

The first question is legal responsibility. What sort of road is it, and which public authority has responsibility for its development or maintenance under applicable law and administrative arrangements? A road's location in a state does not automatically make it a state road, just as a road running through a town is not necessarily a local-government road.

The second question is financial responsibility. Has money been appropriated? Which budget contains the project? Was it funded, procured, delayed or abandoned? The institution with a legal role may lack adequate funds, while another institution may finance a project through an authorised intervention.

The third question is operational responsibility. Who commissioned the work? Which agency supervises it? What contract, timetable, standard and reporting mechanism apply?

Sometimes these three questions lead to the same office. Sometimes they lead to different institutions.

This is why blaming every failure on the most visible political figure can be analytically satisfying but institutionally inaccurate. It is also why a public official's claim that a matter belongs to another level of government should not automatically be accepted.

Citizens need to be able to follow responsibility through law, money and implementation. A federal system makes that competence particularly valuable.

6. Local Government: Government Close to the People, or Government Between Powers?

Nigeria's local-government system illustrates why constitutional vocabulary must be matched with institutional reality.

Section 7 guarantees a system of democratically elected local-government councils, while providing for state laws on their establishment, structure, composition, finance and functions, subject to the Constitution. The Fourth Schedule identifies functions to be conferred upon councils. Section 162 contains provisions concerning public revenues and local-government allocations. [1]

These arrangements create important questions about the relationship between state governments and local councils. A council may be described as the level of government closest to the people, yet its capacity to act depends on elections, finances, legal powers and institutional autonomy.

On 11 July 2024, the Supreme Court, in Attorney-General of the Federation v. Attorney-General of Abia State & 35 Others (SC/CV/343/2024), ruled against the retention and use by states of funds meant for local councils, and supported direct payment of allocations to democratically elected councils. It also addressed the unlawful displacement of elected councils by appointed arrangements. [3]

That was a significant legal development. But a court's decision and its implementation are not the same event. Reporting in August 2026 described continuing disputes and uneven practice concerning council finances and autonomy. Such reporting should be distinguished from the binding judgment itself. [4]

The deeper question remains: what makes local government genuinely capable of governing? A constitutional guarantee, a revenue allocation, an election, independent administration, and practical accountability each address different parts of that question.

Financial autonomy without public scrutiny can merely relocate opportunities for misuse. Political control without adequate financial independence can make elected councils ceremonial. A serious account of local government must examine both dangers.

7. Money Is Part of the Constitution of Power

A government may have authority to make decisions yet lack the resources to carry them out.

This is why political federalism cannot be understood without fiscal federalism: the arrangement through which public revenue is raised, shared, borrowed, budgeted and spent across levels of government.

Section 162 establishes the Federation Account and provides a constitutional framework for revenue allocation. The law also recognises public finances and fiscal responsibilities at the state and local levels. [1]

The practical question is not simply, How much money does a state receive? It is also, How much can it lawfully raise, how much of its budget is already committed, what obligations must it meet, and how efficiently does it turn resources into results?

A state may demand additional responsibility while resisting fiscal discipline. A federal authority may claim national responsibility without putting in place a workable financing or implementation arrangement.

Neither position can be evaluated merely through a slogan about centralisation or restructuring.

In examining a proposed reform, citizens should look for the complete chain: legal power, sustainable revenue, administrative capacity, independent oversight, and measurable outcomes.

If one link is missing, redistribution of power may change the location of failure rather than its underlying causes.

8. Does Federalism Protect Diversity—or Simply Move Domination Closer?

One moral argument for federalism is that different communities should have room to express their priorities within a shared country.

But the word state can mislead us here. A constituent state is not always a culturally uniform community. Within each Nigerian state are people of different histories, faiths, languages, economic interests and political loyalties.

If more authority is transferred to a state government, does that necessarily increase the freedom of every person living within that state? Not unless the rights of minorities, opposition voices, migrants and less influential communities remain protected.

The mere movement of authority from Abuja to a state capital is not the same thing as the movement of accountability toward ordinary people.

Imagine a reform that gives governors a new power. Supporters may reasonably hope for faster decisions and better local knowledge. Critics may reasonably fear that the same authority could be used to punish opponents or marginalise vulnerable communities.

Both questions deserve attention. What problem does the new power solve? What prevents its abuse? Which courts or oversight bodies can intervene? How will residents challenge decisions made in their name?

A mature federalism must think not only about the rights of states against the centre, but also about the rights of persons against every level of government.

9. The State Police Debate as a Test of Federal Reasoning

Policing offers a useful contemporary example of why the language of federalism must remain precise.

Under section 214 of the Constitution as set out in the 2023 revised text, the Nigeria Police Force is the constitutionally established police force, with the existing provision restricting other police forces. [1]

By October 2026, a proposed Sixth Alteration to establish state police services had progressed through the federal legislative process and received reported approvals from at least the constitutionally required number of state legislatures. Reports dated 23–26 October described further formal steps toward presidential assent; they did not establish that the proposal had already become operative constitutional law. [5]

Supporters of state policing argue that institutions closer to communities may possess better intelligence and respond more quickly to local security conditions. Opponents raise concerns about possible politicisation, discriminatory enforcement and the use of coercive power against dissenters.

These are not arguments to dismiss by attaching labels to their supporters.

The more serious inquiry is institutional: what safeguards would separate operational policing from partisan control? How would personnel be recruited, trained, funded and disciplined? What protection would exist for minorities and political opponents? How would federal and state authorities coordinate when crimes cross state boundaries?

Even where one favours or opposes the reform, those questions remain necessary. A policy position becomes stronger when it faces the most serious difficulties in its own preferred design.

10. Federalism Is Not an Excuse to Stop Thinking

There are two easy intellectual shortcuts in Nigerian debates.

One is to say that every major problem comes from excessive centralisation. The other is to say that any transfer of power threatens national unity.

Both statements may capture legitimate concerns in particular circumstances. Neither is a satisfactory method of analysis.

A federal arrangement can permit policy experimentation, give room to local priorities, and reduce the concentration of decision-making. It can also produce unequal capacity, overlapping authority and disputes over responsibility.

A national institution can help uphold common standards, protect rights across state boundaries and coordinate large systems. It can also become remote, inflexible or excessively dominant.

The task is not to choose one level of government as permanently virtuous and another as permanently suspect.

It is to decide which public functions require nationwide coordination, which benefit from state initiative, which belong close to communities, and what mechanisms of cooperation and review are required.

And it is to understand that federalism and good governance are not synonyms. Federalism is a design of authority. Good governance is an achievement that must still be demonstrated.

Conclusion: The Real Question Is Not Only Who Has Power

When a community asks who will repair its road, it is asking a practical question. But beneath that question is an entire constitutional system.

Who may legislate? Who may act? Who receives revenue? Who approves spending? Who administers the project? Who can be challenged when something goes wrong?

A federation separates or shares answers to those questions among different institutions.

This division can protect liberty and accommodate difference. It can also make the path to accountability difficult for citizens who do not know how power is organised.

The citizen's task is not to memorise every schedule to the Constitution. It is to acquire a disciplined habit of inquiry:

Find the authority. Follow the money. Identify the institution. Examine the result.

The most productive question in Nigerian political argument may not be, Should power belong to Abuja or to the states?

It may be: Which arrangement of power is most capable of delivering justice, competence, freedom and accountability—and how would we know?

Federalism does not eliminate the need to answer that question. It makes the question unavoidable.

Questions for the Reader

  1. What is the difference between a federal state and a unitary state that merely decentralises administration?
  2. When federal and state governments both claim responsibility for a policy, how can a citizen determine the lawful division of power?
  3. Is transferring a public function to a state government necessarily the same as bringing it closer to ordinary citizens?
  4. What evidence would demonstrate that a state is prepared to exercise a newly devolved power effectively?
  5. Can a federal arrangement protect diversity while still allowing minorities within individual states to suffer exclusion?
  6. In evaluating local-government autonomy, how should legal authority, financial independence and accountability be measured separately?
  7. What safeguards would be necessary before a new state-level coercive power could be considered acceptable?
  8. Where public responsibility is shared, how can citizens prevent every authority from blaming another?
