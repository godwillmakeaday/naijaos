Is the POS business still profitable in Nigeria?

The 2026 economics. Float, charges, daily revenue ranges, the multi-surface rule, and the failure modes most agents discover too late. A working diagnostic for anyone considering — or already inside — the agent banking trade.

Contents

01The frame

The question is not whether POS is profitable.

The question is whether this version of the POS business — in this location, at this float level, with these surfaces — is profitable for this operator, given the conditions documented in the pillar briefing.

The standard answer to "is POS still profitable" is yes or no, and both are wrong. Profitability is not a property of the trade. It is a property of the configuration the operator brings to the trade — and the configuration that worked in 2022 does not work in 2026.

The pillar briefing — how money actually works in Nigeria — names the three forces moving income outcomes in 2026: the fuel transmission, the FX convergence, and the cash cycle. POS sits inside the third. It is the most stable of the three forces. It is also the most saturated. The market matured while the advice columns kept calling it new.

This post is the diagnostic the pillar gestures at. It treats POS as a configuration problem, not a yes/no question.

A POS business is not a business. It is a position taken on the cash cycle, the float requirement, and the operator's ability to absorb a slow week.
02The short answer

Yes — with three conditions.

The POS business in Nigeria is profitable in 2026 if three conditions hold. The number of operators meeting all three is smaller than the headline industry numbers suggest.

One: the location is not saturated. The official industry estimate of roughly 1.9 million terminals nationwide means most easy locations — the corner of every busy street, every market entrance, every bus stop — are taken. New entrants in saturated locations face structural underperformance regardless of effort.

Two: the operator runs multiple surfaces. Withdrawal-only agents earn one stream of commission. Multi-surface agents — withdrawals plus airtime, transfers, utility payments, and increasingly bills — earn two to three times the same daily traffic. The differentiator is no longer location alone. It is what happens at the location.

Three: the float is separated from the operator's personal money. This is the rule that most determines whether the business survives its first slow week. Agents who treat the float as a liquidity reserve for personal expenses do not last. Agents who treat the float as untouchable working capital — separated, replenished, never borrowed against — survive cycles that destroy the others.

Everything in the rest of this post is the texture behind those three conditions.

03The numbers

The economics, in 2026 ranges.

The figures below are working ranges drawn from operator interviews, agent-bank disclosures, and field reports across major Nigerian cities in early 2026. They are not point estimates. The actual numbers in any specific location will sit somewhere inside these ranges — or, in saturated areas, below them.

Charges, commission rates, settlement windows, and daily limits change frequently. Confirm current figures with at least two providers — Moniepoint, OPay, PalmPay, Baxi/Paga — before committing capital. The ranges below are diagnostic, not prescriptive.

Three observations matter more than the figures themselves.

The gross-to-net spread is not small. An agent doing ₦10,000 gross on a typical day will not take home ₦10,000. After charges, data, the rent share if the location is paid for, and the small daily contribution toward float top-up, the take-home figure is usually 50 to 70 percent of gross. Operators who model their business on gross numbers are setting themselves up to fail at the cash-out stage.

Break-even is a transaction count, not a naira figure. A single-surface withdrawal-only agent needs roughly 18 to 25 transactions per day to clear costs. In saturated locations, that count is no longer reliable. In high-traffic locations, multi-surface agents pass the count well before the day is half over — and everything after is margin.

Float is rotated, not spent. The starter float figure is not capital that disappears. It is the working buffer the agent rotates between cash-in and cash-out cycles. The number that matters is not how much float is in the box at the end of the day, but how many days the float survives without external top-up. Three days is fragile. Seven days is workable. Fourteen days is solid.

04The surfaces

Why multi-surface agents earn two to three times the same traffic.

The pillar's P·02 — multiple revenue surfaces applies to POS more cleanly than to almost any other Nigerian income path. A surface is a service line that the same physical location, the same terminal time, and the same customer relationship can generate revenue from. Stacking surfaces is what separates a sustainable agent from a marginal one.

SURFACE · 01

Withdrawals

The base service. Customers cash out from accounts using ATM cards on the POS terminal. Margins are thin per transaction; volume is everything. The surface that built the industry — and the surface that no longer pays the bills alone.

Share of single-surface agent revenue · ~ 100 %
SURFACE · 02

Transfers

Bank-to-bank transfers initiated through the agent's app or terminal. Higher per-transaction commission than withdrawals. Many customers prefer agent-initiated transfers over their own banking apps when network is slow or app trust is low.

Lifts multi-surface revenue by · ~ 30 – 50 %
SURFACE · 03

Airtime & data

Recharge cards, instant top-ups for MTN, Airtel, Glo, 9mobile. Data bundles. Lower per-unit commission than financial services, but volume is high and customers buy daily. Smooths the revenue across days when withdrawal traffic is slow.

Smoothing impact · slow-day floor
SURFACE · 04

Bills & utilities

Electricity tokens, cable subscriptions, water bills, school fees, government payments where local. Higher commission than airtime, more concentrated traffic — most customers pay bills monthly, not daily. The surface that distinguishes a neighbourhood agent from a generic one.

Commission band · premium

An agent running all four surfaces earns roughly two to three times the daily revenue of an agent running only Surface 01, on the same foot traffic. The lift does not come from a single surface dominating — it comes from the surfaces compensating for each other. On a day when withdrawals are slow, airtime and bills carry the floor. On a day when bills are quiet, transfers and withdrawals do the lifting.

This is the structural reason the POS industry has not collapsed despite saturation. The operators who adapted from one surface to four have absorbed most of the maturing market's pressure. The operators who stayed on one surface are the ones quietly closing.

05How agents fail

The four failure modes — and the death spiral.

Most POS agents who close did not fail at running a POS. They failed at running the financial architecture around it. The pillar's § 04 names this in general terms; here it is in operational terms specific to the trade.

M · 01

The float-personal blend.

The single most common failure. The agent treats the float as one pool of money, dipping into it for personal needs because "the money is there". A school fee, a hospital bill, a family obligation. The float drops below the level required to serve the location's typical demand. Customers begin to be turned away. The location's reputation as a reliable agent decays. The slow week becomes a slow month. The business closes.

The correction is structural, not behavioural. Float must live in a separate account or a separate physical container. If the agent has to think about whether to take from float, the architecture has already failed.

M · 02

The borrowed top-up.

A variant of M·01. The agent runs out of float, borrows to top up — from a money-lender, an aggregator, a friend at high effective rates. The borrowed top-up restores operations for a week. The repayment terms outpace the daily margin the business can produce. The agent ends the month with the float still short and the debt now larger than the original gap.

The correction: a buffer reserved for float top-up — separate from operating margin, separate from personal money — built before scaling, not after a crisis arrives.

M · 03

Single-surface stagnation.

The agent operates withdrawals only. For months it works. Then a new agent opens fifty metres away, a bank cash point gets installed nearby, or a fintech app captures the demographic. Withdrawal volume drops 30 percent over a quarter. Without secondary surfaces, there is no compensating revenue. The business does not collapse; it bleeds quietly until it is no longer worth running.

The correction is to add surfaces before they are needed, not after the primary one weakens.

M · 04

The location decay curve.

Saturation does not announce itself. It arrives gradually as new agents open, as customer routines shift, as the location's reputation as the reliable agent fades. By the time the operator notices that daily revenue is half what it was a year ago, the trend has been running for six months and three competitors have established themselves within the same catchment.

The correction: monthly revenue tracking, location-level competitor scans, and the willingness to relocate a terminal that has clearly entered decay rather than waiting for it to recover.

The death spiral. The four failure modes rarely arrive separately. They arrive in sequence. Float-personal blend creates the first shortage; the borrowed top-up papers it over; the operator becomes too cash-stressed to invest in additional surfaces; saturation arrives during the same window. Twelve months from start to close. The agent did not fail at one thing. The architecture failed at four.

06Before you start

Eight questions to answer before the first terminal arrives.

If three or more of these questions cannot be answered with a clear yes, the position is not yet ready to take. The cost of waiting two months to set the architecture properly is smaller than the cost of failing six months in.

Have I scouted the location at three different times of day for at least a week?

Foot traffic at 8 a.m., 1 p.m., and 6 p.m. tells a different story than a single visit. Saturation reveals itself in the gaps between peaks.

Do I know how many existing agents operate within a 200-metre radius?

Three or more in a typical neighbourhood is saturated. One or two is contestable. Zero is either an opportunity or a sign that the location does not support the trade.

Is my float in a separate account or container, with a written rule against personal use?

The rule is the architecture. The architecture is the business. Without it, every other decision compounds into eventual failure.

Have I budgeted for at least 60 days of fixed costs beyond the float?

Rent share, data, charges, daily personal allowance. The buffer is what carries the business through the first month before traffic patterns settle.

Do I know the charge structure of at least two providers in detail?

Moniepoint, OPay, PalmPay, Baxi/Paga each charge and settle differently. Picking a provider on signage familiarity rather than economics is a common entry mistake.

Have I planned my Surface 02 and Surface 03 from day one?

Multi-surface is not a phase-two decision. It is a launch decision. Adding surfaces three months in is harder than starting with them and growing volume.

Do I have a backup terminal or a backup provider?

Provider downtime is real. An agent unable to transact for half a day on a strong day loses more than the day's margin — they lose the trust of customers who walk to the next agent and may not return.

Have I read the pillar briefing on how money actually works in Nigeria?

The conditions in B/01 — fuel, FX, cash — shape the environment this trade operates inside. Skipping the briefing is starting a business without reading the weather.

07FAQ

Common questions this playbook keeps getting asked.

Q · 01

Is the POS business still profitable in Nigeria in 2026?

Yes, but only for operators who treat it as a multi-surface business — withdrawals plus airtime, transfers, utility payments, and increasingly bills. Single-surface withdrawal-only agents in saturated locations are no longer reliably profitable once float costs, charges, and rent are deducted. The trade still works; the configuration that made it work has changed.

Q · 02

How much float do you need to start a POS business in Nigeria?

A working starter float typically sits in the ₦150,000 to ₦400,000 range for a single-terminal agent, depending on the daily withdrawal demand of the location. Agents serving high-traffic markets often run floats of ₦500,000 and above. Float is not capital — it is rotated, not spent — but it is the buffer between the agent and a customer turned away.

Q · 03

How much can a POS agent earn per day in Nigeria?

Daily gross revenue for a single-terminal agent ranges from around ₦3,000 on slow days to ₦15,000 on strong days in typical neighbourhood locations. Multi-surface agents in high-traffic locations can clear higher. Net of charges, data, rent share, and float top-up costs, take-home is generally 50 to 70 percent of gross — the spread depends on operator discipline more than location.

Q · 04

What kills most POS businesses in Nigeria?

The death spiral is consistent: a slow week, a personal expense, the operator borrows from the float, the float runs short, customers are turned away, the location loses its reputation as a reliable agent, the slow week becomes a slow month. The business did not fail. The financial separation between operator and float was missing from the start.

Q · 05

Which POS provider is best for new agents in 2026?

The four dominant providers — Moniepoint, OPay, PalmPay, and Baxi/Paga — each have different charge structures, settlement timing, and support quality. The best choice depends on the location and the surfaces the agent intends to operate. Most successful agents run two terminals from different providers as redundancy against downtime; relying on a single provider is a structural exposure that catches operators on the day it matters most.

Q · 06

Can I run a POS business as a side hustle?

Possible but harder than it appears. POS rewards consistent presence — customers return to agents who are always there. A part-time agent loses traffic to a full-time agent in the same catchment over weeks, not months. The side-hustle version works best when run by a trusted operator on the agent's behalf, with the agent providing capital, terminal, and float top-ups while the operator runs the daily location. The economics tighten because the operator must be paid; the model only works if the location supports the additional cost.
