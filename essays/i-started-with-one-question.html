I Started with One Question

How a question about selling online became an inquiry into Nigeria.

By Paul A. Magaji · 7 min read · How a world becomes visible

Personal essay

Download Word document

The question was practical. I wanted to sell something on the internet, and I wanted to know what Nigerians would buy.

I did not begin with a complete theory of the country. I had no research department, no subscription to a large market database, and no settled picture of the market I hoped to enter. I had questions and access to a few tools with which to pursue them.

So I asked ChatGPT. I asked Grok and Claude. I also tried DeepSeek. I approached the question from different directions, hoping that another answer might reveal something I had missed.

What do Nigerians need? What information will they pay for? What can someone like me create that would be useful enough to purchase?

The answers often returned to earning money, practical problems, and the urgency of making a living. I could recognise the setting they described. But I could also feel a distance between that description and the work I wanted to do.

I had developed intellectual interests that mattered to me. I wanted to understand institutions, power, production, and the conditions under which people become capable of doing more. I was looking for a commercial opening, but I did not want to abandon those interests simply because the easiest answer seemed to lie elsewhere.

That tension kept the question alive.

***

There is a difficulty hidden inside the phrase “what Nigerians will buy.”

It makes the market sound as though it is waiting in one place, ready to be described. But which Nigerians? People living where? With what income, responsibilities, access, and confidence in the seller?

Even the difference between needing something and buying it is substantial. A person may need a service but lack the money. Someone else may have the money but see no reason to trust the offer. Another may already solve the problem through a relationship that an outsider has failed to notice.

I began to realise that I needed a better account of the people and circumstances behind the answer.

Repeated answers from AI could help me generate possibilities. They could not, by repetition alone, tell me that a market existed. To establish that, I would have to meet the world outside the conversation: ask people, examine what they already used, make an offer, and observe what happened.

The tools could help me think. I still had to learn how to investigate.

***

I had encountered the sale of information much earlier.

A friend introduced me to Success Digest. I followed it closely and became absorbed in its world. Here was a proposition I could understand: learn something useful, organise it, and make it available to people who could benefit from it.

Useful information could be a product. A person could take a problem seriously enough to produce something another person would pay to read or use.

That discovery mattered. It gave me a way to connect learning with enterprise.

But later I discovered how much economic activity had been developing beyond my attention. There were startups, software businesses, and companies working on problems I had scarcely considered commercially.

I was already a lawyer. Some of those businesses needed precisely the kinds of professional understanding I might have developed. Yet I had not seen the opportunity clearly enough to pursue it.

I had been attentive within one world and poorly informed about another.

This became one of the questions that would eventually belong to NaijaOS: how does a person discover the economy in which his abilities might become useful?

We often ask whether someone has skills. I also want to ask whether that person can see where the skills could lead, what additional knowledge is required, and how to reach the people who might need them.

***

My own picture of Nigeria kept encountering contradictions.

I grew up in Jos with a strong concern for dependable employment. As a boy, I thought a government job might be essential to a secure life. Later, I became interested in enterprise and productive independence.

But familiar descriptions of enterprise were not always reliable guides.

During visits connected with legal work in Abia, I heard Enugu described as a civil service state. I also heard people discuss its commercial prospects and the role of investment by people who had gone abroad.

Those accounts made me think about the businesspeople from Enugu whom I had encountered elsewhere. How did their activity fit the description I was hearing?

Conversations about Aba opened another line of inquiry. I was told about production taking place in very small workshops, sometimes in a single room.

I could not establish the scale or performance of an entire manufacturing economy from those conversations. But I could recognise a question worth following.

How much productive capacity can a small space hold when it is connected to the right skills, suppliers, customers, and working relationships?

The visible premises might tell only part of the story. Understanding the enterprise would require understanding what it could draw upon beyond its walls.

***

I was also carrying older questions about power.

If someone succeeds, how secure is what he builds? What relationships must he maintain? Can he rely on rules, or does he feel obliged to seek protection through personal access?

I had feared dependence, but I had also feared the vulnerability that might accompany wealth. Both concerns were present in my search for a livelihood.

That made it difficult to accept an account of business that ended with “make more money.” I wanted to know what earning could make possible, what it could leave unresolved, and what conditions would allow a productive life to endure.

I also wanted to examine the expectations people inherit.

A child can learn that certain ambitions are sensible and others are dangerous before he knows enough to assess either. An adult can remain inside those conclusions while believing he is simply being realistic.

I could see reasons to ask that question of myself.

***

This is where the idea of an operating system became useful to me.

I began to think of the formal rules, informal expectations, institutions, relationships, and practical arrangements through which people try to act. Together, these help determine what an effort encounters after it leaves the imagination.

There is a limit to the metaphor. Nigeria does not have one coherent programme that explains everyone’s experience. A farmer, a civil servant, a workshop owner, a lawyer, and a software founder may encounter very different conditions. Place, income, identity, relationships, and circumstance can change what is available to each.

NaijaOS must investigate those differences. Otherwise, the name would promise understanding while concealing complexity.

The task I see is to make particular arrangements more legible. How does this market work? What enables this producer? Where does this process break down? Who can gain access, and what happens to someone who cannot?

An answer should identify something the reader can examine. Where possible, it should help that reader make a better decision or attempt something with a clearer understanding of the conditions.

***

The founding question has therefore grown.

I still care about what Nigerians will buy. Any enterprise I build must eventually face the discipline of an actual customer choosing whether to pay.

But I also care about what Nigerians could produce, what they might become capable of, and what would have to change for more people to participate.

Existing purchases can reveal demand. They cannot provide a complete inventory of future possibilities. Some things are absent because nobody wants them. Others may be absent because the conditions needed to make, deliver, afford, or trust them have not yet been established.

Telling the difference requires investigation.

This is the work I want NaijaOS to undertake: to understand how Nigeria works in specific settings, explore what could become possible, and examine how those possibilities can be created and governed.

My own journey supplies questions. It cannot supply all the answers. The work must remain open to evidence, disagreement, and experiences beyond my own.

I started by asking what Nigerians would buy because I needed a way to make a living.

I kept asking because I wanted to understand the country in which that living would have to be made.
