The Grammar of Nigeria / Essay 001

What Is Nigeria? A Nation, a State, or a Country?

Before we can decide what Nigeria should become, we must learn to distinguish what Nigeria is.

Paul MagajiLong-form civic education3,288 words

The Country We Know, and the Country We Understand

Almost every Nigerian knows that Nigeria is a country.

We know its name, its flag, its national anthem, its capital, and something of its history. We know that we have a president, governors, legislators, courts, and a Constitution. We know that Nigerians belong to different ethnic, linguistic, religious, and cultural communities.

Yet beneath these familiar facts lies a question that deserves more attention than it usually receives:

What exactly is Nigeria?

Is Nigeria a country? Certainly.

Is it a state? Yes.

Is it a nation? That question requires greater care.

And if Nigeria can be described by all three words, what does each description tell us that the others do not?

The distinctions may appear merely academic. But consider how often Nigerians debate national unity, state failure, citizenship, restructuring, sovereignty, government legitimacy, and the future of the federation.

Each of these debates depends on ideas that are frequently used without being examined.

We argue about the Nigerian nation without first asking what makes a nation. We demand that the state act without always distinguishing the state from the government. We speak of the country as though its territory, people, institutions, and political leadership were one indivisible concept.

They are not.

This essay begins a series called The Grammar of Nigeria. Its purpose is not to prescribe what Nigerians must think about their country. It is to develop the conceptual tools through which Nigerians can think more clearly for themselves.

And the first tool is distinction.

1. Nigeria as a Country: The Place We Call Ours

In ordinary speech, a country is a recognisable political and geographical entity.

It has a territory, a population, boundaries, settlements, economic activities, and a name by which it is known to the world.

We speak of travelling across Nigeria, living in Nigeria, investing in Nigeria, or leaving Nigeria.

In these expressions, Nigeria is understood primarily as a place.

Its physical territory stretches across landscapes containing cities, villages, mountains, rivers, forests, farms, industries, and other forms of human settlement.

Within that territory live people whose experiences of the country differ considerably.

For one Nigerian, the country may first be encountered through the bustling commercial life of Lagos. For another, it may be the farming communities of Nasarawa, the historic landscapes of Kano, the markets of Onitsha, or the waterways of the Niger Delta.

These places belong to the same country, but they do not necessarily produce identical experiences of it.

A country, therefore, is not exhausted by any one locality or any one community's understanding of itself.

Nor is the word country always a precise legal concept. In everyday language, it can refer broadly to a territory and its people, while in international relations it is often used interchangeably with the word state.

For more rigorous political thinking, however, we need to examine the state separately.

Because the existence of a territory does not, by itself, tell us how political authority is organised within it.

A geographical space is not automatically a sovereign political order.

2. Nigeria as a State: The Organisation of Political Authority

In political theory and international law, the state is more than a geographical location.

It is an organised political and legal order exercising public authority over a population within a territory.

The 1933 Montevideo Convention, frequently used as a reference point in discussions of statehood, identifies four commonly recognised characteristics: a permanent population, a defined territory, a government, and the capacity to enter into relations with other states.

These characteristics offer a useful starting point, although questions of statehood can be more complicated in actual international practice.

Nigeria possesses the essential characteristics of a sovereign state.

It has a population, territory, institutions of government, a legal order, and the capacity to conduct international relations.

Section 2 of the Constitution of the Federal Republic of Nigeria 1999, as amended, establishes Nigeria as a sovereign state organised as a federation.

But there is a distinction within this description that deserves particular attention.

The state is not the same thing as the government.

A government is the arrangement of persons and institutions through which political authority is exercised at a particular time.

A state is the more enduring political and legal order within which governments operate.

Presidents come and go. Governors complete their terms. Ministers resign. Political parties win and lose elections.

Yet Nigeria continues.

A change in government does not ordinarily require the creation of a new Nigerian state.

The international obligations of Nigeria do not disappear simply because a new president assumes office. Its constitutional institutions, public assets, legal relationships, and many of its obligations persist across administrations.

This distinction has profound implications for political reasoning.

When we criticise a government, are we necessarily criticising the state?

When we oppose a president, are we opposing Nigeria?

When we demand changes to public institutions, are we rejecting the existence of the country?

The answers are not automatically yes.

Indeed, confusing the government with the state can lead to a dangerous political assumption: that loyalty to the country must mean loyalty to whoever governs it.

That assumption deserves to be challenged.

In a constitutional democracy, the government exercises authority under law. It does not own the state, and it does not exhaust the political identity of the people.

The distinction also works in the opposite direction.

Not every national problem can be explained by the personal failures of the current president or governor. Some difficulties arise from enduring institutions, constitutional arrangements, administrative practices, inherited incentives, and deeper historical conditions.

Changing an officeholder may be necessary without being sufficient to change the operation of the state.

To understand Nigeria, therefore, we must learn to study both its governments and the political order within which they function.

3. Nigeria as a Nation: The Question of Belonging

The word nation introduces a different kind of question.

A state concerns organised political authority.

A nation concerns a community's understanding of its collective identity and belonging.

Nations are often associated with shared history, culture, language, traditions, memories, territory, political aspirations, or a sense of common destiny.

But these characteristics do not always exist together, and scholars have long disagreed about which are necessary.

Some understand nationhood primarily through common cultural and historical identity.

Others emphasise a shared political identity: a people who understand themselves as belonging to the same civic community, even where they differ in language, ancestry, or religion.

This second understanding is especially significant for Nigeria.

Nigeria contains many communities with distinctive histories, languages, customs, institutions, and cultural memories.

Some of these communities existed as organised political societies long before the emergence of the modern Nigerian state.

Their histories did not begin with Nigeria.

The incorporation of different territories into a single colonial administration in 1914 did not suddenly erase their earlier identities.

Nor did independence on 1 October 1960 automatically resolve every question concerning how these communities would understand their collective political belonging.

Nigeria became a republic on 1 October 1963, but the deeper work of constructing a shared civic identity could not be completed by a single constitutional event.

Here we encounter one of the most important questions in Nigerian political thought:

Must a country contain one culturally homogeneous people before it can be considered a nation?

If the answer is yes, the idea of Nigerian nationhood becomes difficult to defend without suppressing the significance of the country's diverse communities.

But if nationhood can also be founded upon a common political identity, shared citizenship, mutual obligations, and a commitment to living under common institutions, another possibility emerges.

People need not possess identical ancestries to develop a shared civic identity.

They need not abandon all their inherited cultural loyalties to recognise a wider political community.

A person may belong meaningfully to a family, an ethnic community, a locality, a state within the federation, and Nigeria itself.

The existence of these different levels of belonging does not necessarily make them mutually exclusive.

What matters is how their relationships are understood and organised.

This is why the question of Nigerian nationhood cannot be answered merely by counting ethnic groups or identifying linguistic differences.

Nor can it be settled merely by declaring that Nigeria is one nation.

The more useful inquiry is whether the different peoples of Nigeria possess, or can develop, sufficiently shared commitments to sustain a common political life.

That is an inquiry into identity, institutions, trust, justice, participation, and experience.

It is also an inquiry that should remain open to evidence and competing arguments.

4. What the Constitution Says—and What It Cannot Simply Command

Nigeria's Constitution provides an interesting meeting point between the legal existence of the state and the aspiration toward nationhood.

Its preamble expresses a commitment to unity, harmony, freedom, equality, and justice.

Section 2 establishes Nigeria's sovereign statehood and federal structure.

Section 14 declares that sovereignty belongs to the people of Nigeria and establishes democratic principles for the exercise of public authority.

Section 15 addresses national integration, including the encouragement of common belonging across ethnic, linguistic, religious, and other sectional differences.

These provisions are worth reading together.

They do not merely describe territory and institutions. They also express an idea of the political community that Nigeria is intended to be.

Notice the significance of national integration appearing as a constitutional objective.

If the existence of a common state automatically produced complete national cohesion, why would national integration require deliberate constitutional attention?

The distinction suggests an important insight.

Political unity can be legally established, while social unity remains an unfinished human undertaking.

A constitution can establish common institutions. It can prescribe rights and duties. It can distribute powers and provide procedures for resolving disputes.

But a constitution cannot, by declaration alone, make every citizen feel equally respected, represented, protected, or included.

Those experiences depend in part upon how institutions operate.

A citizen's confidence in national belonging may be shaped by the fairness of public appointments, the administration of justice, the protection of rights, access to opportunity, freedom of movement, and the conduct of public authorities.

This does not mean that constitutional commitments are unimportant.

It means that their significance must be understood at two levels: as legal and political principles, and as standards against which institutional practice can be examined.

We should therefore distinguish what Nigeria constitutionally declares itself to be from how Nigerians experience the country in practice.

The distinction allows us to evaluate the country without confusing aspiration with accomplishment.

5. Does Nigeria Exist Because of Its Government or Because of Its People?

Consider a further question.

Suppose every person currently occupying a major political office were replaced tomorrow through lawful constitutional processes.

Would Nigeria cease to exist?

Plainly, no.

Now consider a different possibility.

Suppose Nigeria retained its familiar institutions and territory, but a growing number of its citizens ceased to believe that those institutions treated them as equal members of a common political community.

Would the state necessarily disappear?

Again, no.

Yet something important about its social and political foundations could be weakened.

This thought experiment reveals the difference between the continuing legal existence of the state and the strength of the relationships that sustain it.

It also helps us understand a notable constitutional proposition.

Section 14(2)(a) declares that sovereignty belongs to the people of Nigeria, from whom government derives its constitutional powers and authority.

The government is therefore not presented as the original owner of political authority.

It exercises authority derived through the constitutional order.

But this declaration opens another question rather than closing all debate:

Who are the people, politically speaking?

Are they merely an aggregation of individuals living within a territory?

Are they a collection of historical communities joined under one Constitution?

Or are they also a civic community capable of acting together, despite profound differences, in matters concerning their common political future?

These possibilities are not necessarily exclusive.

They invite an examination of how citizenship, representation, public participation, and political identity relate to one another.

They also remind us why the idea of the people is so central to democracy.

Government is not an end existing independently of those it governs.

Its authority requires justification, and its exercise remains subject to constitutional limits.

6. The Difference Between Citizenship and Belonging

There is another distinction that deserves attention.

Citizenship is a legal status.

Belonging is also a social and psychological experience.

The Constitution provides legal rules through which Nigerian citizenship may be acquired, including citizenship by birth, registration, and naturalisation.

But possessing Nigerian citizenship does not necessarily mean that every citizen experiences the same degree of acceptance in every part of the country.

Someone may be a Nigerian citizen and still encounter questions about ancestry, place of origin, ethnic identity, or local belonging.

This raises an important problem for any country seeking durable national cohesion.

If citizenship provides a common legal identity, what should that identity mean in everyday public life?

Does citizenship merely establish membership in the state?

Or should it also influence how public institutions treat citizens, irrespective of the communities from which they originate?

A serious discussion of Nigeria must distinguish these questions.

The legal meaning of citizenship can be studied through constitutional provisions.

The lived meaning of citizenship requires attention to the practices of institutions and the experiences of citizens.

One cannot simply be substituted for the other.

Nor should the difference automatically lead to the conclusion that shared national identity is impossible.

Rather, it provides a way of examining the conditions under which civic belonging becomes credible.

7. Three Different Questions, Three Different Kinds of Answers

We can now return to our opening question.

What is Nigeria?

When we call Nigeria a country, we identify a recognisable territorial and political community—the place to which people refer when they speak of their homeland.

When we call Nigeria a state, we identify an organised sovereign legal and political order capable of exercising public authority and participating in international relations.

When we call Nigeria a nation, we enter the more complex subject of collective identity, shared belonging, historical memory, and common political life.

These descriptions can refer to the same Nigeria without meaning the same thing.

And this is where conceptual clarity becomes useful.

If someone says, "Nigeria has failed," we should ask what the person means.

Have particular governments failed to fulfil their responsibilities?

Have institutions failed to perform their constitutional functions?

Has economic performance deteriorated?

Has confidence in the political community weakened?

Or is the person making a much wider claim about the viability of the Nigerian state itself?

These are different propositions requiring different kinds of evidence.

Similarly, when someone calls for national unity, we should ask what kind of unity is being proposed.

Unity of territory?

Unity under constitutional institutions?

Unity of political purpose?

Equality of citizenship?

Cultural uniformity?

Mutual loyalty among diverse communities?

A person may support one of these forms of unity without necessarily endorsing every other interpretation.

The ability to distinguish them makes disagreement more productive.

It helps us move from emotionally powerful language to propositions that can actually be examined.

8. Before We Decide What Nigeria Should Become

Perhaps the temptation in Nigerian public discussion is to move too quickly from dissatisfaction to prescription.

We encounter a problem, identify an individual or institution to blame, and propose a remedy.

Sometimes the remedy is a new president.

Sometimes it is a new Constitution.

Sometimes it is restructuring, institutional reform, stronger national identity, or a different allocation of political authority.

These proposals deserve examination.

But their usefulness depends partly on whether we understand the nature of the problem they seek to solve.

If a problem concerns poor administrative performance, a change in constitutional structure may not, by itself, resolve it.

If a problem arises from the distribution of constitutional authority, changing a political officeholder may leave the underlying difficulty untouched.

If a problem concerns public trust or national belonging, an institutional reform may require accompanying changes in civic practice and political conduct.

We need not reject any particular reform to recognise these distinctions.

We only need to ask more carefully what kind of problem confronts us.

That is one of the central purposes of The Grammar of Nigeria.

Not to replace political opinions with an approved set of opinions, but to improve the quality of the questions from which opinions emerge.

A person who understands the difference between the nation and the state is better prepared to examine claims about national unity.

A person who distinguishes the state from the government is better prepared to judge political responsibility.

A person who distinguishes legal citizenship from lived belonging is better prepared to examine the relationship between constitutional promises and social reality.

And a person who understands the difference between constitutional design and institutional performance is better prepared to evaluate competing proposals for reform.

None of these distinctions supplies every answer.

They make better answers possible.

Conclusion: Nigeria Is Also a Question

Nigeria is a country.

Nigeria is a sovereign state.

Nigeria is also a political community whose national identity, cohesion, and common aspirations deserve continuing examination.

Its legal existence is not dependent upon every citizen agreeing about its history or political future.

Yet the quality of its common life cannot be understood by legal existence alone.

It is shaped by how its institutions function, how its citizens relate to one another, how authority is justified and exercised, and how its people understand the obligations that arise from belonging to a common political order.

We began by asking whether Nigeria is a nation, a state, or a country.

We have discovered that the question contains several distinct inquiries.

The country directs our attention to place and people.

The state directs our attention to institutions, law, and sovereignty.

The nation directs our attention to identity, belonging, and common political life.

To understand Nigeria intelligently, we must learn to move among these meanings without confusing them.

Because before we can responsibly ask what Nigeria should become, we must acquire the intellectual discipline to understand what we are talking about when we say Nigeria.

And perhaps that is where a more serious national conversation ought to begin.

Not with the demand that every Nigerian think alike, but with the possibility that more Nigerians might learn to think more clearly.

Questions for the Reader

1. Can a country be politically united while its people retain different national or cultural identities? 2. If a government acts against the interests of citizens, does opposing that government constitute opposition to the country? 3. What distinguishes being legally recognised as a Nigerian from feeling that one belongs to Nigeria?

4. Can a Constitution establish political unity without automatically producing mutual trust? 5. Which of Nigeria's difficulties arise from individual officeholders, which from enduring institutions, and which from deeper questions of collective identity? These questions are not a test with predetermined answers. They are invitations to examine the ideas on which our public arguments depend.
