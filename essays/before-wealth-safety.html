Before I Wanted Wealth, I Wanted Safety

The search for security beneath the ambition to earn.

By Paul A. Magaji · 7 min read · How a world becomes visible

Personal essay

Download Word document

I started with a question that sounded like market research: What will Nigerians buy?

I wanted to sell something on the internet. I did not have access to the market research that might have helped me decide what to create, who needed it, or what they could afford. So I took my questions to the tools I could reach: ChatGPT, Grok, Claude, and, at some point, DeepSeek.

Their answers often returned to practical needs, earning money, solving immediate problems, and the familiar language of hustle.

I recognised some of that world. Yet something about the answers left me dissatisfied. There were things I wanted to understand and create that did not fit comfortably within the picture being presented to me.

Eventually, I began to see that my question had a history. Long before I wondered what Nigerians would buy, I had wondered how I would survive.

As a boy growing up in Jos, I thought that failing to get a government job could make life terribly difficult. I cannot now attach that conviction to one conversation or one afternoon. But I remember the expectation: a salary would provide something dependable from which to organise a life.

There would be children to feed. There would be responsibilities. How would I meet them without a regular income?

I was perhaps thirteen or fourteen.

When we describe someone of that age as “just a child,” we can overlook how much interpretation is already taking place. A child watches adults. He notices who appears secure, who must ask for help, whose requests receive attention, and whose difficulties continue without resolution.

He may not know the language of institutions or economic opportunity. Still, he is forming conclusions about where safety lives.

Mine seemed to live close to government.

Looking back, I do not want to ridicule that younger version of myself. He was trying to prepare for the world as he understood it. But I do want to examine the world he thought he understood.

What had I seen? What had I been told? And what possibilities had never entered the conversation?

***

Wealth itself did not seem entirely safe to me.

I carried a fear that becoming very rich might expose a person to powerful people who could interfere with his freedom. If you built something substantial, could somebody take it away? Would you need protection? Would independence eventually require an accommodation with those who controlled access?

These were fears, not conclusions established by research. Yet fears can organise a life before they have been properly examined.

My experience of authority also had a physical dimension. In secondary school, I was beaten in the name of discipline. I remember the pain and the fact that someone had the power to impose it.

I would not claim that this explains everything I later thought about government or money. But it belongs in the account. When you have experienced power through pain, questions about freedom can become very concrete. Who can compel you? What can they use against you? What happens when you cannot refuse?

Perhaps this helps explain my interest in stories such as 24 and The Blacklist. I found myself watching the uses of pressure and leverage: how people were made to act, what they were threatened with losing, and how they tried to recover control.

I was trying to understand something.

***

A friend helped introduce another possibility.

I began thinking more seriously about being productive, independent, and useful. I worked on the farm. Then my friend brought me a magazine called Success Digest.

Through it, I encountered a world in which useful information could become a product. Someone could understand a problem, assemble knowledge that addressed it, and sell that knowledge to people who needed it.

The attraction was immediate. There was something I could do. I could learn, organise, explain, create.

I followed the magazine closely. It helped me imagine earning through initiative rather than waiting for a place to be given to me.

But much later, I discovered another economy developing beyond the one I had been following: startups, software companies, technology businesses, and the professional work surrounding them.

I was already a lawyer. These developments should have mattered to my practice. Yet much of that world had remained outside my attention.

That discovery unsettled me. I had been learning, but within a particular field of vision. The information that opened possibilities for me had also become the information through which I expected possibilities to appear.

How much else was happening beyond it?

I began learning programming: Ruby, then HTML, CSS, JavaScript, and Python. I did not yet have a complete understanding of how the pieces fitted together. I was following clues, discovering that things I used could also be things people built.

Each discovery changed the questions I could ask.

***

Travelling and practising law brought other disruptions to my picture of Nigeria.

During a visit connected with a matter in Abia, conversations about Enugu surprised me. I heard it described as a civil service state. Elsewhere, I heard expectations of growing commercial activity and investment by people returning from abroad.

I also heard about Aba’s manufacturing capacity: people making things in remarkably small spaces, sometimes working from a single room.

These conversations were not enough to establish a definitive account of either place. They were enough to expose the weakness of my own categories.

I had inherited broad descriptions of people and places. Actual livelihoods were more complicated.

A small workshop made me wonder about everything beyond its walls. Where did the skills come from? Who supplied the materials? How did buyers find the producer? What relationships allowed the work to continue?

The person working in that room might know an economic world that remained almost invisible to someone passing outside.

I began to wonder whether opportunity sometimes exists beyond the boundaries of the knowledge available to us—and how often those boundaries are mistaken for the limits of reality.

***

There was another tension I struggled with.

I sometimes encountered admiration for prosperous people alongside resistance to the choices through which someone nearby might pursue prosperity. Success received recognition; an uncertain attempt could receive suspicion.

But I must be fair here. Concern is not always hostility. People may have watched ventures fail. They may understand obligations that enthusiasm overlooks. Some warnings deserve to be heard.

The difficulty is learning to distinguish a warning that improves your judgment from one that merely returns you to familiar limits.

I have needed that distinction myself. Fear can present itself as wisdom. Ambition can also present itself as certainty. Both require examination.

Then, amid these struggles, I lost my mother.

I cannot turn that loss into a neat argument. It remains a loss.

But it changed the weight of time. Immediate needs can become more urgent when you understand, painfully, that the future does not arrive with everyone still beside you.

There are things that require money now, care now, presence now. A vision must find some way to live alongside them.

***

This is part of how NaijaOS began.

I wanted to understand how Nigerians make money. The question widened because every answer seemed to depend on something else: information, skills, institutions, relationships, infrastructure, authority, and the expectations people carry about their own lives.

I also wanted to understand my own expectations.

Which ambitions had I chosen freely? Which had grown around a fear? Which possibilities had I dismissed before I knew enough to judge them?

My childhood desire for a salary contained a reasonable wish for dependability. My later interest in enterprise contained a wish to create and contribute. I am still working out how to bring those wishes into a life that can support its responsibilities.

NaijaOS gives me a place to investigate that work publicly: to examine how Nigeria functions, how people find room to act within it, and what we might build to make that room wider.

I began by asking what Nigerians would buy.

I now find myself asking what Nigerians need to know, possess, and be able to rely upon so that they can attempt more.

Before I wanted wealth, I wanted safety. I want to understand what it would take for safety to give more of us the confidence to build.
