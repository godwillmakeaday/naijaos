When the Telephone Arrived, I Thought It Had Just Been Invented

The distance between what exists and what we can access.

By Paul A. Magaji · 7 min read · How a world becomes visible

When mobile phones began entering everyday Nigerian life, I thought, at first, that the telephone itself had only recently been invented.

I later encountered its longer history: the companies, the earlier devices, the years of development that preceded our own experience. What felt new to me had already existed elsewhere.

I had confused its arrival in my world with its arrival in the world.

That misunderstanding has stayed with me because it raises a question much larger than telecommunications: how much of what we believe is impossible is simply something we have never been able to access?

There were landlines in the surroundings I remember. But their presence did not make telephone communication an ordinary part of life. Some seemed more like relics. We could play around with them without inhabiting a world in which calling someone from one house to another was dependable and routine.

An object can be present while the possibility it represents remains absent.

A telephone in a house does not, by itself, give a child the experience of a connected society. That requires a working service, someone to call, affordable use and enough reliability for people to organise their lives around it.

What I remember most is the distance between seeing the instrument and living with its usefulness.

Then mobile telephony began to change the boundaries.

The historical distinction matters. This was the opening associated with GSM mobile services, before smartphones became commonplace. Nigeria’s digital mobile licence auction took place in January 2001, during President Olusegun Obasanjo’s administration. The NCC’s records document the licensing process behind that opening. [1]

For me, the important discovery came later: Nigerians had not suddenly acquired the human capacity to speak across distance. The technology had not waited for us to imagine it. Something had changed in the arrangements through which we could obtain and use it.

That discovery unsettled an assumption I had scarcely known I possessed.

If something could be unavailable for so long and then become part of ordinary life, what other absences had I mistaken for permanent conditions?

The world we learn to expect

A child does not begin with a complete account of what humanity can do. He begins with the adults, objects, institutions and routines around him.

He sees how people find work. He hears which professions are respectable. He notices who can obtain help and who must wait. He learns which ambitions attract encouragement and which provoke laughter.

From those observations, he begins to construct a world.

In my own early understanding, the government job occupied an enormous place. It offered the prospect of a salary, a recognised position and some capacity to provide for a family. Other ways of sustaining a life were less visible to me, or appeared less secure.

The telephone belongs to this same history of learning what to expect.

When a useful thing is consistently absent, we may stop asking about it. We organise around the absence. We develop substitutes. Eventually, the arrangement can feel so ordinary that questioning it seems impractical.

This adaptation has value. People must live under the conditions they actually face. But adaptation can also conceal how much effort those conditions demand.

The person who spends a day delivering a message may become exceptionally resourceful. He still deserves to ask why delivering that message required a day.

Discovery can happen without invention

There is a particular kind of discovery that occurs when you encounter something other people have long taken for granted.

You discover a service, a profession, a business model or a way of organising work. It is new to you, although you have invented nothing.

The discovery can be exhilarating. It can also be painful. You begin to see decisions you might have made differently had you known earlier.

I experienced something of this through Success Digest. It helped me see that useful information could become a product. Someone could identify a problem, organise knowledge around it and sell a practical answer.

That expanded my understanding of enterprise.

Yet I later discovered a startup world developing beyond the part of business I had been following. Companies were being created, technical capabilities were being assembled, and professional opportunities existed that I had barely understood—even as a lawyer who might have found useful work within them.

One source had widened my view. I had allowed that view to become too complete.

I now have to carry the same caution into my use of artificial intelligence. A system can help me discover possibilities while presenting an incomplete picture of the people and markets I want to understand. Repeated answers can become familiar without becoming sufficient.

The question is whether I continue investigating beyond the first convincing account.

What exactly is stopping us?

“Why can’t we?” can express frustration. It can also begin serious work, provided we are willing to become specific.

Sometimes we lack knowledge. Sometimes the knowledge exists, but there is no accessible route to learning it. Sometimes trained people cannot obtain equipment, financing or customers. Sometimes a public rule prevents entry. Sometimes the rule permits entry, but its administration makes participation uncertain.

And sometimes several of these conditions reinforce one another.

Calling all of this a failure of mindset gives the individual too much responsibility for conditions he cannot change alone. Calling all of it a failure of government can leave us inattentive to capabilities we could begin developing ourselves.

We need an account precise enough to distinguish them.

The telephone offers a useful discipline here. Once we ask how a working service reaches a household, the vague question of possibility becomes a set of concrete questions about technology, investment, infrastructure, regulation, prices and operation.

We can then investigate what failed, what changed and what remains unfinished.

That is more useful than either declaring Nigerians incapable or insisting that determination can overcome everything.

Access is a beginning

There is another limit to the telephone analogy that deserves attention.

Being able to buy and use a technology is different from being able to design, manufacture, maintain or improve it.

Access can transform lives while leaving important productive capabilities elsewhere. A country can become a substantial market for something it has limited capacity to build.

This does not diminish the value of access. It gives us a further question.

Once a useful technology becomes available, what can we learn to do with it? Can we build services around it? Develop technical expertise? Produce components? Conduct research? Create businesses that solve problems its original designers did not anticipate?

These are different tasks, requiring different levels of investment and learning. They should not be collapsed into a single announcement that we have “embraced technology.”

The ambition should become more precise as our access improves.

My question about semiconductor manufacturing comes from this concern. I want to understand why some societies develop the capacity to produce technologies others mainly purchase. But a serious answer requires more than pointing to money that could have been spent differently. We must examine how expertise, infrastructure, management and sustained learning come together—and how they fail to come together.

“Why can’t we?” becomes demanding when we accept the work of finding out.

What else have we mistaken for impossible?

I cannot recover the opportunities I did not recognise at the time. I can become more careful about treating my present view as the full extent of reality.

That is part of what I want NaijaOS to do.

It should help us investigate the distance between what Nigerians experience and what could become available to them. It should examine the institutions, knowledge and productive arrangements required to cross that distance.

Some barriers will prove harder than we expect. Some ambitions will require revision. Others may turn out to have been waiting for a decision, a connection, a skill or an institution we can begin to build.

I think again of those telephones we could see without experiencing the ordinary usefulness they promised.

What would it have taken for them to work as part of everyday life? Who could have made that happen? How many choices were shaped by the fact that it had not happened?

These are questions about the past. They are also a way of looking around now.

What is absent from our lives today that we have learned to regard as beyond us—and what would it take to make it ordinary?
