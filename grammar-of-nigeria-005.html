The Grammar of Nigeria / Essay 005

State, Government and Officeholder: Is the President the Government?

The occupant of an office may become the most visible face of power. But visibility is not ownership.

Paul MagajiLong-form civic education3,731 words

The Country That Remains When a President Leaves

Imagine a presidential inauguration in Nigeria. A president finishes his term, another takes the oath, and the cameras capture the exchange of authority. In the language of political commentary, one government has ended and another has begun.

But what, precisely, has ended? Nigeria has not disappeared. Its territory has not become another territory. The courts do not have to be invented anew. Every public school does not cease to exist. A debt properly owed by the Federal Government does not automatically become somebody else's personal debt because the election has produced a different winner. Public institutions may be restructured, laws may be amended, and policies may be reversed through lawful processes; yet the legal order within which these changes occur is understood to continue.

Something has changed, but something else remains.

This is the intellectual problem at the centre of this essay. We often speak about Nigeria as though the country, the state, the government, the presidency and the president were merely different names for the same thing. In ordinary conversation such shorthand may be harmless. In serious political reasoning it can produce remarkable confusion.

When a government disappoints us, we say Nigeria has failed. When a president performs well, we say he has built Nigeria. When a governor issues a public instruction, we sometimes speak as though the instruction were itself the law. When a public official is criticised, supporters may interpret the criticism as disloyalty to the state.

What if we began by distinguishing the things we are talking about?

The question is not whether a president matters. A president matters enormously. The question is what kind of power the president possesses, where that power comes from, what limits it, and what continues when the person holding it leaves office.

1. A Country, a State, a Government and a Person

A country is a familiar territorial and political community: the Nigeria to which we travel, within which we live, and about which we speak as a homeland.

A state, in the language of political theory and law, is the more enduring structure of sovereign authority: a constitutional and institutional order that claims and exercises public powers over a territory and its people. Here the word state means the Nigerian sovereign state, not one of the thirty-six constituent states of the federation. That double meaning is itself an excellent reason to define our terms.

A government is the set of institutions and arrangements through which the authority of the state is exercised. The word also has a narrower, everyday sense: the particular administration temporarily directing public affairs. We may say “the government announced a new policy,” meaning the executive administration, without suggesting that the legislature or courts have disappeared from constitutional government.

An office is a legally constituted position carrying duties and powers. The presidency, a governorship, a seat in the legislature, a judicial office and many public-service positions exist as offices distinct from the individuals who occupy them.

The officeholder is the person temporarily authorised to perform the office's functions.

These are not differences invented to complicate political language. They are differences that help us locate responsibility. A country may remain while a government changes. A government may be removed from office without the abolition of the state. An office may persist while its occupant resigns, loses an election, dies, or is lawfully removed. And a person may be influential without possessing any constitutional authority at all.

The first discipline of civic reasoning, then, is to ask: Am I speaking about Nigeria, its institutions, an administration, an office, or the individual currently holding that office?

2. The President Is Powerful, but Power Has a Constitutional Address

Nigeria's Constitution is unusually clear about the position of the President. Section 130 establishes the office and describes the President as Head of State, Chief Executive of the Federation and Commander-in-Chief of the Armed Forces. Section 5 vests the executive powers of the Federation in the President, subject to the Constitution, and provides for their exercise through the Vice-President, ministers and officers in the federal public service.

None of this makes the President the whole of the state.

Section 4 assigns federal legislative powers to the National Assembly. Section 6 assigns judicial powers to constitutionally recognised courts. These provisions do not turn the three branches into completely isolated islands; they establish distinct powers that interact through constitutional procedures.

The President may initiate policy, propose legislation, exercise constitutionally assigned executive authority and lead the federal administration. But the President is not, merely by holding office, the National Assembly. Nor is the President the Supreme Court. A presidential announcement does not, by its own force, repeal an Act of the National Assembly, override a valid judicial order or rewrite the Constitution.

Indeed, Section 1 makes the Constitution supreme and binds all authorities and persons to it. The President's immense executive powers are therefore powers under a constitutional order, not powers standing above it.

It is possible to recognise the political importance of a president without reducing every public institution to the presidency. That is not hostility to leadership. It is an accurate description of constitutional leadership.

3. What Does the Word “Government” Actually Mean?

We need to be fair to ordinary language. People who say “the government changed in 2023,” or in any other election year, are not necessarily making a constitutional mistake. They usually mean that the political leadership of the executive administration changed.

But the meaning of government becomes more complex when we ask whether the president alone can decide an issue, spend a particular sum, change a law or overrule a court.

In one sense, government includes the public institutions through which legislative, executive and judicial powers are exercised. In another, it means the executive administration of the day. In a third, we sometimes use it to refer loosely to the entire machinery of public administration—ministries, departments, agencies and officials.

Each usage can be legitimate in its setting. The danger begins when an argument silently moves from one meaning to another.

Suppose a citizen says, “Government should fix this road.” Before assigning responsibility, one may need to ask whether the road falls within federal, state or local responsibilities; which institution has legal authority; who controls the relevant budget; whether there is a contractual obligation; and who is accountable for its execution.

The President may be prominent in the citizen's imagination, but prominence does not settle the jurisdictional question.

Similarly, suppose a citizen says, “Government passed a law.” Was the law passed by the National Assembly or a State House of Assembly? Did the executive assent to it, where required? Is the matter within the relevant legislature's constitutional competence? The shorthand is insufficient for a careful answer.

The useful habit is not to ban the word government. It is to ask, whenever precision matters: Which part of government, acting under what power, is being discussed?

4. The Office Exists Before the Person Arrives

Consider the presidency as an institution. Before a candidate wins an election, the constitutional office already exists. It has recognised qualifications, procedures for election, rules about tenure, an oath and established arrangements for the performance of its functions.

The election does not permit the winner to invent the scope of the office. Rather, the winner acquires authority to occupy and exercise an office already defined by law.

Section 140 requires a person elected President to make the prescribed asset declaration and take the relevant oaths before performing the office's functions. This is more than ceremonial theatre. The law recognises a difference between being declared elected and being duly authorised to perform official functions.

Sections 145 and 146 make the distinction even more visible. They provide arrangements for acting presidential functions during temporary inability or absence and for succession when the presidency becomes vacant. The constitutional order anticipates circumstances in which the occupant cannot continue, yet the office and its functions remain necessary.

This yields a striking principle: a well-designed public office does not require the biological, political or personal permanence of its holder.

A person can be exceptional; a republic cannot be organised on the assumption that the person is indispensable.

Institutional continuity is not proof that institutions function well. An agency can persist while becoming ineffective. A ministry can retain its name while losing institutional memory. But continuity is still an essential question: what arrangements allow public duties to be performed when the personalities at the top change?

5. A Public Office Is Not Private Property

Much of our political language becomes clearer if we refuse to confuse office with possession.

A public official may occupy an official residence, control certain administrative decisions, approve expenditure within the law or direct public officers within the lawful scope of an office. None of these privileges turns public authority into a private estate.

The fact that a president can direct aspects of the executive branch does not mean the federal treasury is a personal bank account. The fact that a governor occupies Government House does not make Government House inherited family property. The fact that a minister supervises a ministry does not mean the ministry's legal duties disappear when that minister loses office.

This is not to say that all public resources are held under one identical legal doctrine of trust. Their ownership, custody, control and management depend on the relevant law. The broader constitutional point is that public powers are conferred for public purposes and must be exercised within legal limits.

It follows that access to office should not be confused with entitlement to personal loyalty from public institutions. A civil servant's obligation is not necessarily identical to a political appointee's; each position has its own legal framework. But neither the existence of a political party nor the authority of an individual can erase the duties that law attaches to a public office.

The distinction matters especially where citizens treat appointments as rewards to be consumed rather than responsibilities to be discharged. A political appointment may carry status. Its constitutional significance lies in the functions entrusted to its occupant.

6. The Invisible Government: Institutions That Must Outlast Administrations

Presidents and governors attract attention partly because we see them. The institutions that make public administration possible often operate with much less visibility.

The Constitution establishes a federal civil service under Section 169, and state civil services under Section 206. It also provides for offices, commissions and other public bodies whose functions cannot be reduced to the daily political activities of one officeholder.

Consider the records of a ministry. Contracts have been signed; budget allocations have been made; court proceedings may be pending; civil servants possess specialised knowledge; programmes may be underway. A new administration may change priorities, and lawfully review or terminate arrangements where permitted. But orderly government requires officials to know what has been decided, what remains legally binding, what must be reviewed and why.

When such records are lost, concealed or ignored, a country pays for the weakness of its institutions. Projects may be restarted without learning from previous work. Public money may be wasted. Citizens may be unable to discover who authorised a decision or on what evidence.

The deeper point is that public administration is not only the art of announcing a policy. It is also the organised capacity to remember, implement, account and correct.

A state whose public machinery cannot retain knowledge across changes of political leadership becomes dependent on personalities. Each new leader must rediscover what the government should already know.

That is one reason institutional memory matters. It gives a nation the possibility of learning cumulatively rather than beginning again whenever an election produces a new winner.

7. Political Loyalty and Constitutional Responsibility

We should now examine one of the most emotionally charged consequences of our distinctions.

If a citizen criticises a president, is that citizen attacking Nigeria?

Not necessarily.

A president can represent the state in specified constitutional capacities, but representation is not identity. The president can be criticised for decisions without denying the legitimacy of Nigeria as a sovereign state. Likewise, a person may support the constitutional order while opposing the programme of the administration in office.

There is also a corresponding responsibility on critics. Disagreement with a leader does not automatically establish that every official act is unlawful, that public institutions have no legitimacy or that the country's interests should be disregarded.

Conceptual clarity works in both directions. It protects criticism from being falsely equated with disloyalty, and it protects analysis from dissolving into indiscriminate condemnation.

Consider a public officer faced with pressure from a powerful political figure. The question is not simply who has greater social standing. It is whether the instruction comes from an authority legally empowered to give it, whether it concerns a matter within that authority's competence, and whether obeying it would comply with the Constitution and applicable law.

A republic cannot function well if official obligations are replaced by a permanent competition to demonstrate personal devotion to powerful individuals. Nor can it function without lawful direction and accountability in the executive branch.

The challenge is to preserve both: effective leadership and limits that make the leadership answerable.

8. Continuity Does Not Mean That Everything Must Remain the Same

At this point, an objection may arise. If the state is enduring and institutions matter, does that mean incoming governments should simply preserve the decisions of their predecessors?

No.

Elections would lose much of their purpose if voters could not authorise changes in policy or leadership. A new administration may have a democratic mandate to reform institutions, revise priorities, propose new laws, appoint officials or change spending decisions through lawful procedures.

The state is continuous; its policies are not necessarily permanent. Continuity refers to the persistence of a legal and institutional order, not an obligation to freeze every decision made within it.

This distinction makes it possible to ask better questions about transitions. Which obligations remain legally binding? Which projects deserve continuation because their benefits outweigh their costs? Which policies should be reversed because they are ineffective or unjust? What procedures must be followed to make such changes lawful? What information does the incoming administration need from the outgoing one?

When those questions are asked, institutional continuity and political change cease to be enemies. They become complementary features of constitutional government.

An administration should be able to change direction without pretending that history began on inauguration day.

9. A Problem of Responsibility: Who Should a Citizen Hold Accountable?

Suppose a public hospital lacks essential equipment. A citizen may understandably blame “government.” But the word does not identify the responsible decision-maker.

The inquiry should proceed through a chain of responsibility. Who owns or operates the facility? Which authority has the legal duty to manage it? Who approved its budget? Were funds released? Who was responsible for procurement? Were contracts performed? Did an oversight body identify failures? What remedy is available to the affected citizens?

The answers could point to a minister, commissioner, hospital management, procurement officials, legislators, contractors or several actors together. They might also expose defects in institutional design rather than one person's negligence.

This is not an argument for removing political responsibility from presidents and governors. Senior executives may be answerable for leadership, priorities, appointments and supervision even where another actor performed the immediate administrative task. But leadership accountability and direct legal responsibility must not be confused.

A useful civic instrument should therefore ask four distinct questions:

  1. Authority: Who had the legal power or duty to act?
  2. Action: Who actually took or failed to take the relevant step?
  3. Control: Who controlled the money, personnel or information necessary to act?
  4. Accountability: Through what lawful process can the decision be questioned or corrected?

Such questions are more demanding than shouting the name of the most prominent officeholder. They also offer the citizen a better chance of finding the point at which a public problem can be addressed.

10. Two Errors That Undermine Republican Government

One error is personalisation: imagining that the state rises and falls entirely with the qualities of a leader. This can create exaggerated expectations of good leaders and exaggerated despair under bad ones. It may also encourage a politics in which institutions are treated as obstacles to charismatic action.

The opposite error is institutional evasion: treating the state as so vast and impersonal that no individual can be held responsible. Officials then hide behind departments, processes and committees. The public is told that “the system” failed, as though decisions were not made, warnings were not received and legal duties did not belong to actual persons.

Both errors are dangerous.

A country cannot thrive on heroic personalities alone. Neither can it thrive on institutions whose personnel never answer for their conduct.

The more difficult task is to connect institutional continuity with personal accountability. We need offices that endure, rules that matter and records that survive; and we need occupants who can be questioned, evaluated and, where the law permits, removed or sanctioned.

The constitutional system provides parts of this architecture. For example, the National Assembly possesses investigative powers under Section 88 for specified legislative and oversight purposes. Courts exercise judicial powers under Section 6. The precise availability of remedies, the standing of a complainant and any applicable immunities or procedural limits depend on the law and circumstances.

No single institution supplies the whole solution. Accountability is a relationship among legal authority, information, public scrutiny and consequences.

11. Is the President the Government? A Better Answer

We can finally answer the question in its several meanings.

If by “government” we mean the executive administration of the Federation, the President is its constitutional leader and chief executive—not the only person or institution through which executive functions are carried out.

If by “government” we mean the full constitutional apparatus through which public power is exercised, the President is one central officeholder within a larger distribution of legislative, executive and judicial authority.

If by “Nigeria” we mean the sovereign state and its people, the President is not identical to either.

The distinction is not intended to diminish the presidency. It locates its significance correctly.

The President has authority because the Constitution establishes the office and confers powers upon it. The officeholder's electoral mandate is important, but election does not dissolve constitutional limits. Effective leadership is valuable, but it is not proof that the officeholder owns the institutions he leads.

And when that occupant departs, the constitutional question is not how to preserve the individual's dominance. It is how to ensure that lawful powers pass to another officeholder, public obligations are properly handled and the state remains capable of serving its citizens.

Conclusion: A Country Must Be Larger Than Its Leaders

Every political community needs leadership. Decisions must be taken, policies developed, priorities set, institutions directed and failures corrected. We should not mistake constitutional limitation for an argument against energetic government.

But a country becomes vulnerable when its citizens can no longer distinguish the public office from the individual, the administration from the state, or lawful authority from personal influence.

A president may give a country direction for a period. A country should not lose its direction merely because a president leaves.

A governor may reform an institution. The institution should not cease to function when the governor's term ends.

A minister may bring imagination and competence to a ministry. The ministry should also preserve the knowledge necessary for the next person to serve competently.

Good leadership and strong institutions are not alternatives. Each is a test of the other. The most consequential leader may be the one who leaves behind an office that can function well without him.

In the previous essays, we examined the country, the state, sovereignty, federalism, republicanism and democracy. The distinction between the state, government and officeholder brings those ideas into contact with the everyday operation of power.

It gives citizens a more disciplined way to ask who is responsible for what—and why.

The country is not the president. The state is not the administration. And public office is not private possession.

To understand those distinctions is not to know everything about Nigeria. It is to possess one more instrument for thinking intelligently about how Nigeria is governed.

Questions for the Reader

  1. What, exactly, changes when a new president is sworn in—and what remains legally and institutionally continuous?
  2. When people say “government has failed,” which institution or officeholder might they mean?
  3. Can a president have a strong electoral mandate and still act beyond a constitutional power?
  4. How should citizens distinguish political accountability for leadership from legal responsibility for a particular decision?
  5. What institutional records and capabilities should survive a change of administration?
  6. Can criticism of an officeholder be an act of commitment to the constitutional order rather than hostility to the country?
  7. What happens when civil servants, public bodies or legislators treat personal loyalty as more important than their legal duties?
  8. Which is the more enduring political achievement: an exceptionally effective individual leader, or institutions capable of effective government after that leader leaves?
