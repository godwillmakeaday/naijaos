Knowing the Lay of the Land

Learning enough about conditions to act thoughtfully and revise the map.

By Paul A. Magaji · 7 min read · Responsibility and informed action

Personal essay

Download Word document

I began by asking how Nigerians make money.

I wanted to know what I could sell on the internet. Without access to the market research I thought I needed, I took the question to several AI systems and tried to approach it from different directions.

The answers brought me into a larger inquiry. Before deciding what to offer, I needed to understand the setting in which the offer would have to work.

Who needed it? What were they already doing about the problem? What could they afford? Why would they trust me? What would I have to learn before I could deliver something useful?

I began to think of this as knowing the lay of the land.

The expression appealed to me because it connected understanding with action. I was seeking knowledge that could help me find a way through conditions I did not fully understand.

I had carried an earlier map of that land since childhood.

As a boy growing up in Jos, I thought life might be terribly difficult without a government job. A salary represented the possibility of feeding children and meeting responsibilities.

I also feared that becoming wealthy could expose someone to people powerful enough to interfere with what he had built.

Those beliefs helped organise my expectations before I had investigated them. They made some futures seem secure and others dangerous.

Looking back, I can recognise the concern for responsibility within them. I can also recognise how incomplete my knowledge was.

The country I imagined at thirteen or fourteen was assembled from the limited world I had encountered. It could not contain every arrangement through which people earned, cooperated, and built.

Learning more required me to reconsider the map.

A friend and a magazine helped me discover another route.

Success Digest introduced me to useful information as something that could be organised into a product. Knowledge could address a particular difficulty, and someone might pay for the help.

I followed that world closely. Later, I discovered software businesses and a startup economy that had remained outside much of my attention.

I was already a lawyer. Some of those activities could have mattered to my professional development, but I had not understood them well enough to recognise where I might contribute.

That gap changed what I meant by being informed.

I could read regularly and still overlook a field. I could possess a qualification without understanding all the settings in which its abilities might become useful.

Knowing the lay of the land would require deliberate attention beyond the places where I had already learned to look.

Conversations during my legal work in Abia raised a related difficulty.

I heard Enugu described as a civil service state. I also heard expectations of commercial growth and accounts of people returning from abroad to invest. These descriptions surprised me because of the businesspeople from Enugu whom I had encountered elsewhere.

I initially heard a contradiction.

But the accounts might have concerned different things: employment in one locality, activity by people working elsewhere, and investment moving between places.

I needed to specify the question before deciding which explanation answered it.

Similarly, what I heard about production in small rooms in Aba made me wonder about the relationships beyond the room. Where did skills, materials, orders, and customers come from?

A visible workshop could be connected to an arrangement much larger than its premises. I would have to investigate those connections to understand its capacity.

These discoveries have made me more interested in explanations that identify a process.

A broad description can direct attention, but it can also conceal the details on which an attempt depends.

“Nigerians hustle” does not tell me who would buy a particular service. “There is money in technology” does not explain what work I could competently perform. A claim that a place is commercially active does not establish how someone without substantial resources could enter its economy.

I need questions that reach the work.

What happens first? Who performs the next task? What does it cost? What knowledge is required? Where does payment enter the arrangement? What happens when something goes wrong?

The answers might reveal an opportunity. They might also reveal why the opportunity is unsuitable for me.

Both would improve my judgment.

I want to be careful about the military associations of the metaphor.

My interest in the lay of the land grew partly from questions about scarcity, power, and security. I wanted to understand what could make a life vulnerable and what might provide room to act.

But a market is also a place of cooperation. A customer has purposes of his own. A colleague, supplier, or employee cannot be understood adequately as an obstacle to my victory.

If I bring only suspicion to an inquiry, I may fail to see how useful work depends upon people contributing different things.

The understanding I seek must therefore include mutual benefit and responsibility. What does each participant need? What has each undertaken? What makes the arrangement worth continuing for the people involved?

I want to become capable of building relationships that others can reasonably rely upon.

AI gave me a way to begin asking questions I could not previously pursue as easily.

It could help me generate possibilities, compare explanations, and identify subjects to investigate. That access mattered.

Yet a plausible explanation still needed contact with the activity it described. Several systems repeating a similar picture would not establish the existence of a particular market.

I would need to speak to people, examine what they already used, and understand the choices they faced. If I made an offer, their response would tell me something a conversation about the offer could not.

I want to preserve that distinction in my work.

An explanation can help me prepare for an encounter. The encounter may expose an assumption that the explanation left untouched.

Knowing the land requires returning to it.

There is a practical limit to preparation.

I could keep studying until the search for understanding became a reason to postpone every attempt. I know why that would be tempting: an uncertain beginning might expose how much I still do not know.

But some information becomes available only through doing.

I can therefore ask what a responsible first attempt would look like. What could I make or investigate within my means? What would I expect to observe? What result would make me change the plan?

The attempt needs boundaries because responsibilities remain real. The time, money, and help committed to it come from somewhere.

A small test cannot establish everything. It can answer a sufficiently specific question to improve the next decision.

That is a form of progress I can examine.

I also need to distinguish understanding conditions from accepting them as permanent.

If I learn that a process is difficult to enter, I should investigate the difficulty. Is there a skill I lack? A requirement I do not understand? A cost I cannot meet? An arrangement whose fairness deserves scrutiny?

Each explanation points towards different work.

Some conditions require me to prepare better. Others may become subjects for collective action or institutional change. I cannot make that distinction responsibly if I treat every barrier as either my personal failure or proof that nothing can be done.

NaijaOS should help make the barrier specific enough to discuss.

Understanding a setting can support an immediate decision while also revealing something that ought to change.

The loss of my mother gave this inquiry another urgency.

I had been asking how to build towards a more secure future. Her death made the claims of the present harder to place indefinitely behind that future.

I still want to pursue work that takes time. I also want to ask when its useful part will reach the lives it is supposed to serve.

For an explanation, that means making contact with a decision. For an enterprise, it means examining whether the work can meet responsibilities. For a larger ambition, it means being honest about what is happening now and what remains a hope.

The questions began in a concern for livelihood. They should remain answerable to the life in which that livelihood matters.

This is the direction I want NaijaOS to take.

My experience supplies starting questions, but other people's work and evidence must be able to change the answers. I want to investigate specific settings closely enough that readers can see what enables action, what obstructs it, and what further understanding is needed.

No single description of Nigeria will be sufficient for every person or purpose.

A useful account should say where it applies. It should also leave a reader better able to investigate the circumstances beyond it.

I began with a question about what Nigerians would buy. I now see how much understanding is required to make a useful offer, build a dependable arrangement, or attempt a new possibility.

Knowing the lay of the land will never mean knowing everything.

It means learning enough to act thoughtfully, observing what the action reveals, and being willing to revise the map.
