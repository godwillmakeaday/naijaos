The Economy I Did Not Know Existed

Discovering startups and where existing skills might belong.

By Paul A. Magaji · 7 min read · How a world becomes visible

Personal essay

Download Word document

By the time I began discovering the startup economy, I was already a lawyer.

That is the part of the story I keep returning to. Companies were being created. People were building products and organising work around problems I had not learned to recognise as business opportunities. Some of that activity could have been relevant to my professional development.

Yet much of it had remained outside my attention.

I do not mean that I had never heard of a technology company. I mean that I had not understood this field well enough to see how I might participate in it. It existed in the same country in which I was trying to build a livelihood, but it was absent from much of my working picture of that country.

The discovery raised an uncomfortable question: what else could be happening around me without becoming visible as a possibility for me?

***

I had been reading and learning. Through Success Digest, I had become interested in useful information as a product. I understood the appeal of identifying a problem, preparing knowledge that addressed it, and trying to sell the result.

That gave my attention a direction. It also meant that I approached enterprise with certain forms already in mind.

When I later encountered accounts of startups and software businesses, I found people attempting things that did not fit neatly into those forms. Their work suggested other ways of connecting knowledge, products, customers, and organisations.

I had to learn what the activity involved before I could judge where I belonged in it.

The word “startup” itself was insufficient. A name for a field does not explain its work. I needed to understand particular companies, the people they served, the difficulties they faced, and what made their products useful enough for someone to adopt.

Without that understanding, I could admire an unfamiliar economy and remain outside it.

***

My legal qualification made the gap especially striking.

I had acquired a profession, but acquiring a profession had not automatically supplied a complete map of the people who might need its services. I could not assume that professional identity alone would keep my understanding of opportunity current.

Consider the questions I might ask about an unfamiliar software business. What does it promise its customers? Who does the work? Who owns what is created? How are decisions made between the people building it? What happens when a customer complains or a working relationship ends?

These are questions through which a lawyer might begin investigating a business. They do not establish that I already possess every competence required to advise it. They reveal subjects I would need to understand.

That distinction matters to me. Discovering an opportunity should also mean discovering what further preparation it demands.

I could have approached the field by assuming that every new company simply needed a lawyer. A more useful approach would have been to learn what a particular company was trying to accomplish and where my work could make a concrete contribution.

***

This experience changed how I thought about opportunity.

I had often considered it through the resources someone possessed or lacked: money, qualifications, support, connections. But here was another difficulty. A person could possess some relevant ability and still fail to recognise a setting in which it might become useful.

There is a difference between knowing that a field exists and knowing how to enter it.

A headline might announce that a company has been created. It may say little about the work inside it, the suppliers it depends upon, or the professional capabilities it needs. A successful founder can become widely visible while the ordinary work surrounding that success remains poorly understood.

I wanted explanations that reached that ordinary work.

What would someone need to learn first? Which tasks could he already perform? Who could help him understand the rest? What evidence would show that his proposed contribution was useful?

Those questions would have helped me move beyond the feeling that something important was happening elsewhere.

***

I also began learning programming.

I started with Ruby, then encountered HTML, CSS, JavaScript, and Python. My route was exploratory. I followed explanations and examples without yet possessing a complete account of how the pieces fitted together.

The experience made me more aware of the distance between using a finished product and understanding its creation.

A website could appear simple to the person visiting it. Trying to build one gave me further questions about what had to happen behind it. Each piece I learned showed me something I could attempt and something else I still needed to understand.

I would not describe those beginnings as technical mastery. They were a way of making an unfamiliar field more concrete.

They also helped me reconsider the relationship between my profession and my interests. I did not have to decide immediately that learning code meant abandoning law. I could investigate whether understanding how a product was made would improve the questions I brought to the people building it.

The value of learning could extend beyond performing every task myself.

***

There is a danger in telling this story too triumphantly.

It would be easy to say that I discovered startups, learned programming, and therefore found the future. That would give the discovery more certainty than it deserves.

An unfamiliar field can contain real opportunity and exaggerated promises. A business can be interesting without being viable. My enthusiasm cannot establish that customers want a product or that I am prepared to serve them.

Nor does discovering a fashionable field make familiar work less valuable. A workshop, a farm, a professional practice, and a software company each deserve to be understood through the work they perform and the conditions they encounter.

What I needed was a wider field of inquiry. I did not need another label under which to place all my hopes.

That requires patience. Once something previously invisible becomes exciting, there is a temptation to treat it as the answer before understanding the question it actually addresses.

***

This is part of the work I want NaijaOS to do.

I want it to explain economic activity closely enough that a reader can see possible forms of participation. That means asking more than who became successful or how much attention a company received.

For a particular enterprise, I would want to understand the customer's problem, the work involved in addressing it, and the arrangements that allow the work to continue. I would also want to know what an outsider is likely to misunderstand.

A reader should be able to leave with a better question to investigate, a skill to examine, or a person whose experience might clarify the next step.

These accounts should include the difficulties. If entering a field requires training, equipment, relationships, or time, those requirements belong in the explanation. Making an opportunity visible while concealing its demands would leave the reader with an incomplete understanding.

My own experience gives me a reason to care about that completeness.

***

I also want to remember the person who does not yet know what to search for.

Once I encounter the vocabulary of a field, it becomes easier to find explanations of it. Before that, relevant information can exist without appearing relevant to me.

That is a demanding problem for a publication. It has to connect unfamiliar work with questions a reader already understands, while preserving what makes the work unfamiliar.

For me, the starting question was how to make a living. Later, it became how my abilities could contribute to things I had not previously understood.

I would like NaijaOS to help other people make that journey with clearer explanations than I had at the beginning.

***

I cannot know what would have happened if I had discovered this economy earlier. Earlier knowledge might have changed my choices. It would not have guaranteed a successful enterprise or professional practice.

What I can examine is the gap itself.

I was qualified, curious, and interested in earning. I still lacked an adequate understanding of a field that could have mattered to my work. Recognising that gap has made me less willing to assume that the opportunities I currently see are the full range available.

It has also given me a more practical task than regretting what I missed: keep investigating where useful work is being done, learn what it requires, and test where I can contribute.

An economy can exist around me long before I understand how to enter it.

I want to become better at noticing.
