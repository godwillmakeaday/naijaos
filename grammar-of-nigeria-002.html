The Grammar of Nigeria / Essay 002

Who Owns Nigeria? Understanding Sovereignty, the People, and Political Authority

An elected office is a temporary authority, not a title deed to a country.

Paul MagajiLong-form civic education2,989 words

The Question Behind the Question

Imagine a governor speaking of a state as though it were a possession. Imagine a president describing public power as a prize won, rather than a responsibility received. Imagine a citizen approaching a government office as though seeking a personal favour from someone who owns the institution.

These are not necessarily statements that anyone has made. They are ways of imagining power. And the way we imagine power can quietly shape what we permit those who exercise it to do.

We often ask who controls Nigeria. We ask which party holds the presidency, which interests influence appointments, which region has gained advantage, or which individuals command the resources of government. These can be important questions. But they are different from another question, one that should come first:

Who owns Nigeria?

At first the question sounds like a question about property. Countries are not ordinary assets to be bought, inherited, or transferred like a parcel of land. The word owns is therefore a metaphor, and one we must handle carefully. Yet it helps expose a confusion: the person who exercises public authority is not necessarily the person to whom that authority ultimately belongs.

The task of this essay is to separate four ideas that political conversation too often merges: the people, sovereignty, government, and constitutional power.

1. What Does Sovereignty Mean?

Sovereignty is one of the largest words in political language. At its simplest, it concerns ultimate public authority. But it is used in more than one sense.

In relations among countries, sovereignty refers to the standing of a state as politically independent and not ordinarily subject to another state's authority. When we say that Nigeria is sovereign, we are speaking partly about its position in the international order.

Within a country, sovereignty raises a different question: where does ultimate political authority come from, and how may it legitimately be exercised?

These two questions should not be confused. Independence from foreign control does not answer whether domestic government is democratic. A state can be externally sovereign while its citizens possess little influence over how power is exercised. Conversely, democratic participation within a state does not eliminate the state's external obligations or the constraints of international law.

For Nigeria, the constitutional language is unusually direct. Section 14(2)(a) of the Constitution provides that sovereignty belongs to the people of Nigeria and that government derives its powers and authority through the Constitution.

Read that sentence slowly. It contains a political theory in miniature.

First, it identifies the people as the holders of sovereignty. Second, it identifies the Constitution as the legal channel through which government receives authority. Third, it presents government not as the original owner of public power, but as an institution exercising constitutionally derived power.

The sentence is not a licence for every individual to make whatever demand they wish in the name of the people. It is a principle about the source and justification of the political order.

And it invites the next question: if sovereignty belongs to the people, why do public officials possess powers that ordinary citizens do not?

2. The Difference Between Having Authority and Owning It

Consider a judge. The judge has authority to determine disputes within the court's jurisdiction. But the judge does not own the court, the law, or the citizens who appear before it.

Consider a legislator. The legislator participates in making laws within the legislature's constitutional competence. But the legislator does not own the legislative power as personal property.

Consider a president. The president exercises executive power of the Federation, subject to constitutional and statutory limits. But the presidency is an office within the state, not an estate transferred to its temporary occupant.

This is the distinction between holding an office and possessing a country.

The Constitution distributes state powers among institutions. Section 4 vests federal legislative power in the National Assembly and provides for legislative powers of state Houses of Assembly. Section 5 vests federal executive power in the President, and state executive power in governors, subject to constitutional limits. Section 6 vests judicial power in the courts.

The distribution matters because it makes public authority intelligible. If someone issues a command, makes a rule, spends public money, or restricts another person's freedom, a citizen is entitled to ask: which office, which power, which legal provision, and which procedure support this act?

An answer such as “because I am in charge” does not by itself establish lawful authority.

For civic reasoning, this produces a practical rule: power should be traced to its source, scope, purpose, and limits.

3. If the People Are Sovereign, Why Is the Constitution Supreme?

Nigeria's Constitution begins with another striking claim. Section 1 establishes its supremacy and binding force over authorities and persons throughout the Federation. It also rejects unconstitutional seizure or control of government and provides that inconsistent laws are void to the extent of their inconsistency.

How can the people be sovereign while the Constitution is supreme?

There is no necessary contradiction, because the statements operate at different levels.

Popular sovereignty addresses the foundational political claim about where the authority of government comes from. Constitutional supremacy addresses the legal rule for how that authority is organised, limited, and exercised within the existing order.

To say that the people are sovereign is not to say that a crowd, a political party, or even a winning electoral majority may disregard the Constitution at will. And to say that the Constitution is supreme is not to say that its provisions are beyond political argument or lawful reform.

Indeed, Section 9 provides a formal process for constitutional alteration involving the National Assembly and state legislatures, with heightened requirements for particular provisions. A demand for change is therefore different from a claim that change has already acquired legal force.

This distinction matters whenever political arguments leap from an appeal to “the will of the people” to a conclusion about what a particular official may lawfully do.

A claim to popular approval is not a substitute for constitutional competence. Yet an action may be technically lawful and still deserve serious democratic criticism.

The Constitution tells us what the law authorises. Political reasoning also asks whether authorised power is being used well.

4. Who Are “the People”?

The expression sounds simple until we attempt to identify its members and understand its meaning.

Are the people those who voted for the winning candidate? Obviously not. An election produces an officeholder; it does not transform supporters of the losing candidate into political outsiders.

Are the people only adults eligible to vote? That would leave children, and others without the franchise, outside the broader community whose welfare government is meant to serve.

Are the people only the most numerous ethnic, religious, or regional group? Such a definition would mistake the logic of political majority for the existence of a common civic community.

The people of Nigeria include citizens with divergent political beliefs, languages, local histories, interests, and identities. They may disagree deeply over what the state should do while remaining members of the same political community.

This makes the idea of popular sovereignty more demanding than the slogan “the majority has spoken.” Elections are a vital means of choosing representatives and determining who may exercise specified powers. They are not a transfer of citizenship from opponents to victors.

Nor is majority rule the entire content of constitutional democracy. The protection of rights, adherence to lawful procedures, and recognition of political equality also matter. Section 14(3) and (4), for example, articulate principles of federal character and diversity in the composition and conduct of public institutions.

A useful test follows. When someone claims to speak for “the people,” ask: which people, by what representative process, for what decision, and with what regard for those who disagree?

5. An Election Is an Authorisation, Not an Abdication

An election has consequences. It can give a person a legitimate claim to occupy an office for a prescribed term, exercise enumerated powers, and pursue a political programme within constitutional limits.

But it does not mean the electorate has surrendered every future judgment to the victor.

A voter may support a candidate and later oppose a policy. A citizen who voted against a candidate remains entitled to equal treatment. A government with a broad electoral mandate is still constrained by laws, institutional checks, and constitutional rights.

Political authority is therefore better understood as conditional and structured than as absolute and personal.

This does not imply that any unpopular decision is unlawful. A government sometimes has to make choices that are necessary, contested, or initially misunderstood. Nor does declining popularity automatically terminate a lawful term of office.

We should keep three separate inquiries in view:

  • Legal authority: Did an institution act within the powers and procedures established by law?
  • Democratic authorisation: Through what elections, representation, or other legitimate process was the institution empowered to act?
  • Public justification: Is the use of that authority defensible in light of its purposes, consequences, and effects on the governed?

The answers can differ. An official act may be legally competent yet politically unwise. A popular proposal may be constitutionally impermissible. A necessary policy may require a government to explain itself more carefully rather than simply invoke the election that brought it to office.

The distinction protects both constitutional stability and democratic criticism.

6. For Whose Benefit Is Power Exercised?

Section 14(2)(b) identifies the security and welfare of the people as the primary purpose of government. Section 14(2)(c) speaks to participation by the people in their government.

These provisions are not decorations. They tell us something important about the constitutional imagination of public office: government exists for purposes beyond its own preservation.

At the same time, intellectual care is required. Section 14 appears in Chapter II, the Fundamental Objectives and Directive Principles of State Policy. Under Section 6(6)(c), these objectives are generally not directly justiciable merely by alleging that government has failed to satisfy them, except as otherwise provided by the Constitution or applicable law.

This makes a distinction essential. A constitutional objective is not automatically the same thing as an immediately enforceable individual remedy.

But the lack of an automatic remedy does not empty the objective of political or interpretive significance. It remains part of the constitutional framework by which public policy and institutional conduct may be assessed. In appropriate circumstances, legislation and other constitutional provisions can give concrete legal effect to related obligations.

Imagine a government announcing an expensive project. The fact that it is publicly funded does not tell us whether it is necessary, fairly procured, competently delivered, or genuinely useful. Nor does public criticism alone establish that it is unlawful.

The more disciplined inquiry asks: What public purpose is claimed? Which institution authorised it? What funds were approved? What evidence supports the expected benefits? Who bears the costs? What oversight applies?

That is how a declaration about the people's welfare can become an instrument of public reasoning.

7. Public Office Is Not Personal Ownership

Public institutions have offices, records, budgets, land, buildings, vehicles, contracts, and powers. Different legal regimes determine who may hold, use, manage, transfer, or account for these resources. Not every public asset is owned in the same legal way.

It would therefore be imprecise to say that each citizen personally owns a fractional share of every government building, or that an elected official can dispose of an asset merely because he directs the agency responsible for it.

The better idea is public stewardship: holders of public authority should exercise powers and administer resources for legally authorised public purposes and subject to accountability. This is a civic and ethical principle, supported in different contexts by constitutional, administrative, financial, and other legal duties; it should not be mistaken for a single rule of private property law.

Think of a government hospital. Its purpose is not exhausted by the identity of the governor who commissioned it, the minister who approved its budget, or the official who appoints its administrators. Those individuals may perform important functions. But the public significance of the hospital is measured by its lawful operation and its service to the community.

Or consider a government office in which a citizen is treated as though a routine lawful service depends upon the personal goodwill of a public servant.

The citizen should be able to ask whether the service is a legal entitlement, a discretionary decision governed by stated criteria, or a benefit for which the law provides a formal application process.

Such questions move public life from personal dependence toward institutional accountability.

8. The Harder Historical Question

There is a complication that a serious project on Nigerian sovereignty cannot ignore.

The preamble to the 1999 Constitution speaks in the voice of “we the people.” But the Constitution emerged through a particular historical transition rather than a fresh nationwide referendum approving every provision.

This does not, by itself, settle the question of its current legal validity. The Constitution is the operative supreme law of the Nigerian constitutional order, as altered. Yet it does create a legitimate subject for historical and political inquiry: how should we distinguish the constitutional language of popular authorship from the actual process by which a constitution came into force?

One can recognise the legal authority of a constitutional text while questioning the history of its adoption or arguing for its reform. Those are different acts of reasoning.

Likewise, one can advocate constitutional change without claiming that a preferred change has already become law.

A mature civic conversation should be able to hold these distinctions together. Neither an appeal to history nor a declaration of constitutional supremacy should be allowed to substitute for careful argument.

9. Five Questions to Ask Whenever Power Is Exercised

The question “Who owns Nigeria?” becomes especially useful when converted from a slogan into a method.

First: Who is acting? Identify the particular institution or official. “Government” is often too vague to be a useful answer.

Second: Where does the authority come from? Look for the relevant constitutional provision, statute, regulation, delegation, judicial order, or other lawful source.

Third: What are the limits? Ask whether the authority is federal, state, or local; whether it belongs to a particular branch; and what procedures must be followed.

Fourth: For whose benefit and with what effects? Examine the stated purpose, available evidence, distribution of benefits and burdens, and consequences for rights and welfare.

Fifth: How can the decision be questioned or corrected? Consider legislative scrutiny, administrative review, litigation where available, elections, public participation, access to information, and other lawful accountability mechanisms.

These questions do not guarantee agreement. They improve the conditions under which disagreement can be meaningful.

A citizen who asks them is no longer required to choose immediately between applause and outrage. The citizen has begun an investigation.

Conclusion: A Country Is Not a Prize

Who owns Nigeria?

In the literal sense of property, the question is misleading. The Nigerian state is not a company whose shares belong to politicians or a private estate that an election winner inherits.

In the constitutional language of sovereignty, the answer begins with the people. But the answer is incomplete unless we add that the authority of government is organised through the Constitution, divided among institutions, exercised through lawful procedures, and subject to limits.

The president is not Nigeria. A governor is not a state. A party is not the people. A majority is not the whole political community. And criticism of a government is not, by that fact alone, hostility to the country.

The deeper meaning of popular sovereignty is not that every citizen becomes a ruler. It is that rulers must be able to account for the authority they exercise over citizens.

A country becomes easier to dominate when public office is mistaken for private possession. It becomes easier to understand—and potentially easier to govern well—when citizens learn to distinguish power from ownership, legality from wisdom, electoral victory from unlimited authority, and government from the people it serves.

The next time someone claims to own the political future of Nigeria, we need not begin by shouting in agreement or disagreement.

We can begin with a question: By whose authority, under which law, and for what public purpose?

That is not the end of civic reasoning. It is a serious beginning.

Questions for the Reader

  1. What is the difference between a government holding power and the people being sovereign?
  2. Can a public official act lawfully while still using power unwisely? What would establish each claim?
  3. If an election authorises government, what forms of accountability remain necessary between elections?
  4. When a political leader says a decision represents “the will of the people,” what evidence should we ask for?
  5. What is the difference between a constitutional objective and a directly enforceable legal right?
  6. How can people question the legitimacy or history of a constitution without confusing that inquiry with the current law?
  7. Which public decision in your own community could you examine using the five-question authority test?
