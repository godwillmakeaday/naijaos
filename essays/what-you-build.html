Who Can Take Away What You Build

Ownership, dependence and the security of productive work.

By Paul A. Magaji · 7 min read · Who gets room to attempt

Personal essay

Download Word document

Before I understood how someone became wealthy, I worried about what could happen to him afterwards.

I carried a fear that a rich person might attract the attention of people powerful enough to interfere with his freedom. The more he had built, the more there might be to take away.

I cannot now give that childhood belief the status of a researched account of Nigeria. It was an expectation I had formed, and it mattered because of the future it encouraged me to imagine.

A government job seemed to offer security. Wealth outside it did not necessarily seem to offer freedom.

Looking back, I can see a question beneath both thoughts: what could I rely upon without placing too much of my life at someone else's discretion?

***

This question complicates the usual account of ambition.

I wanted to become productive, useful, and independent. Earning through something I created could help me provide for myself and meet responsibilities. But I also wanted to know whether what I earned would remain available to the life it was meant to support.

It was possible to imagine success and still feel vulnerable within it.

The concern was not confined to physical possessions. Somebody might control access to something important. A relationship could carry conditions. A person who could open an opportunity might also appear capable of closing it.

These were possibilities that interested and worried me. To understand any particular one, I would have to investigate how the dependence actually worked.

Otherwise, I could spend my life responding to a general fear without knowing where a specific danger began.

***

The first distinction I need is between different meanings of “take away.”

A person can lose something through his own error, a change in demand, the failure of a working arrangement, or another person's misconduct. An official decision can affect an enterprise. So can the withdrawal of a customer.

These events may all feel like a loss of control. They are not interchangeable.

If I describe every setback as someone taking what I built, I conceal questions I need to answer. Did I misunderstand what I possessed? Had I assumed that an arrangement would continue? Was a promise broken? Did the work cease to serve the people it depended upon?

An explanation should identify what happened closely enough for a response to become possible.

That discipline matters especially when a fear has existed longer than my understanding of its subject.

***

I also need to examine the phrase “what I built.”

It expresses effort and attachment. It does not, on its own, establish every part of ownership or control.

Suppose, as an illustration, I create a publication that readers reach through an account someone else administers. I may have written the work while remaining dependent on another person for access to it.

Suppose I develop a business with a colleague. We may share enthusiasm while holding different assumptions about who can decide what happens next.

These examples do not prove misconduct. They reveal questions that ought to be made explicit.

What exactly belongs to whom? Who can make decisions? What must another person continue to provide? What happens if the relationship ends?

The answers require attention before a disagreement makes them urgent.

***

My interest in these questions has a personal history.

At school, pain showed me one way in which somebody could impose his will. Later, stories such as 24 and The Blacklist drew my attention to other forms of leverage.

What did a person need to protect? What could be used to compel him? What choice remained when somebody else controlled the thing he could not afford to lose?

Fiction did not provide evidence about the operation of actual institutions. It gave a setting in which I could recognise the questions I brought with me.

When I began thinking seriously about enterprise, those questions followed.

I wanted a livelihood that could provide more room to act. I needed to understand the dependencies that would come with it.

***

There is a difficulty here for my idea of independence.

Useful work often involves relying on people. Someone supplies a material, maintains a service, performs a task, pays an invoice, or contributes knowledge I do not possess.

If I define independence as needing nobody, I set myself a standard that gives cooperation very little place.

A more useful aim, for me, is to understand the relationships on which the work rests and the choices available within them.

Can I explain what each party has undertaken? Do we understand our respective responsibilities? Can the arrangement survive a disagreement? If it cannot continue, what would an orderly ending require?

These questions do not remove dependence. They make it more visible and more open to judgment.

That is a different ambition from trying to become untouchable.

***

I am particularly interested in the difference between personal reassurance and a dependable arrangement.

A person may say, “There will be no problem.” His goodwill may be sincere. Yet a productive life can extend beyond the circumstances in which that reassurance was given.

People change their minds. Responsibilities change. A person may become unavailable. Other people may interpret the same understanding differently.

I would want to know what remains intelligible when the original conversation can no longer settle the matter.

This is not a reason to regard every relationship with suspicion. It is a reason to make important expectations clear enough for the people concerned to examine them.

Trust can include a willingness to explain what we are asking each other to rely upon.

***

The same care must apply to my own conduct.

If I am concerned about someone using dependence against me, I need to ask how I behave when another person depends upon something I control.

Would I change an understanding because I had acquired more power to do so? Would I leave someone's contribution unrecognised because he lacked the means to insist upon recognition? Could the people working with me understand the decisions that affected them?

These questions are uncomfortable because they do not allow me to treat vulnerability as something experienced only by myself.

The freedom I want to build has to leave room for other people's freedom.

Otherwise, success could place me in a position to reproduce the kind of insecurity I feared.

***

For NaijaOS, I see an inquiry into how productive work acquires durability.

It would require particular cases rather than a single verdict about Nigeria. A workshop, a professional practice, a software service, and a trading business may depend upon different arrangements.

I would want to understand what each person believed was secure, what actually sustained it, and where a disagreement or interruption exposed a weakness.

An account should distinguish what participants say from what can be established. It should also examine the owner's decisions. Not every vulnerability originates in somebody else's hostility.

Sometimes an assumption has remained untested because the work was going well. Sometimes an important task has remained with one person because nobody planned for his absence.

These are subjects to investigate, not conclusions to impose on every enterprise.

***

I also want to keep this inquiry from becoming a reason never to begin.

A person can seek so much reassurance that he postpones useful work indefinitely. My childhood fear could easily support that habit: first become certain that nothing can be taken away, then attempt something worth building.

I cannot obtain that certainty.

What I can do is make the proposed work more understandable, identify important dependencies, and take responsibility for the decisions within my reach. I can learn enough to distinguish a concern that needs action from a possibility I am merely imagining.

For a particular legal or commercial problem, that means establishing the facts and examining the relevant arrangements. A general essay cannot settle those questions in advance.

The purpose of this reflection is to improve the questions I bring to the work.

***

I still understand why wealth once seemed capable of making someone vulnerable.

The more a person cares about what he has built, the more consequential its loss can become. But I no longer want that observation to stand alone as my explanation of success.

I want to know what the person has created, how it is held and maintained, whom it serves, and what makes its continuation dependable.

That is a more demanding inquiry than either admiring wealth or fearing it.

I began with the question of who could take away what I built.

I now also ask what I must understand, clarify, and sustain so that the work can endure.
