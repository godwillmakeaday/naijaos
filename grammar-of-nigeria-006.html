The Grammar of Nigeria / Essay 006

The Constitution and Political Reality: Why the Law on Paper Is Not Always the Country in Practice

The Constitution tells us how public power is supposed to operate. Institutions and citizens' experiences tell us how it actually operates.

Paul MagajiLong-form civic education3,891 words

The Country Described and the Country Experienced

Imagine a citizen reading the Nigerian Constitution for the first time. The text speaks of constitutional supremacy, democracy, justice, the security and welfare of the people, and obligations imposed upon public institutions. It describes offices, distributes authority and provides rules intended to restrain arbitrary power.

Now imagine that same citizen leaving the reading room and entering a public office. A lawful application may receive no response. A community may have an elected representative yet struggle to understand how its public funds were spent. A person may possess a constitutional right while lacking the means to obtain an effective remedy when it is violated.

What should the citizen conclude? That the Constitution is false? That public complaints are exaggerated? That laws have no significance until every official obeys them?

None of those conclusions follows automatically.

A Constitution is not a daily report of everything a government does. Nor is a citizen's experience, however painful or illuminating, by itself a complete account of an entire constitutional system. The text and the experience belong to different levels of inquiry. They must be connected, but not confused.

This essay completes the first foundational sequence of The Grammar of Nigeria. We have considered nation, state, country, sovereignty, federalism, democracy, government and officeholding. We must now confront the question that makes those distinctions politically urgent: How do we move from knowing the rules of Nigeria to understanding the reality of Nigeria?

1. A Constitution Is a Rulebook, Not a Photograph

A photograph attempts to capture something as it appears at a particular moment. A Constitution does something different. It establishes, authorises, prohibits, distributes and directs. It tells institutions what they may do, how they are formed and, in many cases, what they must not do.

Section 1 of the Constitution of the Federal Republic of Nigeria 1999, as amended, establishes constitutional supremacy. Its legal claim is that public authorities and persons are bound by the Constitution and that inconsistent laws yield to it. This is a rule of validity and authority. It is not a prediction that every institution will obey every rule on every occasion.

Consider the speed limit on a road. The existence of the limit does not prove that every driver obeys it. A photograph of one speeding driver does not, in turn, prove that the limit does not exist. To understand the road, we need to know the rule, the prevalence of compliance, the enforcement arrangements and the consequences of violation.

The analogy is imperfect—constitutional authority is more complex than a traffic rule—but it teaches an essential habit. Do not read a prescription as though it were a description.

If a Constitution provides for democratic government, that provision does not settle the empirical question of whether particular elections are well administered. If it provides for a right, it does not establish that every person enjoys effective access to that right. And if an official violates a constitutional duty, the violation does not necessarily erase the duty.

A serious society must know both what its law demands and what its institutions deliver.

2. Three Levels of Constitutional Reality

I propose a simple analytical framework for this series: the text, the institution and the experience.

The first level is constitutional design: the provisions that create an office, allocate powers, establish rights or declare objectives. Here our evidence is principally the Constitution, relevant legislation and authoritative judicial interpretation.

The second level is institutional operation: what officials, agencies, courts, legislatures and administrators actually do. Here we examine decisions, procedures, budgets, records, enforcement practices and measurable conduct.

The third level is lived consequence: what citizens experience because of these arrangements. Here we investigate whether people can obtain services, exercise rights, participate meaningfully, receive fair treatment or secure remedies.

These levels can disagree without any of them becoming irrelevant. A legally well-designed institution may operate poorly. An efficient office may deliver services while violating important procedural safeguards. A citizen may experience a desirable outcome because of an unofficial personal connection rather than because institutions have become fairer.

Conversely, an administration may experience a temporary failure without the underlying constitutional rule being defective. One cannot diagnose the cause merely from the existence of a disappointing result.

This three-level distinction can discipline public debate. Instead of saying only, 'The system is broken,' we can ask: Is the difficulty in the legal design, the behaviour of the institution, or the citizen's access to its benefits? Is it possible that all three contribute? What evidence would distinguish among them?

A framework does not settle the argument. It makes the argument investigable.

3. Constitutional Supremacy Requires Human Institutions

The supremacy of a Constitution is a profound legal proposition, but the written page cannot investigate misconduct, refuse an unlawful instruction, publish a budget or execute a judgment. These acts require institutions and people.

Courts interpret law and decide disputes within their jurisdiction. Legislatures make laws and exercise oversight according to their constitutional powers. Executive authorities administer programmes and implement lawful decisions. Auditors, administrative officers, election bodies, police officers, journalists, civil society organisations and citizens can also influence whether a constitutional promise acquires practical force.

These actors possess different powers, interests and constraints. Some are independent by constitutional design; others are connected through appointment, funding, supervision or political incentives. Legal powers may be clear while administrative capacity is weak. A judgment may be authoritative while compliance depends on many subsequent decisions.

This means that the route from constitutional rule to public outcome is rarely a straight line. It is closer to a chain: norm → institution → decision → resources → implementation → oversight → experience.

A broken link matters. Funds may be authorised but not released. Funds may be released without being spent properly. A court may pronounce a remedy but encounter non-compliance. An agency may establish an accessible complaint procedure yet lack staff to investigate complaints.

We should not assume corruption whenever a service fails; nor should we accept 'lack of capacity' as an unquestionable excuse. Different explanations require different evidence. Sometimes a problem is deliberate abuse. Sometimes it is administrative incapacity, conflicting rules, inadequate coordination or a policy that has been poorly designed.

To speak accurately about political reality, we need to find the broken link before deciding who is responsible for it.

4. The Difficult Distinction Between Duty and Enforceability

One of the most revealing places to examine the distance between constitutional language and practical consequence is Chapter II of Nigeria's Constitution: the Fundamental Objectives and Directive Principles of State Policy.

Section 13 places a duty upon organs of government and authorities exercising public powers to conform to, observe and apply Chapter II. Section 14 states that Nigeria is to be based on democratic principles and social justice and identifies the security and welfare of the people as the primary purpose of government. Other Chapter II provisions address matters such as social policy, education, the economy and environmental protection.

Yet an important legal qualification appears in Section 6(6)(c). Subject to the Constitution's exceptions and the legal effect of implementing legislation, courts generally cannot be invited to adjudicate a claim simply on the allegation that an act or omission fails to conform to Chapter II objectives. This is commonly described as the problem of non-justiciability.

A crucial distinction follows: a constitutional objective may impose a public obligation without automatically conferring an individually enforceable cause of action in every circumstance.

Non-justiciability does not mean that the objectives were written for decoration. Nor does it mean that legislative action cannot give them practical legal effect. Nigerian judicial reasoning, including the Supreme Court's treatment of legislation connected with Chapter II objectives, shows why the relationship among Section 6(6)(c), the legislative powers in the Second Schedule and implementing statutes must be examined carefully.

We must also distinguish Chapter II from Chapter IV, which protects specified fundamental rights and provides routes to redress. Even then, the scope of a claim, the proper forum, evidence, procedure and available remedies matter. The presence of a constitutional right is not identical to an automatic victory in court.

For civic reasoning, this is not a technicality to hide in a footnote. It is the difference between three questions: What does the Constitution value? What does the law currently require in a legally enforceable way? And what institutions or legislation would be necessary to turn an aspiration into a more effective guarantee?

Confusing these questions breeds unrealistic expectations. Ignoring any of them breeds unnecessary cynicism.

5. An Illustration: Local Government Autonomy

There is a contemporary Nigerian example that brings the distinction into sharp focus.

On 11 July 2024, the Supreme Court delivered a consequential decision concerning financial autonomy for democratically elected local government councils. The judgment addressed the control of allocations and condemned arrangements by which state authorities retained or used funds meant for local governments. It also addressed the constitutional requirement for democratically elected councils rather than caretaker arrangements.

That decision was not merely a statement of political preference. It was an authoritative judicial act concerning constitutional arrangements and the legal obligations of public authorities.

But a judgment, however significant, does not transfer funds by moving words across a page. Administrative accounts, payment systems, institutional cooperation, compliance procedures and monitoring must all translate the legal rule into operations.

Reports published in 2026 continued to describe difficulties and uneven implementation of the ruling. Such reporting does not by itself establish the position in every council, and individual claims require verification. But it makes the analytical point clear: the date of a judgment and the date of its complete practical fulfilment need not be the same.

We can analyse this example across the three levels. Text and interpretation: what do the Constitution and the Supreme Court decision require? Institutional operation: who controls payment channels, council accounts, elections and expenditure decisions? Lived consequence: does a resident see greater transparency, responsive service or meaningful local accountability?

Even full financial autonomy would not guarantee capable administration. It could remove a constraint while leaving problems of competence, procurement, local political competition or oversight unresolved. Conversely, weak service delivery cannot justify withholding lawful autonomy.

Here is the deeper lesson: a legal victory is not the same as a completed institutional reform; an institutional reform is not the same as a public outcome.

6. Why Laws and Institutions Sometimes Move at Different Speeds

It is tempting to imagine a single cause for the distance between written rules and actual conditions. But political reality is seldom so accommodating.

Institutions are shaped by incentives. An official may be praised for announcing a project, yet face fewer rewards for maintaining it. A budget may pay for construction without reliably funding operation. A ministry may possess formal authority but depend on another institution for information, approvals or resources.

Institutions are also shaped by habits. Procedures persist because people have learned to work around them. Informal relationships become substitutes for predictable service. Citizens may begin to seek patrons rather than procedures because experience has taught them that the latter are unreliable.

Once such expectations become widespread, they can reproduce the very failures people deplore. If citizens believe only personal influence works, they invest in personal influence. The resulting dependence makes impersonal institutions even harder to build.

Yet the opposite possibility exists. Consistent enforcement can gradually change expectations. Reliable publication of decisions can make secrecy more costly. Transparent service standards can make favouritism easier to expose. The improvement of a modest administrative process can make constitutional values more visible than a hundred speeches praising them.

This is why civic analysis should be interested in paperwork, systems and ordinary procedures—not only dramatic national events. Political reality is produced in the everyday repetition of institutional acts.

7. When Should We Amend the Constitution?

Whenever a serious problem arises, constitutional amendment often enters public debate. Sometimes this is exactly the right conversation. A constitutional rule may allocate authority badly, leave essential questions unresolved or create incentives that obstruct accountability.

But sometimes an institution already has lawful authority to act and simply fails to use it. Altering the Constitution will not necessarily correct a defective procurement process, repair an administrative database or produce an honest investigation.

The distinction can be made through a practical test.

First, identify the outcome people want. Second, identify the existing legal power or duty related to that outcome. Third, determine whether the law prohibits the desired action, permits it but is ambiguous, or already requires it. Fourth, examine implementation records. Only then ask whether legal alteration, institutional reform, resources, enforcement or some combination is the most plausible remedy.

A Constitution can be too rigid for changing circumstances. It can also become a convenient scapegoat for officials unwilling to perform duties they already possess.

The question is not whether Nigeria should amend its Constitution or preserve it unchanged. The question is more precise: Which defect are we trying to repair, and where in the chain from law to lived outcome is that defect located?

This question is especially important in a federation. A matter may involve federal, state and local actors; a sound diagnosis identifies the precise distribution of lawful powers rather than blaming 'government' as though it were a single undifferentiated person.

8. The Citizen as Investigator, Not Merely Spectator

A citizen does not need to be a constitutional lawyer to practise careful public reasoning. The first requirement is to become more exact about claims.

Suppose a public school has no functioning toilets. An angry statement—'The Constitution has failed this community'—may communicate genuine distress, but it does not yet explain the problem. Which institution owns or manages the school? Which government level has responsibility under the relevant legal framework? Was funding approved? What did the procurement record show? Who was to inspect the works? What changed over time?

We can call the method the Constitution-to-Reality Inquiry:

  1. Locate the rule. Find the relevant constitutional provision, statute, regulation or formal policy. Record what it actually says, not what people say it says.
  2. Locate the authority. Identify which office or institution has legal responsibility, and distinguish the power to make a rule from the duty to implement it.
  3. Locate the decision. Establish whether a budget, approval, appointment, contract, court order or administrative instruction was made.
  4. Locate the evidence. Seek official records, reliable data, independent observation and the experiences of affected people. Distinguish allegations from verified facts.
  5. Locate the gap. Compare the legal expectation, institutional conduct and lived outcome. Do not assume a single cause before testing alternatives.
  6. Locate the remedy. Ask whether the problem calls for compliance, judicial review, legislative oversight, better administration, disclosure, budget correction or reform of the law itself.
  7. Return and test. Check whether the proposed intervention changed anything. A promise and a press release are not evidence of a completed result.

The method is not designed to suppress indignation. It is designed to make indignation better informed and more useful.

Where relevant, Nigeria's Freedom of Information Act 2011 supplies a legal route for requesting records from covered institutions, subject to the Act's procedures and exemptions. That instrument does not eliminate difficulties in obtaining information. But it shows how a civic question can be converted into a specific request capable of producing evidence.

9. What Counts as Evidence of Political Reality?

We should not assume that only official records are credible. Official records may be incomplete, delayed or wrong. But neither should we assume that a compelling anecdote represents the whole country.

A powerful complaint may identify a real injustice without establishing its frequency. A national statistic may reveal a pattern without explaining the experience of a particular community. A judicial finding may establish a legal conclusion without measuring the social consequences of compliance.

Different questions require different evidence. To investigate whether a rule exists, inspect the legal text. To investigate whether money was allocated, examine budget and financial records. To investigate whether a service works, combine administrative data with observation and user experience. To investigate whether an institution discriminates, examine relevant rules, patterns of treatment, comparable cases and possible alternative explanations.

The National Bureau of Statistics, public audit bodies, court judgments and agency records can be valuable starting points. Independent journalism, academic research and credible civil society investigations can test or complicate official claims.

Evidence should be dated and geographically specific. A study of one state is not automatically a description of all thirty-six. An institutional practice documented in 2022 may have changed by 2026. A proposed constitutional amendment is not an enacted one. And the publication of a government policy is not evidence that the policy has been fully delivered.

One of the habits this series should teach is the ability to say, without embarrassment, 'We do not yet know.' That sentence is not a failure of civic understanding. It is often the beginning of it.

10. Constitutional Faith Without Constitutional Illusion

Two inadequate attitudes confront people who examine the distance between constitutional promises and political experience.

The first is naïveté: because the Constitution contains admirable declarations, one assumes that the country must already embody them. The second is despair: because the country falls short of those declarations, one concludes that the Constitution has no serious value.

Both fail to distinguish a standard from compliance with the standard.

The fact that an official violates a rule can be a reason to insist on the rule, not evidence that the rule is worthless. Equally, the existence of a good rule does not excuse poor implementation or remove the need to revise a defective institutional design.

An effective constitutional culture must hold two ideas together. The written law is a source of authority, standards and procedures that constrain public power. And the experience of citizens is indispensable evidence for judging whether those constraints and obligations have practical meaning.

Neither law without evidence nor experience without concepts is sufficient.

A Constitution can give citizens language with which to name an abuse, a standard by which to judge a policy, a procedure through which to seek a remedy and a structure through which power may be changed peacefully. But these possibilities become reality only through institutions and people that make them work.

There is a special danger when politics becomes a contest between those who cite constitutional text to deny suffering and those who cite suffering to deny constitutional law. Both sides lose the opportunity to investigate and repair the connection between them.

11. A More Exact Language for Nigerian Public Life

After reading this essay, we should be more reluctant to use certain public expressions carelessly.

Instead of merely saying, 'The government failed,' ask which institution had what duty, and at what stage performance failed.

Instead of merely saying, 'The Constitution guarantees this,' ask whether the provision establishes an enforceable right, a constitutional objective, an institutional competence or a political principle, and what legislation or remedy gives it effect.

Instead of saying, 'A court has settled the problem,' ask whether the decision has been implemented and what evidence demonstrates compliance.

Instead of saying, 'We need a new Constitution,' ask whether the particular difficulty arises from the existing constitutional text or from a failure to apply it.

Instead of saying, 'That policy worked,' ask for whom, where, when, against what baseline and at what cost.

Greater precision will not eliminate disagreement. It may reveal that people who appear to disagree are answering different questions. It may also reveal disagreements that are real and consequential—but now capable of being examined in public.

This is not an attempt to make political discussion emotionless. It is an effort to give public emotion a sharper intelligence.

Conclusion: Between the Promise and the Practice

A country lives under rules, but its citizens live through institutions.

The Nigerian Constitution describes a political order and expresses fundamental commitments. It distributes public powers, states limits, establishes rights and directs attention toward public purposes. Its importance does not depend upon the fiction that every office faithfully performs its duties.

At the same time, the dignity of constitutional language cannot substitute for the realities of lawful administration, fair treatment, reliable services and remedies against abuse.

We should therefore refuse two shortcuts: treating the constitutional text as a complete picture of Nigeria, and treating every failure in Nigeria as proof that constitutional order is meaningless.

Between these shortcuts lies the work of civic intelligence. We identify the rule. We identify the institution. We investigate the conduct. We examine the experience. We ask what explains the difference—and what might change it.

This is where the first journey through The Grammar of Nigeria arrives. We began with words such as nation, state and country. We passed through sovereignty, federalism, democracy, government and public office. We now see why those concepts matter: they let us distinguish what Nigeria is legally organised to be from how its people actually experience its political life.

The Constitution is not the finished country. It is part of the framework within which the country is governed, judged, contested and continually made.

The task of a thinking citizen is neither to worship the written promise nor to dismiss it. It is to learn how to measure the distance between promise and practice—and to understand what it would take to close it.

Questions for the Reader

  1. What is the difference between a constitutional declaration, a legally enforceable right and a policy promise?
  2. Can an institution operate legally while still failing to deliver satisfactory public outcomes?
  3. What additional evidence is needed before concluding that a public failure was caused by corruption rather than incompetence, unclear authority or lack of resources?
  4. If a court rules against a government practice, what would count as proof that the ruling has been implemented?
  5. When does a public problem require constitutional amendment, and when does it require compliance with existing law?
  6. How might the experience of citizens reveal a problem that official institutional reports overlook?
  7. Can one negative experience support a claim about the whole Nigerian state? What further evidence would be necessary?
  8. Which public problem in your community could you investigate using the seven steps of the Constitution-to-Reality Inquiry?
