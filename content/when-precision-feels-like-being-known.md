# When Precision Feels Like Being Known
Understanding particular needs as an act of recognition

By Paul A. Magaji

I have been thinking about why precision matters so much.

My first thought was practical. If we understand a particular need, we can produce something more useful for the person experiencing it. We can ask better questions, explain the relevant difficulties and design a response that fits.

But there is another dimension. Precision can make a person feel known.

Trying to understand someone’s particular circumstances is, in one sense, like trying to learn their name. We are moving beyond the crowd. We are recognising that there is a person here whose situation deserves attention.

A name says whom we are addressing. An accurate understanding of someone’s circumstances suggests that we have been listening.

## From “people like that” to “someone like me”

Imagine opening an article about money. It tells you to budget carefully, save consistently and prepare for emergencies. You may agree with everything it says.

Then you encounter another article. It describes the difficulty of managing household expenses when income arrives unpredictably. It recognises that a good business day may be followed by several weak ones, while food, transport and family obligations continue.

If that is your situation, your response may change. You are no longer simply agreeing with general principles. You are recognising your own difficulty in the explanation.

You might think: this person understands the problem I am trying to solve.

The article has crossed a distance. It has moved from discussing a subject to recognising a situation. That movement is part of what makes precision valuable.

## The meaning of a name

There is a familiar saying about how much people enjoy hearing their own names. I think the significance goes beyond the sound.

A name can interrupt anonymity. Someone has distinguished us from everyone else in the room. We have become the particular person being addressed.

Of course, a person can remember my name while understanding very little about me. They can also repeat it mechanically, making the interaction feel calculated.

The deeper experience comes when recognition is supported by attention.

Someone remembers a concern I raised. They ask about a difficulty without making me explain everything again. They notice that a recommendation which works for others may be unsuitable for my circumstances.

That is a more substantial form of recognition. It tells me that something about my situation has entered their thinking.

## Precision as recognition

This helps me understand what I want specialised publications and services to accomplish.

A publication can identify a group without understanding it. It can place “for entrepreneurs” in a headline, add a few familiar expressions and continue offering the same general advice.

Recognition requires more work. What kind of entrepreneur? At what stage? Facing which decision? With what resources, responsibilities and alternatives?

A furniture maker waiting for customers to settle their balances may experience cash pressure differently from a consultant paid in advance. Both operate businesses. The arrangements through which money enters and leaves those businesses matter.

If our explanation ignores those differences, the audience label contributes little.

But when the explanation accurately describes how the difficulty arises, someone may feel that their experience has finally become visible.

Precision begins to feel personal because the work reflects circumstances that are personally consequential.

## When someone gives your difficulty words

There is a particular relief in encountering an explanation for something we have experienced but struggled to express.

We may have felt the problem without being able to organise it. Perhaps we blamed ourselves for failing to follow advice that assumed conditions we did not possess. Perhaps we knew a process was difficult but could not identify which part made it difficult.

Then someone describes the mechanism.

They explain why a predictable monthly budget may require adaptation when income is irregular. Or why possessing a qualification does not automatically reveal the settings in which that qualification could become useful.

An experience that felt private or confusing becomes easier to examine. The explanation gives us language.

That can matter even before it provides a complete solution. We can ask a clearer question, explain ourselves to another person or recognise what further information we need.

Being understood can help us understand ourselves.

## Empathy becomes visible in the response

This is where I connect precision with empathy.

To understand someone’s circumstances, I must make an effort to see the problem from where they stand. I need to investigate what makes sense within their situation, including choices that might look puzzling from outside it.

Why do they continue using an inconvenient service? What makes an apparently better option difficult to adopt? What would they risk by changing?

Those questions can reveal constraints that a general explanation misses.

But understanding becomes more meaningful when it changes our response.

If I know that readers have limited time, I should consider how much work I require them to do before reaching the useful part. If a process is unfamiliar, I should explain its steps and terms rather than assume confidence. If income is uncertain, I should examine how that uncertainty affects the proposed approach.

Empathy becomes visible through these decisions. The person encounters a resource and discovers that someone has anticipated a difficulty they would otherwise have had to manage alone.

## The tactical dimension

There is a tactical dimension to this understanding. When people feel accurately understood, they may become more willing to listen. An explanation that recognises their circumstances has a stronger claim on their attention than one that overlooks them.

I use this as a working observation about communication, rather than a claim that recognition always produces trust or agreement.

That creates an opportunity. It also creates responsibility.

Understanding a person’s frustrations can help us offer something useful. It can also help us exploit those frustrations. A publisher might recognise someone’s fear and provide a careful explanation. Another might intensify the fear to make a sale.

Both may know something about the audience. Their purposes and effects differ.

For the family of publications I want to build, recognition must be connected to service. The work should help people assess their choices, including the choice to disagree, wait or decline an offer.

Trust becomes fragile when people discover that the appearance of understanding was mainly a route to their money.

## Feeling known is not the same as being known

A person can feel understood by a statement broad enough to fit almost anyone.

A flattering description may create immediate recognition. So may a familiar complaint. Neither necessarily demonstrates a deep understanding of the individual reading it.

I must therefore be careful about what I regard as success.

If a reader says, “This sounds like me,” that is an encouraging beginning. The next question is whether the explanation remains accurate when the reader adds details.

What happens when their circumstances depart from our example? Can the framework accommodate that difference? Do we recognise where our understanding ends?

A useful publication should be able to say: this applies under these conditions; your situation may require a different answer.

Precision includes knowing the limits of the fit.

## People must remain free to surprise us

There is a danger in narrowing an audience too confidently. We may begin with an attempt to understand people and end with a rigid picture of who they are.

We decide what women over a certain age want. We assume what traders worry about. We imagine that people in a particular location share the same ambitions.

The category becomes a substitute for listening. That would undermine the recognition we hoped to create.

People need room to correct our description. They may share a difficulty while disagreeing about its importance. They may want an outcome we did not anticipate. Their priorities may change.

A precise publication needs an open ear.

The purpose of defining an audience is to direct attention towards people whose circumstances we want to investigate. Their actual experiences must remain capable of changing the definition.

## A family of work that pays attention

This is the deeper ambition behind my interest in specialised publications.

I can imagine a broad platform supporting several focused branches, each becoming attentive to a particular set of circumstances. The achievement would be more than having many names or websites.

Each branch would develop a memory of the questions people repeatedly ask, the advice that fails to fit, the obstacles that remain hidden and the explanations that help. That knowledge would need to stay alive through continued investigation.

A family of publications could share what it learns while allowing each branch to sustain its particular responsibility.

Readers would have a reason to return because the work consistently recognises something important about the decisions they face.

## The discipline of paying attention

For NaijaOS, this returns me to a larger question: how do we understand people well enough to create useful possibilities with them?

Part of the answer may be learning to notice the circumstances that broad descriptions leave out.

There is a person behind the market category. There are obligations behind the purchasing decision. There is a history behind the hesitation.

We cannot know all of it. We can become more careful about what we assume and more attentive to what people tell us.

That is why precision interests me beyond its commercial potential.

At its best, it communicates a simple message: your circumstances deserve to be considered.

A person’s name tells them whom we are addressing. The care we take with their situation tells them whether we have been paying attention.
