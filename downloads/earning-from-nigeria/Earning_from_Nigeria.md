# What Can Someone Else Pay You to Do?

Turning knowledge, judgment and practical skills into a useful service

By Paul A. Magaji

One question sits beneath my ambition to earn from Nigeria by creating value for the world:

What can someone else pay me to do?

It sounds straightforward. Yet it asks something more demanding than listing my qualifications, interests or abilities.

I may know a subject well. I may notice things others overlook. I may be able to research, explain, organise or build. A potential buyer still needs to understand how those capabilities would help with a particular difficulty.

That is where I want to begin: finding the connection between something I can do and something another person needs done.

I am exploring how to develop that connection through my websites. This is a working approach, rather than an account of international service income I have already established.

## Look for the task inside the subject

My interests include law, institutions, enterprise, technology and opportunity.

Each is broad enough to occupy a lifetime. None, by itself, tells a buyer what to purchase.

“I am interested in enterprise” leaves many questions unanswered. “I can research the organisations operating in a defined sector and prepare a source-linked briefing” describes something more concrete.

The second statement identifies a task. Someone can consider whether they need it, ask what it includes and assess whether I am capable of delivering it.

This distinction helps me think about my own work.

When I am drawn to a subject, I can ask: what are people trying to accomplish here? Which parts take time, create confusion or remain unfinished? Where could my contribution make a useful difference?

The service begins to take shape when the subject becomes a task.

## Recognise capabilities that have become ordinary to you

Some abilities become so familiar that we stop noticing the effort they save other people.

A person who writes clearly may underestimate how difficult another person finds preparing a coherent document. Someone who can organise scattered information may assume everyone can do it. A patient researcher may overlook the value of finding, comparing and explaining material that another person has neither the time nor the inclination to examine.

My legal background has trained my attention in particular ways.

I tend to ask what a claim rests on, what a document actually says and which important detail is missing. I look for responsibilities, competing interpretations and consequences.

Those habits may contribute to useful work in several settings. But I still need to identify where they are relevant and demonstrate that I can apply them to the task.

A capability becomes commercially meaningful when someone can see what it helps them accomplish.

## Define a buyer precisely enough to understand them

“People abroad” is too broad an audience for a first service.

People elsewhere have different needs, budgets and alternatives. An individual creator commissioning a short research brief is making a different decision from an organisation seeking continuing support.

I need to picture the person closely enough to understand the circumstances.

What are they responsible for? What has prompted the need? What have they already tried? What would they otherwise do if they did not engage me?

Consider a hypothetical example.

An editor planning a series about enterprise in Nigeria might need an initial briefing: relevant institutions, promising lines of inquiry, publicly available reports and questions requiring further investigation.

That is a more useful starting point than offering “Nigerian insights” to an undefined international market.

It also reveals the limits. A desk research brief would not establish what interviews, fieldwork or access to private records might reveal.

Precision helps both sides understand the possible engagement.

## Give the service a visible shape

Before someone purchases a service, much of its quality remains uncertain.

A clear description reduces some of that uncertainty.

For a research assignment, I might need to specify the question, the sources I will examine, the form of the briefing and the delivery date. I would also need to explain what information the buyer must provide and how questions or revisions will be handled.

The description should make the work imaginable.

“Research support” could mean almost anything. A briefing addressing one agreed question, with a summary, source links and unresolved issues, gives the buyer something they can assess.

The same principle applies to other work.

A workshop needs a learning purpose. A writing engagement needs an audience and a defined piece of writing. A website assignment needs an agreed set of pages or functions.

The clearer the promised work, the easier it becomes to discuss whether it meets the need.

## Promise something you can control

A service can contribute to an outcome without controlling every condition that determines it.

A research brief may help someone make a decision. The decision and its eventual consequences involve other factors. A website may give a business a clearer public presence. Sales also depend on the offer, audience, pricing and follow-through.

I need to understand that distinction before describing what I can deliver.

An ambitious promise can attract attention while creating expectations the actual work cannot satisfy.

My responsibility is to explain the contribution accurately. What will I produce? What standard will I work towards? What depends on information, participation or decisions outside my control?

These boundaries belong in the offer from the beginning.

They also help me recognise assignments I should decline, narrow or undertake with someone more experienced.

## Let a sample answer part of the trust question

A potential buyer should have some way to examine my work before committing.

That is one practical role for my websites.

A carefully sourced article can show how I handle evidence. A project account can show how I define a problem and respond to difficulties. A demonstration briefing can show how I organise information for a reader.

The sample should resemble the capability I am offering.

An essay may demonstrate clear thinking, but it does not establish every skill involved in delivering a workshop or maintaining a website. Each service needs relevant evidence.

If I create a sample from a fictional scenario or a self-directed exercise, I should describe it that way. It can still help someone assess my approach without being presented as a completed client engagement.

Credibility grows when the claim and the example fit each other.

## Count the work surrounding the work

The central task is only part of an engagement.

There may be an initial conversation, clarification of the brief, preparation, communication, revisions and administration. Some assignments require paid tools or assistance. Others create a continuing support burden.

I need to account for those demands when deciding whether an offer is sustainable.

An amount that appears attractive when converted into naira may carry a commitment much larger than I first imagined.

The question is whether the fee supports doing the whole engagement properly.

That requires a realistic understanding of my time, costs, capability and existing responsibilities. If the proposed service depends on availability I cannot sustain, its scope needs changing.

## Learn from a bounded first engagement

It is tempting to design a complete service business before testing one offer.

A smaller engagement can provide better information.

I could begin with a defined task, a clear endpoint and a buyer whose need I understand. Afterwards, I would want to examine the experience carefully.

Was the brief clear? Did the buyer find the result useful? Which parts took longer than expected? What caused revisions? Could I repeat the work at a dependable standard?

Those observations can improve the next offer.

They may also show that the service needs a different audience, a narrower scope or more preparation.

A purchase provides evidence that someone was willing to pay. Understanding what happened during delivery helps establish whether there is a service worth developing.

## Use my location with specificity

Working from Nigeria gives me proximity to particular experiences, institutions and questions.

That proximity may matter to some buyers. Its value depends on what I can observe, explain or investigate reliably.

Living in Nigeria does not make me knowledgeable about every Nigerian industry or community. I need to identify the subjects I understand, the sources I can access and the questions that require further work.

The useful offer describes that knowledge precisely.

My location can provide context. My work must show what I can do with it.

## Write the first offer

A practical starting point is a short description:

I help a particular person or organisation address a defined problem by producing a specified piece of work within an agreed scope and timeframe.

Every part of that sentence needs thought.

It asks me to move from a broad sense of potential to something another person can consider purchasing.

I do not need to fit everything I know into the first offer. I need to identify one useful contribution I can explain, demonstrate and deliver responsibly.

For me, that is where earning through services begins: someone has a task that matters to them, and I become capable of doing it well.

# Create Once, Sell Repeatedly: Turning Knowledge into Useful Products

By Paul A. Magaji

A service usually begins with someone asking me to do a piece of work.

A digital product asks a different question: can I prepare something useful that another person can purchase and use without commissioning the work from the beginning?

That possibility interests me.

A guide, worksheet, template, research publication or small software tool could carry part of my knowledge beyond the hours I am personally available. Someone could discover it through an article, examine what it offers and decide whether it would help them.

I am exploring this as a direction for my websites. I am not claiming that a catalogue of products has already produced an international income.

The attraction is understandable. But “create once, sell repeatedly” describes a possibility more neatly than it describes the work.

A product still needs a buyer, a useful purpose and a dependable way to deliver what was promised.

## Begin with a task someone needs to complete

A subject can be interesting without suggesting a product.

I might write about enterprise, institutions or building from Nigeria. To develop something purchasable, I need to look more closely at what a reader is trying to do.

Perhaps they need to organise information before a meeting. Perhaps they want to compare options, prepare a project brief or keep a record they currently struggle to maintain.

Those are tasks around which a product could take shape.

A worksheet might help someone organise the necessary information. A guide might explain the sequence. A small tool might perform a calculation or produce a structured output.

The starting question is practical: what would become easier for the person using this?

That gives the product a purpose against which it can be assessed.

## Distinguish information from assistance

There is a great deal of information available online.

A potential buyer may already have access to articles, videos and AI-generated explanations about the subject I want to address.

That makes it important to understand what my product adds.

It might organise scattered material into a clear sequence. It might help the user recognise a missing consideration. It might provide a structure that saves them from designing their own. It might explain a difficult task in language suited to their circumstances.

The contribution needs to be visible.

A collection of pages can contain accurate information while leaving the user uncertain about what to do next. A shorter resource can be more useful if it helps them complete the task.

I want to judge the product by the assistance it provides, including whether someone can understand and use it without me standing beside them.

## Choose the form that fits the work

A digital product does not have to be an ebook.

The right form depends on what the person needs to accomplish.

A checklist can help someone remember a sequence of checks. A worksheet can help them think through a decision. A spreadsheet can organise entries and calculations. A reference guide can help them find an explanation when they need it.

A small browser tool may be useful where repeated inputs need to produce a structured result.

For example, someone preparing to commission a website might benefit from a project brief that asks about audience, purpose, required pages, available materials and continuing maintenance.

That resource could be a simple document. Its quality would depend on the questions it asks and the clarity of the instructions.

Adding software would only help if it made the task easier.

I need to resist making the product more elaborate than its purpose requires.

## Make the intended user recognisable

A resource designed for everyone can struggle to address anyone closely.

Someone building a first personal website has different needs from an organisation replacing a large existing site. A beginner may need explanations that an experienced project manager would find unnecessary.

The product description should help a person recognise whether it suits them.

Who is it for? What should they already know? What will they need to provide? What can they expect to have completed after using it?

It should also identify circumstances in which a more individual assessment is needed.

My legal background makes me attentive to the danger of a reusable resource appearing more universally applicable than it is. A document can be carefully prepared and still be unsuitable for a particular situation.

Clarity about intended use is part of making a useful product.

## Let a sample show how it works

A buyer should have enough information to make a considered decision.

For a worksheet, I could show a page and explain the prompts. For a guide, I could provide the contents and a representative extract. For a tool, a demonstration could show the inputs and resulting output.

A completed fictional example may be especially helpful.

It can reveal how much effort the resource requires, what kind of thinking it supports and what the finished result looks like.

The example should be labelled accurately. It should not imply a customer outcome that has never been observed.

A useful preview also helps me examine my own product. If I struggle to explain what someone receives, the product may still need a clearer purpose.

## Test whether someone can use it independently

Creating the resource is only part of the task.

I need to observe whether someone can use it.

A prompt that seems obvious to me may confuse another person. An instruction may assume knowledge the intended buyer does not possess. A spreadsheet may be easy to damage through an ordinary entry.

Where possible, I would want a person from the intended audience to attempt the task with the resource.

What do they misunderstand? Where do they stop? Which explanation do they look for? Does the result help them proceed?

Their difficulty is useful evidence.

It may show that the instructions need revision, that the product attempts too much or that I have misunderstood the problem.

Testing the resource asks whether my knowledge has become usable in someone else’s hands.

## Understand what “sell repeatedly” requires

A downloadable file can be delivered to more than one buyer without recreating it each time.

That does not make every later sale effortless.

Someone still needs to discover the product and understand its relevance. Payment and delivery need to work. Questions may require responses. Errors may need correction.

Some products also depend on information that changes.

A resource about a changing process may need regular review. A software tool may require maintenance as its dependencies or operating environment change.

I need to account for those obligations before deciding what the product costs to provide.

The first version is an asset only to the extent that it remains useful and dependable.

## Give ownership and permission their proper place

A product may contain original writing alongside material obtained elsewhere.

Before offering it, I need to understand what I have created, what I have permission to include and how any external material should be acknowledged.

The buyer also needs an understandable account of permitted use.

Can they use it within their own organisation? Can they adapt it? Does wider distribution require a separate arrangement?

The appropriate terms depend on the product and the circumstances. The practical aim is to avoid leaving either side to guess.

This is one area where my legal training can contribute to the surrounding structure. It does not replace the need to make the product useful in the first place.

## Let free work and paid work support each other

An article can give a reader a complete and useful explanation.

Some readers may then want a resource that helps them apply it.

An essay about defining a first service could connect to a worksheet for describing the buyer, problem, deliverable and scope. An article about managing a project could connect to a record for tracking decisions and unresolved questions.

The relationship should be clear.

The article helps the reader understand. The product offers a particular form of practical assistance.

I want a reader who purchases nothing to have gained something worthwhile. That public usefulness is also part of the basis on which someone might trust a later offer.

## Begin with one product and learn what happens

It is possible to imagine a large catalogue before learning whether anyone wants the first item.

A single, bounded resource would give me more concrete questions to investigate.

Did people understand the description? Did the sample address their uncertainty? Could buyers access the file and use it on their devices? Did the product help with the task?

I would also want to understand the support burden and what needed updating.

Those observations could guide a better version. They might reveal a related product, or show that the problem is better addressed through a service or workshop.

The purpose of the first product is to serve a real need while giving me evidence about what I should develop next.

## Give knowledge a form that can travel

What attracts me to digital products is the possibility of making useful work available beyond a single conversation or engagement.

A person elsewhere could use a resource I prepared in Nigeria at a time when I am occupied with something else. They would not need to commission the whole process from the beginning.

For that to happen well, the product must carry enough clarity with it: a purpose, instructions, appropriate limits and a result worth the user’s effort.

The practical next step is to identify one recurring task I understand and prepare the smallest resource that could help someone complete it.

Then I need to see whether it works for them.

That is how I want to turn knowledge into something people can use—and, where the value justifies it, purchase.

# What You Know Can Help Someone Else Learn

By Paul A. Magaji

There are things I understand today because someone took the trouble to explain them.

Sometimes the explanation came through a teacher. Sometimes it came through a book, a conversation or a practical demonstration. In each case, knowledge became more useful because someone found a way to make it accessible.

That creates another possibility for earning through useful work.

Someone may be willing to pay me to help them understand a subject, develop a skill or complete a task they cannot yet manage independently.

My experience as an English teacher gives this possibility a personal connection. My legal training and my attempts to build websites give me further subjects to examine. But knowing something and teaching it effectively are separate accomplishments.

If I want to earn through teaching, I need to become attentive to what happens in the learner.

I am exploring this as a direction for my work. It is not a claim that I have already established a successful international teaching business.

## Begin with something the learner wants to do

A subject can be large enough to make a course sound impressive while leaving its purpose unclear.

“Understanding technology” covers too much for a first workshop. Helping someone prepare a clear brief for a first website gives the learning a more manageable destination.

The distinction matters because the learner brings a purpose of their own.

They may want to perform a task, understand a responsibility or make a decision with greater confidence. They may need enough knowledge to work more effectively with someone they have engaged.

I need to identify that purpose before deciding what to teach.

What should the participant be able to explain, assess or do afterwards? What difficulty is preventing them from doing it now?

Those questions give the teaching a direction.

## Choose a starting point you can serve responsibly

My interests extend beyond the subjects in which I have the strongest experience.

That is useful for learning. It requires judgment when deciding what to teach.

I can discuss an investigation while still making clear that it is unfinished. I can document an attempt without presenting myself as an expert in every tool involved. A structured course, however, asks learners to rely on my selection and explanation of the material.

I need a sound basis for that responsibility.

An appropriate starting point may be a task I have performed repeatedly, a subject I have studied carefully or a process I can demonstrate and explain accurately.

Where my knowledge is still developing, the offering needs to reflect that. A discussion group examining a question makes a different promise from a workshop teaching an established procedure.

The description should let participants understand what kind of learning they are joining.

## Understand what the learner already knows

An explanation can be accurate and still miss its audience.

A beginner may need ordinary terms defined before the main idea becomes accessible. Someone with experience may need help with a particular difficulty rather than an introduction to the entire subject.

People also arrive with different assumptions.

A participant learning to commission a website might believe that paying for the initial build includes every future change. Another might not yet distinguish a domain name from hosting. A third may understand those matters but struggle to describe the site’s purpose.

Those starting points affect the lesson.

Before preparing the material, I would want to understand the intended audience well enough to recognise what requires explanation and what can reasonably be assumed.

Otherwise, I risk teaching the lesson I want to give rather than the lesson they need.

## Organise the learning into a workable sequence

When I understand a subject, its parts may appear connected in ways a newcomer cannot yet see.

Teaching requires making those connections available gradually.

For a workshop on preparing a website brief, I might begin with the intended audience and the task the site should support. Pages, content and functions would follow from those decisions. Costs and maintenance would then have a clearer context.

Starting with a list of technical features could leave participants selecting things before understanding why they need them.

Sequence is part of the service.

Each stage should give the learner enough understanding to approach the next one. The material needs room for explanation, examples and questions.

A short workshop can only carry so much. Choosing what to leave for later may be necessary to help participants learn the essentials properly.

## Give people an opportunity to attempt the work

Listening to an explanation can produce a feeling of understanding.

Attempting the task reveals more.

A participant might agree that a project needs a clear audience and still struggle to describe their own. Someone may understand an example while being unsure how to apply the method to different circumstances.

That is where an exercise becomes useful.

I could ask learners to prepare a short brief, examine a fictional scenario or explain a distinction in their own words. Their attempts would show where the explanation had become usable and where further help was needed.

The exercise should fit the purpose.

If the promised outcome is the ability to assess a document, recalling definitions alone would provide limited evidence. Participants need an opportunity to examine something and explain their judgment.

Teaching becomes more valuable when it helps people move from recognition to application.

## Make feedback specific enough to help

A learner needs to know more than whether an answer is right or wrong.

They may need to understand which part of their reasoning is sound, what they have overlooked and how to improve the next attempt.

That requires attention.

If a participant produces an unclear project brief, I could identify the point at which the intended audience becomes vague or where two different tasks have been combined. A focused comment gives them something they can revise.

This also affects the size and format of the offering.

A recorded course can provide examples and self-check exercises. A small live workshop can allow more individual discussion. A programme promising detailed feedback requires time to examine participants’ work.

The offer needs to explain what kind of support is included and whether I can provide it dependably.

## Let the format follow the learning need

Teaching can take several forms.

A short live session may help people work through one question. A workshop can combine explanation with practice. A recorded lesson can allow someone to pause and revisit the material. A longer programme may support skills that require repeated attempts and feedback.

Each form has its own demands.

A live session depends on scheduling and participation. Recorded material needs instructions that remain clear without immediate clarification. Continuing programmes require a level of availability I must be able to sustain.

The learner’s circumstances matter too.

What device will they use? How much uninterrupted time do they have? Can the materials be downloaded? Is the exercise practical within the resources available to them?

Working from Nigeria makes me conscious of those questions, although I should investigate the actual audience rather than assume everyone shares my circumstances.

## Explain what the fee covers

A teaching offer should describe the experience clearly enough for someone to assess it.

The participant needs to know the subject, intended level, format, duration and expected preparation. They should understand whether materials, exercises, feedback or continuing access are included.

The outcome also needs careful wording.

I can undertake to teach a method, provide practice and respond within an agreed scope. A participant’s later success will depend on factors that extend beyond the session.

My legal background makes me attentive to the relationship between a promise and what can actually be delivered. That attention can help me describe the engagement responsibly.

The quality of the teaching still has to justify the description.

## Test a small lesson before building a large course

Preparing an extensive course can feel productive before I know how learners respond.

A smaller session could provide better information.

I could select one audience, one task and a manageable learning outcome. Afterwards, I would examine what participants were able to do, where they needed help and whether the session addressed a difficulty they considered important.

Their comments would matter. So would their work.

Did they understand the instructions? Could they complete the exercise? Which questions kept returning? Did the material assume too much or spend too long on something they already knew?

Those observations could guide the next version.

They might support a fuller programme. They might also show that a short workshop is sufficient, or that the problem needs a resource people can consult individually.

## Allow teaching to deepen the wider work

Teaching could connect naturally with my writing and other projects.

An article may introduce a question. A worksheet may help a reader organise an attempt. A workshop may provide the explanation and feedback needed to use the method more confidently.

The questions participants ask can then improve future articles and resources.

That relationship interests me because it keeps the work in contact with people trying to use it.

It also exposes gaps in my own understanding. A question I struggle to answer may reveal an assumption I have never examined or a subject requiring further study.

Teaching can become part of how I develop my knowledge, provided I remain honest about what I know and willing to correct the material.

## Make the learning worth carrying away

The possibility of earning through teaching begins with knowledge, but it asks for more.

I need to understand the learner, define a useful purpose, organise the material and create an opportunity to practise. I need some way to assess whether the explanation has helped.

A participant should leave with something they can carry into their own work: a clearer distinction, a better question, a completed attempt or a skill they can continue developing.

That is the contribution I would want someone to pay for.

My next step is to choose one task I can teach responsibly and prepare a small lesson around it. Then I need to observe whether another person becomes more capable through the experience.

What I know becomes a teaching opportunity when I can help someone else make use of it.

# What Would Make Someone Pay You Again Next Month?

By Paul A. Magaji

A person can purchase a guide, use it and feel satisfied that the transaction is complete.

A subscription creates a different relationship.

The buyer agrees to pay again because they expect something useful to continue. That expectation interests me as I think about earning through my websites.

I could imagine a continuing research publication, access to a maintained collection of resources or a membership built around a shared area of work.

But imagining recurring income is easier than identifying a recurring need.

What would make someone decide, month after month, that the work remains worth paying for?

That is the question I need to investigate. I am exploring a possible direction, rather than describing a subscription business I have already established.

## Find a need that continues

Some problems have a clear endpoint.

A person needs a document prepared, a question investigated or an explanation of a particular subject. Once that work is complete, their immediate need may be satisfied.

Other needs return.

Someone responsible for decisions in a changing field may need regular interpretation of developments. A person using a collection of working resources may need revisions as their circumstances change. Members of a professional group may benefit from continuing discussion of problems they encounter.

These situations suggest different possibilities for a subscription.

The important point is to understand why the need continues.

If I cannot explain what the buyer will need after the first month, I may have identified a useful product without identifying a sustainable subscription.

## Specify whose continuing problem I am addressing

“People interested in business” is a broad readership.

A subscription would need a more precise reason for someone to join.

An organisation considering opportunities in a particular sector might need selected developments explained in relation to its work. Someone managing a small institution might need resources and discussions relevant to recurring operational questions.

Their needs would differ in frequency, depth and urgency.

I need to understand what the intended subscriber is responsible for and what happens when they lack the information or assistance I propose to provide.

Do they lose time searching? Do they struggle to distinguish consequential developments from routine announcements? Do they repeatedly face a task for which a maintained resource could help?

The closer I get to the actual difficulty, the clearer the possible offer becomes.

## Explain what continues after payment

A subscription needs a recognisable promise.

A reader should be able to understand what arrives, what becomes available and how often they can expect it.

For a research publication, that might mean a briefing on an agreed schedule, with source links and an explanation of why the selected developments matter.

For a resource collection, the promise might concern access to specified materials, corrections and meaningful updates.

For a learning membership, it might involve a scheduled discussion, exercises or opportunities to submit questions within a defined scope.

These are different commitments.

I should avoid assembling them into a large offer simply because they sound attractive together. Each addition creates work, and some additions create expectations of personal availability.

The description needs to match what I can sustain.

## Understand why someone would return

A subscriber can receive something regularly without finding it regularly useful.

Frequency alone does not establish value.

A monthly publication might contain many pages while leaving the reader uncertain about what deserves attention. A shorter briefing might be more useful if it helps them recognise an important change, understand its implications and identify a question they need to investigate.

That is where judgment can matter.

My legal training encourages me to examine sources, distinguish claims from established facts and ask what a development changes in practice. Those habits could contribute to a continuing publication.

They would still need to serve a defined audience.

The subscriber is paying for work that helps with their responsibilities. I need to understand which decisions or tasks the publication supports, rather than assume that careful research will automatically justify a continuing fee.

## Choose the right kind of continuing relationship

A publication, a membership and an advisory engagement can all involve repeated payments, but they ask different things of the provider.

A publication primarily promises material prepared for its readers.

A membership may also promise participation: discussions, shared activities or access to a community.

An advisory engagement may involve attention to the circumstances of an individual client.

The boundaries matter.

If I offer a paid publication, a subscriber should not have to guess whether their fee includes individual advice. If I establish a discussion group, participants need to know whether I will facilitate every conversation or respond to every question.

I need to decide which relationship I am actually offering.

A modest, clear commitment may serve people better than an expansive promise whose practical demands remain unexplored.

## Treat the subscriber’s attention as part of the cost

The fee is only one thing a subscriber gives.

They also give time and attention.

A continuing publication can become another item they feel obliged to read. A membership can become another group they struggle to follow. A large archive can become difficult to navigate.

That makes selection and organisation part of the work.

Can the subscriber find what they need? Can they understand the main point quickly? Is it clear which material requires close reading and which can wait?

I would want the experience to help someone manage their attention.

Sending more material whenever I worry about demonstrating value could undermine that purpose. The useful amount depends on the need and the reader’s circumstances.

## Establish a schedule I can honour

A subscription turns a publishing intention into an obligation.

I need to account for research, preparation, editing, delivery, corrections and administration. If the offering includes discussion or feedback, that time belongs in the calculation too.

My other professional responsibilities remain relevant.

A weekly commitment may sound appealing while leaving too little room for careful work. A monthly publication may allow greater depth but fail to meet an audience’s need for timely updates.

The appropriate schedule requires understanding both sides.

What frequency serves the subscriber? What can I provide dependably with the resources available?

I should establish the working process before promising a rhythm that depends on exceptional effort every time.

## Make the commercial terms understandable

Someone considering a subscription needs to know what they are agreeing to.

The price, payment interval and included benefits should be clear. They should also be able to understand how renewal works, how to end the subscription and what happens to access afterwards.

An archive raises further questions.

Does the subscription include earlier editions? Can subscribers keep downloaded materials? Which resources remain available when their subscription ends?

The appropriate arrangements will depend on the offering. My responsibility is to work them out and explain them accurately.

A continuing relationship should not depend on uncertainty about the transaction.

## Test the need before building the system

It is possible to spend considerable effort creating a membership platform before learning whether people want the underlying work.

I would prefer to test a bounded version first.

For example, a short series of briefings could help me understand whether the audience recognises the problem, uses the material and wants it to continue.

The trial would need its own clear description. Participants should know its duration, what it includes and whether any further payment requires a new decision.

Afterwards, I would want to examine more than expressions of enthusiasm.

Did people read and use the work? What did it help them understand or accomplish? Which parts did they ignore? Would they pay for another defined period?

Those responses could guide the next version—or show that another format would be more appropriate.

## Learn from renewal and departure

An initial purchase tells me that the offer persuaded someone to try.

A renewal can provide evidence that the experience remained worthwhile, although it does not explain the reason by itself.

I would want to understand why people stay.

Perhaps the publication saves them research time. Perhaps its interpretation helps them prepare for meetings. Perhaps they value a resource they consult occasionally when a difficult task arises.

Departures can teach me something too.

The subscriber’s circumstances may have changed. The material may have become less relevant. The frequency may be inconvenient, or the promise may not have matched the experience.

I need room to hear those explanations without treating every cancellation as a request to publish more.

The purpose is to understand the relationship between the offer and the need.

## Keep the work useful between payments

Recurring income would be valuable to the institution I am trying to build. It could help support continuing research, maintenance and publication.

The subscriber, however, needs a reason to support it through a purchase.

That reason must be present in the work.

## Develop a perspective with substance behind it

Having an opinion gives me something to say. A paid engagement requires me to examine why the contribution deserves the audience’s attention.

What have I studied closely? Which experiences can I describe accurately? What evidence supports my argument? Where does my knowledge end?

My background gives me several possible starting points.

Law has trained me to examine claims, responsibilities and consequences. Teaching has required me to explain ideas. Building websites has introduced practical questions about turning an intention into something people can use.

The connection between those experiences interests me.

For example, I could investigate how a professional working from Nigeria uses digital tools to develop public work while retaining responsibility for its quality.

That could become a useful talk if I support it with actual examples, clear distinctions and lessons an audience can assess.

The perspective needs to be developed through work.

## Give each talk a clear promise

A broad topic can make it difficult for an organiser to understand what a session will contain.

“Technology and opportunity” could cover almost anything. A talk examining what it takes to turn one professional capability into a clearly defined digital offer gives the organiser a more concrete proposition.

A useful description should identify the central question, the intended audience and what the session will help them understand.

The title can attract interest. The description needs to explain the contribution.

I would also want to be clear about the format. A short keynote, a moderated conversation and a practical workshop involve different kinds of preparation and participation.

An organiser should be able to judge whether the proposed session fits the occasion.

## Let the website provide evidence

My website can help someone assess a possible invitation before contacting me.

An article shows how I develop an argument. A project account shows what I have attempted and what I learned. A recording can show how I explain a subject aloud.

These forms of evidence answer different questions.

Strong writing does not establish every aspect of effective speaking. A short recorded explanation could give an organiser a better sense of my pace, clarity and delivery.

If the recording is prepared independently, I should present it as a demonstration. It need not be described as a previous paid engagement to be useful.

Over time, a speaking page could collect a few well-defined topics, relevant publications and representative recordings.

Its purpose would be to make my contribution easier to assess.

## Adapt the material to the actual audience

A talk prepared for young people building their first projects would need different explanations from one prepared for experienced organisational leaders.

The central idea might remain relevant, but the examples, assumptions and questions would change.

I should therefore learn enough about the audience to prepare responsibly.

An organiser’s brief could help me understand participants’ roles, the event’s wider programme and any particular sensitivities around the subject.

That preparation also helps me avoid a familiar mistake: delivering everything I know because I have been given time to speak.

A session has limits. Selecting the material that serves the audience is part of the work.

## Build an argument people can follow

A speaking engagement needs more than a collection of interesting observations.

The audience should be able to follow how the ideas connect.

I might begin with a recognisable problem, examine why it is difficult and develop a practical way to think about it. An example could then show what the argument means in use.

The structure should support the purpose of the session.

If I want people to understand a distinction, I need examples that make the difference visible. If I want them to assess a proposal, I need to explain its assumptions and limitations.

My legal training can contribute to that discipline. My teaching experience reminds me that an argument must also be accessible to the person encountering it for the first time.

## Prepare for questions and disagreement

An audience may ask something that exposes a gap in my explanation.

Someone may have experience that complicates my example. Another participant may challenge the conclusion or ask how it applies to circumstances I have not investigated.

Those exchanges can improve the discussion if I handle them carefully.

I need to distinguish what I can answer from what requires further work. Where a question changes the assumptions, I should acknowledge that change.

A speaking invitation does not require me to appear certain about everything.

It requires preparation, attention and an honest account of what I can contribute.

That is especially relevant when discussing Nigeria with audiences whose experiences differ from mine. I can explain my circumstances and observations without presenting them as a complete account of the country.

## Count the full commitment

The time spent speaking is only part of an engagement.

There may be research, planning conversations, preparation of materials, rehearsal, travel and follow-up. A remote event may require a technical check and arrangements for reliable participation.

Those demands affect whether I can accept the invitation and what fee would support the work.

I would want to understand the complete commitment before agreeing.

A short session may require substantial preparation if the audience or subject is unfamiliar. A request for a tailored briefing may involve more research than a talk based on work I have already developed.

The fee needs to be considered alongside that scope.

## Agree on recording and reuse

An event can continue beyond the live session.

The organiser may want to record it, distribute the slides or publish extracts. I may also want permission to use a short recording as an example of my work.

Those expectations should be discussed early.

The arrangement needs to identify what may be recorded, how the material may be used and whether further distribution requires another agreement.

I would also need to examine what I include in the presentation. Material suitable for a limited discussion may require different permissions or treatment before wider publication.

My legal background makes these questions familiar. Their practical purpose is to help both sides understand what happens to the work after the event.

## Make remote participation dependable

Speaking from Nigeria to an audience elsewhere may be possible without travelling.

That can reduce some demands while introducing others.

The session needs workable timing, clear audio, a suitable environment and a way to manage technical difficulties. Slides and demonstrations should be tested in the actual format where possible.

I should also understand how the event will be moderated.

Who introduces the session? How are audience questions handled? What happens if the connection fails? Is there an alternative way to deliver the essential material?

These preparations support the contribution I have been invited to make.

A thoughtful argument is more useful when people can hear it and follow it comfortably.

## Learn from a small first offer

I do not need a large catalogue of talks before testing one.

A manageable starting point would be a topic grounded in my existing work, a clear audience and a short session with an identifiable purpose.

I could prepare a demonstration, seek informed feedback and revise the material.

After an actual engagement, I would want to understand what participants found useful, where they became confused and which questions remained.

An invitation establishes that an organiser saw potential relevance. The experience helps me assess whether I delivered it.

Those observations can improve both the talk and the writing from which it grew.

## Give the audience something worth carrying away

Speaking interests me because it allows ideas to enter a live exchange.

A person can ask a question, challenge an assumption or connect the argument to their own work. That interaction can make the subject clearer for the audience and for me.

For it to become a dependable income opportunity, I need a contribution that serves the occasion and preparation that supports its delivery.

My next step is to choose one question my work equips me to address, define the audience it could help and prepare a talk someone can assess.

The invitation may begin with something I publish online. Its value will depend on what people gain when I speak.
