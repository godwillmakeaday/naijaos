The Price of Precision

Understanding particular needs in an age of abundance

By Paul A. Magaji · 1 November 2026 · 8 min read

Perspective essay · A working argument and building ambition

Download reading edition (Markdown)

My answer is precision.

I do not mean that everything people need is readily available. In Nigeria, a person can have access to thousands of business articles while struggling to obtain reliable electricity, working capital or a trustworthy service provider. Information abundance can coexist with material scarcity.

But in the world of information, ideas and digital production, abundance is increasingly familiar. There is another article to read, another video to watch and another tool promising to help.

A person can move through all this and still struggle to find something that adequately addresses his circumstances.

That is where I believe a substantial opportunity lies: understanding a particular need deeply enough to respond with precision. I offer this as a working argument about value, rather than a measured ranking of the world's most expensive things.

The distance between an answer and my answer

Imagine a trader whose income arrives unpredictably. Some days are good. Others produce little. Household expenses continue regardless.

Now imagine a salaried worker who receives a predictable monthly income but supports several relatives. Then consider someone approaching retirement, uncertain about the transition into a different financial life.

All three may search for information about managing money. They could encounter the same article.

Yet the trader needs an approach that accommodates uneven income. The salaried worker must consider obligations extending beyond an individual budget. The person approaching retirement faces a change that may be difficult to reverse.

General principles can help. Their usefulness grows when an explanation recognises the conditions under which they must operate.

Precision closes the distance between an answer that exists and an answer someone can use.

The long tail of human needs

Large categories are convenient. We speak about consumers, entrepreneurs, workers, parents and citizens. These descriptions begin a conversation, but can conceal the differences that determine whether a service succeeds.

An entrepreneur might be a woman running a food business from home, a developer selling software internationally, or a furniture maker managing apprentices and delayed customer payments.

Their needs overlap in places. Elsewhere, they diverge sharply. Even the same person needs different things at different moments. Exploring a business idea raises different questions from trying to recover payment from a first major customer.

I think of this as the long tail of needs: the many particular situations that remain after we have named the broad category.

Each may appear small from a distance. To the person living through it, it may be the most important problem in the world.

Why I am thinking about a family

This thinking influences how I approach my websites.

I can imagine The Financial Citizen developing a family of specialised publications, desks or miniature brands. One might explore irregular income. Another might focus on family wealth records. Others might examine retirement transitions or the everyday financial administration of small enterprises.

These are possibilities to investigate. They are not a claim that those specialised services already exist or have demonstrated demand.

Over time, a parent platform might support fifty or sixty branches. The number itself would prove little. The achievement would be distinct areas in which readers find their circumstances understood with unusual care.

Creating another website is a smaller task than developing another field of understanding.

A name, domain and collection of articles can create the appearance of specialisation. Depth requires sustained listening, research and correction. I would therefore begin with focused sections and let evidence of usefulness justify independent brands.

A family can grow from understanding. It can also become an expensive collection of promises if it grows faster than our ability to keep them.

Shared foundations, particular responsibilities

Every branch would not need to begin alone. Publications could share technology, administration, research resources and editorial standards while retaining a clear responsibility to their own readers.

Work on family wealth records, for example, might reveal recurring difficulties with identifying assets, locating documents or knowing who holds important information. Those discoveries could inform work on enterprise continuity and household administration.

Shared learning would make the family stronger. But each branch would still need someone responsible for accuracy, updates and responding when the work falls short.

Some branches may attract strong interest. Some may remain modest. Others may need to be combined or closed. A struggling branch should prompt investigation: did we misunderstand the need, provide an inadequate response, or fail to reach the people concerned?

Failure becomes more useful when its lessons travel through the family.

Precision must earn its premium

Specificity alone does not create value. It is possible to describe an extremely narrow audience and offer it very little.

Precision earns a premium when it improves something that matters. Does it help someone avoid a costly mistake, save time, understand a difficult process or complete a task previously beyond reach?

There is also a difference between usefulness and commercial viability. People may value a resource without being able to pay for it. A need may occur only once. Serving a particular audience may cost more than its income can sustain.

A specialised operation therefore needs a real need, a useful response, a way to reach people and a workable means of supporting the work.

My ambition must be tested against those conditions. Fifty weak businesses do not become strong merely because they belong to one family.

Understanding requires humility

Precision can become overconfidence. Once we describe a group, we may imagine everyone within it behaves alike.

Two traders in the same market may have different obligations, ambitions and experience. A resource designed for one may require adjustment for the other.

A precise approach needs room for questions. What have we assumed? Which circumstances would change this answer? Who is missing from our understanding? What are people telling us that our existing framework cannot explain?

It also requires restraint. Understanding a need does not entitle a publication to collect every personal detail about the reader. Often, a clear question about circumstances is more appropriate than an intrusive profile.

The work becomes more precise as it becomes more willing to be corrected.

What this means for Nigeria

For NaijaOS, this connects to a larger inquiry: how can Nigeria understand, create and govern new possibilities?

The level at which we describe a problem shapes what we can see. The Nigerian market is a starting point. It is rarely a sufficient understanding of the people within it.

Where someone lives, how income arrives, which institutions are accessible and what obligations shape daily decisions can change what makes a service useful.

There may be opportunities inside needs we have treated as too ordinary, local or small to deserve sustained investigation. Paying attention could lead to better publications, teaching, tools, professional services and institutions.

Serving a particular group well can also produce knowledge useful elsewhere. A solution developed for one setting may reveal a principle that travels, provided we test what changes in the next setting.

The cost of becoming useful

Precision takes time. Listening, checking an attractive explanation and staying with a problem all demand attention after the excitement of announcing a project has passed.

The visible result may be a short article or a simple worksheet. Behind it may sit considerable work deciding what belongs, what can be omitted and where the answer must acknowledge its limits.

This is why I believe precision may command a premium in an age of abundant content. More material does not automatically bring us closer to the person who needs it.

My ambition is to build a family of work in which each branch recognises real circumstances, earns trust and helps someone act.

The question each branch must answer is simple:

Whose circumstances do we understand well enough to make a meaningful difference?

Before creating another branch

Name the people and circumstances you want to serve. Identify the decision they struggle with, the evidence you have of that need, and the smallest useful response you can test.

Decide who will maintain the work, how it can sustain itself, and what evidence would justify expanding, combining or closing it.
