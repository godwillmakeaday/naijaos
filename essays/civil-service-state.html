What Does Civil Service State Explain

Enugu and the limits of regional economic labels.

By Paul A. Magaji · 6 min read · Who gets room to attempt

Personal essay

Download Word document

During a visit connected with legal work in Abia, my client described Enugu as a civil service state.

The description surprised me. I had encountered businesspeople from Enugu elsewhere, including people whose commercial energy had made a strong impression on me. How could the place they came from be described in terms that seemed, to my ears, to suggest limited enterprise?

On another journey from Abia, someone driving us spoke about Enugu's commercial future. He thought it could become like Onitsha within ten years. My client, who was present, acknowledged the activity but emphasised people who had gone abroad and returned to invest.

I heard several accounts that seemed to compete. One concerned public employment. Another concerned businesspeople working elsewhere. A third concerned money coming back.

Before deciding which account was correct, I needed to ask whether they were describing the same thing.

***

“Civil service state” sounds like an explanation.

Yet I had not established what my client meant by it. Was she describing the work most visible in the city? The people whose salaries supported local spending? The availability of private employment? Or a comparison with the commercial activity she associated with other places?

Those meanings could lead to different investigations.

There was also a question of scale. Were we discussing Enugu city or the whole state? A conversation can move between the two without announcing the change. A description of the people one person knows can become a description of a population.

I had to acknowledge my own contribution to the confusion. I had heard a label and supplied some of its meaning myself.

Her statement was an account worth examining. It was not, by itself, a measured description of the economy.

***

My surprise also revealed the limits of what I already believed.

I had associated Igbo communities with business so strongly that a description emphasising public employment seemed contradictory. But that expectation had compressed many places and livelihoods into one picture.

It could not adequately explain differences within the communities it purported to describe.

Nor could commercial activity establish anyone's ethnic authenticity. In conversations about regional economies, I have encountered the troubling idea that people are more or less “pure” members of an ethnic group according to the work they do. I see no sound basis for that test. A person's belonging cannot be measured by how closely his livelihood fits somebody else's stereotype.

The question I wanted to investigate was economic: what work exists in a particular place, who can enter it, and what allows it to continue?

An ethnic description could not answer that for me.

***

The businesspeople I knew supplied another source of possible confusion.

Their activity elsewhere might tell me something about the individuals and networks I encountered. It did not automatically establish the employment opportunities available where they came from.

Someone's place of origin, place of work, place of residence, and place of investment need not be identical. If I wanted to understand the relationship between them, I would have to follow it.

Where was the business earning? Where were its workers? What was being purchased locally? What money returned, and what happened to it afterwards?

These questions could help reconcile accounts that initially sounded inconsistent.

A person might be right to describe a local dependence on salaries while another correctly notices commercially active people from that place living elsewhere. To know whether that is what was happening in this case, I would need evidence beyond my conversations.

The possibility was enough to make me reconsider the apparent contradiction.

***

The explanation about investment from abroad deserved the same care.

A returning investor's activity could take many forms. I could not infer its local effects simply from a visible building or a statement that money had come home.

I would want to know what the investment did. Did it create a business with customers? What jobs did it sustain? Which supplies and services did it purchase nearby? Who gained access to the opportunities it produced?

These are questions, not findings about Enugu. They identify the work required before I could turn an impression into an explanation.

They would also help me think more carefully about prosperity. The presence of affluent people is one thing. The existence of a dependable route through which someone else can acquire skills, find work, or build a business is another subject of inquiry.

I was interested in both, but I needed to stop treating one as sufficient evidence of the other.

***

The prediction about becoming like Onitsha introduced a further difficulty.

What did “like Onitsha” mean in that conversation? More trade? A larger market? A particular kind of urban growth? More opportunities for suppliers and traders?

And what would have to happen for the prediction to be fulfilled?

The ten-year horizon made the statement memorable. It did not supply an explanation of the process. I would need to understand the activities expected to grow, why they would grow there, and what could prevent that growth.

A useful prediction should eventually expose itself to observation. Otherwise, the comparison risks remaining an expression of confidence that cannot be properly assessed.

I did not have grounds to endorse or dismiss the driver's forecast. I had grounds to ask him to explain what he was seeing.

***

I could also hear an older concern beneath my interest in these labels.

As a boy, I had thought seriously about the security a government salary might provide. Public employment therefore carried a meaning for me that exceeded a category of work. It was connected to providing for children and meeting responsibilities.

When someone called a place a civil service state, I was hearing through that history.

I needed to separate my feelings about dependence and security from the investigation of a particular economy. Otherwise, I could make the description carry a judgment before finding out what it described.

Public service is work. Trade is work. Production is work. The questions concern what people do, how that work is supported, and what opportunities are available to those seeking to participate.

A label should help me ask those questions more precisely.

***

This gives NaijaOS a practical task.

When a familiar description appears to explain a place, I want to slow it down. Who uses it? What do they mean? Which area and period are they describing? What evidence would support it, and what might it leave out?

For this particular inquiry, I would want to examine the employment mix, sources of household income, kinds of private enterprise, and relationships with activity outside the locality. I would also want accounts from people whose work differs from that of the first person I happened to meet.

Their experiences would not replace broader evidence. They could reveal distinctions that a broad category might obscure.

A useful account should also consider entry. What could a young person without substantial savings actually attempt? Where would the necessary training come from? How would that person reach a customer or employer?

Those details matter to the original question that brought me here: how can someone build a livelihood?

***

I began this story with surprise at my client's description of Enugu. I have not ended it with a verdict on the state.

What changed was my understanding of the investigation.

I could no longer assume that businesspeople from a place explained its local economy. I could no longer hear a comparison between cities without asking which activities were being compared. And I could no longer regard my own inherited associations as an adequate standard against which to judge someone else's account.

The phrase “civil service state” might prove useful if we specify its meaning and test it.

Until then, I want to treat it as the beginning of a question.

What exactly does it explain?
