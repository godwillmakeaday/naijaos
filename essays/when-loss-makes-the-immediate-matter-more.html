When Loss Makes the Immediate Matter More

Grief, present responsibilities and the future we hope to build.

By Paul A. Magaji · 7 min read · Responsibility and informed action

Personal essay

Download Word document

Somewhere in the middle of these struggles, I lost my mother.

I had been thinking about how to become productive and independent. I was trying to understand the country in which I wanted to build: how people earned, how they found opportunities, and what might make a livelihood secure.

Then her death entered the account.

When I began telling this story, I found myself saying that the loss sometimes made the negative view seem true. The practical, immediate argument acquired a force that was difficult to dismiss.

I want to stay with that thought carefully. I do not want to give it an explanation more complete than the experience I have actually described.

For much of my life, security had meant the ability to meet responsibilities.

As a boy, I worried about how I would provide for children without a dependable salary. Later, I began to imagine earning through initiative: useful information, professional work, and things I could create.

The possibilities widened. So did the questions.

There was always something else to understand, another field to investigate, another way to connect what I knew with what someone needed. The work invited me to think about what a life might become over time.

Losing my mother made time itself harder to take for granted.

I cannot assume that everyone for whom I hope to build a better future will be present when that future arrives.

That is a difficult fact to place beside an ambition.

I do not want this essay to imply that money could have prevented her death.

I have offered no basis for that conclusion. Nor have I supplied an account of her final days from which a reader could responsibly draw it.

The question here concerns how the loss affected my thinking about provision, urgency, and the value of what can be done now.

A future achievement can be meaningful. It cannot be carried backwards into every moment in which it might once have mattered.

That limit gives present opportunities a different weight. If there is something useful I can do today, I have to consider it in the time in which it is available.

I cannot settle the question simply by pointing to what I hope eventually to accomplish.

This is where an old argument returns.

Be practical. Find a dependable income. Meet the needs in front of you.

I understand why that argument matters. I understood something of it even as a child. The concern for provision was never a trivial obstacle to my thinking; it helped produce the thinking in the first place.

After a loss, however, the argument can appear to claim more than it establishes.

The urgency of present needs does not, by itself, tell me which occupation to choose. It does not demonstrate that every uncertain project is foolish or that the familiar route would have resolved every difficulty.

I have to distinguish the force of the need from the adequacy of a proposed answer.

A warning may be right about what matters while remaining incomplete about what I should do.

I also have to examine my own use of the future.

An ambition can give effort direction. It can help me learn patiently and persist through work whose value takes time to develop.

But I can also use a future promise to avoid assessing what the work is doing in the present.

If I keep saying that something will matter eventually, what would allow me to recognise that it needs to change? What evidence suggests that it can support itself? Which responsibilities am I asking to wait?

These questions make the ambition more accountable.

They are especially important when the people affected by my choices have needs of their own. Their lives are not merely the setting in which my plans unfold.

I want to remember that when describing the future I hope to build.

Yet I would be wary of making immediacy the only measure of value.

Learning can take time. A professional practice develops through accumulated work. An institution may require preparation long before it becomes dependable.

If I judged every effort solely by what it produced this week, I could abandon work that deserved a longer attempt.

The problem is how to recognise what deserves that time.

I need to understand what is being learned, what progress has actually occurred, and what continuation would require. I also need to be honest about what I can afford to commit.

The present and the future make claims on the same life. I have to organise the work with enough care to hear both.

There is no single sentence that can make those claims cease to compete.

My mother's death does not exist merely to improve my decisions.

She was my mother before she became a subject I could write about. The loss has a meaning that exceeds any argument about enterprise or productivity.

I want to preserve that boundary.

I can write about a question the loss brought into focus without pretending that the question explains her life or exhausts my relationship with her. I can acknowledge its effect without treating grief as a source of instruction that must always yield something useful.

Some parts of an experience deserve to remain larger than what a piece of writing can make of them.

That includes what I cannot yet adequately express.

What I can say is that the search for a livelihood now carries this question more explicitly: when will the useful part of the work reach the lives it is supposed to serve?

The question need not demand an immediate grand result. It can direct attention to something smaller and real.

Has the work helped someone understand a problem? Has it produced a service another person can use? Is it becoming capable of meeting obligations rather than merely expanding the list of things I hope to build?

These are questions I can bring to my own efforts.

They do not measure the worth of a whole person. They help me assess the promises I make on behalf of a project.

That distinction matters when ambition is closely attached to identity.

For NaijaOS, this also clarifies what I mean by useful knowledge.

I began by asking what Nigerians would buy. Beneath that question were concerns about survival, independence, and the ability to provide.

An explanation of how a market or institution works should eventually make contact with a decision someone faces. What can the reader understand more clearly? What must still be investigated? What can reasonably be attempted within the person's circumstances?

I want room for large questions about what Nigeria could become. I also want a reader with an immediate responsibility to find something usable.

The difficulty of bringing those purposes together is part of the work.

My own experience is one reason I cannot treat it as a purely editorial problem.

I am still considering what the loss changes and what it leaves unresolved.

It does not tell me to surrender every long ambition. It does ask me to examine how long I expect the people and needs around me to wait for its benefits.

It does not establish that my earlier fears were correct. It gives me reason to investigate whether the life I am building can support responsibilities in the present.

And it leaves me with a limit I cannot negotiate: some opportunities to care belong to a particular time.

I cannot recover every opportunity that has passed. I can pay closer attention to the ones that remain.

I do not have a triumphant ending for this part of the story.

I lost my mother while I was still trying to understand how to build a life. I continue that work with a sharper sense of what cannot be postponed indefinitely.

There are things I want to create that will require patience. There are people and responsibilities whose claims arrive now. I have to make decisions within that tension.

Perhaps the most honest change I can make is to be more precise about time whenever I describe what my work is for.

Who might it help? In what way? How will I know? And when?

I still want to build towards a better future.

I want to give the present a more considered place in the work.
