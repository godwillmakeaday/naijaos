When Your Perspective Becomes Something People Invite You to Share

What can your perspective contribute to a particular audience?

By Paul A. Magaji · 9 min read · Commercial exploration

A person may encounter an article I have written and think: our people need to hear this discussed.

That possibility creates another connection between public work and income.

An organisation could invite me to address a subject at a conference, contribute to a panel, lead a discussion or deliver a briefing. The invitation might concern something I have studied, something I have built or a question my work helps people understand.

As I develop my websites, I want to explore how that possibility could grow.

I am not describing an established international speaking business. I am asking what would make my contribution useful enough for an organiser to include it in an event—and, where appropriate, pay for it.

The answer needs to begin with the audience.

Identify what the audience needs from the occasion

An event brings people together for a reason.

They may need to understand a development, examine a difficult decision or see possibilities they have not yet considered. They may want practical instruction, informed disagreement or a perspective that connects separate parts of their work.

The speaker’s task depends on that purpose.

Before proposing a topic, I would want to understand who will attend and why the organiser is bringing them together.

What do participants already know? What questions are troubling them? What would make the session worth their time?

Those questions give the talk a direction.

A subject that interests me becomes a possible speaking offer when I can explain its relevance to the people in the room.

Develop a perspective with substance behind it

Having an opinion gives me something to say. A paid engagement requires me to examine why the contribution deserves the audience’s attention.

What have I studied closely? Which experiences can I describe accurately? What evidence supports my argument? Where does my knowledge end?

My background gives me several possible starting points.

Law has trained me to examine claims, responsibilities and consequences. Teaching has required me to explain ideas. Building websites has introduced practical questions about turning an intention into something people can use.

The connection between those experiences interests me.

For example, I could investigate how a professional working from Nigeria uses digital tools to develop public work while retaining responsibility for its quality.

That could become a useful talk if I support it with actual examples, clear distinctions and lessons an audience can assess.

The perspective needs to be developed through work.

Give each talk a clear promise

A broad topic can make it difficult for an organiser to understand what a session will contain.

“Technology and opportunity” could cover almost anything. A talk examining what it takes to turn one professional capability into a clearly defined digital offer gives the organiser a more concrete proposition.

A useful description should identify the central question, the intended audience and what the session will help them understand.

The title can attract interest. The description needs to explain the contribution.

I would also want to be clear about the format. A short keynote, a moderated conversation and a practical workshop involve different kinds of preparation and participation.

An organiser should be able to judge whether the proposed session fits the occasion.

Let the website provide evidence

My website can help someone assess a possible invitation before contacting me.

An article shows how I develop an argument. A project account shows what I have attempted and what I learned. A recording can show how I explain a subject aloud.

These forms of evidence answer different questions.

Strong writing does not establish every aspect of effective speaking. A short recorded explanation could give an organiser a better sense of my pace, clarity and delivery.

If the recording is prepared independently, I should present it as a demonstration. It need not be described as a previous paid engagement to be useful.

Over time, a speaking page could collect a few well-defined topics, relevant publications and representative recordings.

Its purpose would be to make my contribution easier to assess.

Adapt the material to the actual audience

A talk prepared for young people building their first projects would need different explanations from one prepared for experienced organisational leaders.

The central idea might remain relevant, but the examples, assumptions and questions would change.

I should therefore learn enough about the audience to prepare responsibly.

An organiser’s brief could help me understand participants’ roles, the event’s wider programme and any particular sensitivities around the subject.

That preparation also helps me avoid a familiar mistake: delivering everything I know because I have been given time to speak.

A session has limits. Selecting the material that serves the audience is part of the work.

Build an argument people can follow

A speaking engagement needs more than a collection of interesting observations.

The audience should be able to follow how the ideas connect.

I might begin with a recognisable problem, examine why it is difficult and develop a practical way to think about it. An example could then show what the argument means in use.

The structure should support the purpose of the session.

If I want people to understand a distinction, I need examples that make the difference visible. If I want them to assess a proposal, I need to explain its assumptions and limitations.

My legal training can contribute to that discipline. My teaching experience reminds me that an argument must also be accessible to the person encountering it for the first time.

Prepare for questions and disagreement

An audience may ask something that exposes a gap in my explanation.

Someone may have experience that complicates my example. Another participant may challenge the conclusion or ask how it applies to circumstances I have not investigated.

Those exchanges can improve the discussion if I handle them carefully.

I need to distinguish what I can answer from what requires further work. Where a question changes the assumptions, I should acknowledge that change.

A speaking invitation does not require me to appear certain about everything.

It requires preparation, attention and an honest account of what I can contribute.

That is especially relevant when discussing Nigeria with audiences whose experiences differ from mine. I can explain my circumstances and observations without presenting them as a complete account of the country.

Count the full commitment

The time spent speaking is only part of an engagement.

There may be research, planning conversations, preparation of materials, rehearsal, travel and follow-up. A remote event may require a technical check and arrangements for reliable participation.

Those demands affect whether I can accept the invitation and what fee would support the work.

I would want to understand the complete commitment before agreeing.

A short session may require substantial preparation if the audience or subject is unfamiliar. A request for a tailored briefing may involve more research than a talk based on work I have already developed.

The fee needs to be considered alongside that scope.

Agree on recording and reuse

An event can continue beyond the live session.

The organiser may want to record it, distribute the slides or publish extracts. I may also want permission to use a short recording as an example of my work.

Those expectations should be discussed early.

The arrangement needs to identify what may be recorded, how the material may be used and whether further distribution requires another agreement.

I would also need to examine what I include in the presentation. Material suitable for a limited discussion may require different permissions or treatment before wider publication.

My legal background makes these questions familiar. Their practical purpose is to help both sides understand what happens to the work after the event.

Make remote participation dependable

Speaking from Nigeria to an audience elsewhere may be possible without travelling.

That can reduce some demands while introducing others.

The session needs workable timing, clear audio, a suitable environment and a way to manage technical difficulties. Slides and demonstrations should be tested in the actual format where possible.

I should also understand how the event will be moderated.

Who introduces the session? How are audience questions handled? What happens if the connection fails? Is there an alternative way to deliver the essential material?

These preparations support the contribution I have been invited to make.

A thoughtful argument is more useful when people can hear it and follow it comfortably.

Learn from a small first offer

I do not need a large catalogue of talks before testing one.

A manageable starting point would be a topic grounded in my existing work, a clear audience and a short session with an identifiable purpose.

I could prepare a demonstration, seek informed feedback and revise the material.

After an actual engagement, I would want to understand what participants found useful, where they became confused and which questions remained.

An invitation establishes that an organiser saw potential relevance. The experience helps me assess whether I delivered it.

Those observations can improve both the talk and the writing from which it grew.

Give the audience something worth carrying away

Speaking interests me because it allows ideas to enter a live exchange.

A person can ask a question, challenge an assumption or connect the argument to their own work. That interaction can make the subject clearer for the audience and for me.

For it to become a dependable income opportunity, I need a contribution that serves the occasion and preparation that supports its delivery.

My next step is to choose one question my work equips me to address, define the audience it could help and prepare a talk someone can assess.

The invitation may begin with something I publish online. Its value will depend on what people gain when I speak.
