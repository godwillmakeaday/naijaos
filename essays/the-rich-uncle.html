The Rich Uncle Is Doing Several Institutions’ Jobs

The support hidden inside a family relationship.

By Paul A. Magaji · 7 min read · Who gets room to attempt

There is a familiar explanation for why a person has not advanced: he has nobody to help him.

Sometimes it is expressed more specifically. He has no rich uncle. No influential relative. Nobody who can make a call, introduce him to the right person or support him while he finds his feet.

I have been thinking about what this explanation contains.

At first, it sounds like a question about money. But the uncle people describe may be supplying much more than money. He may be providing a place to live, an introduction, someone willing to vouch for a young person, or an assurance that one unsuccessful attempt will not become a family catastrophe.

When we put all of that inside the sentence “I have nobody,” we can lose sight of the separate things that are missing.

The rich uncle becomes the name we give to an entire support system.

What help actually consists of

Imagine two people who want to start the same small business.

One can stay with a relative while learning the trade. Someone introduces him to a supplier. A family member lends him equipment. If his first order goes badly, he can still eat and try again.

The other must pay rent immediately. He approaches suppliers as a stranger. He buys or hires every tool. A failed order may consume the money he needs for food.

These are illustrative people, but the distinction matters. Even if they have equal ability and work equally hard, they are not attempting the same practical task.

One is trying to establish a business. The other is trying to establish a business while financing the conditions that allow him to attempt it.

We can easily praise the first person’s confidence and question the second person’s hesitation without examining what confidence costs each of them.

This does not mean the supported person has done no work. It means that an honest account of his work should include the conditions under which he could perform it.

The introduction before the opportunity

An introduction can accomplish something that money alone cannot.

It can persuade someone to listen.

A young person may have a useful service to offer but no recognised record. The potential customer does not know whether he will deliver. The young person cannot acquire a record without first being trusted to do some work.

A relative’s recommendation can bridge that gap.

Sometimes that recommendation helps a capable newcomer receive fair consideration. Sometimes it secures preferential treatment regardless of competence. We should distinguish those situations. Opening a door for someone and removing every standard for him are different actions.

The question is whether people without an influential sponsor have another credible way to demonstrate what they can do.

Can they complete a small paid assignment? Present work that can be assessed? Enter an apprenticeship through an understandable process? Obtain a reference based on observed performance?

If the only convincing evidence of reliability is a powerful person saying “he is my boy,” then competence remains dependent on personal sponsorship before it can even be examined.

A place to survive the attempt

We often discuss access to capital as though the only relevant money were the money inside the business.

But the founder has to live while the business is developing.

Someone must cover food, accommodation, transport and other obligations. Someone must absorb the delay between beginning useful work and receiving enough income from it.

A family can provide that interval quietly. Its contribution may never appear in the business accounts.

This is one reason I am cautious about advice that tells everybody simply to take a risk. The same decision can carry very different consequences in different households.

A person with dependable support may lose an investment. Another may lose the ability to meet an immediate obligation to someone who depends on him.

That difference does not settle which decision either person should make. It should change how we judge them.

It also changes what an opportunity ought to provide. An unpaid opening may be accessible mainly to those who can afford to remain unpaid. A training programme may be formally open while its transport and accommodation costs exclude the people it claims to welcome.

Access has to survive contact with the participant’s actual life.

What we ask of the family

There is generosity in families supporting their members. I would not want an argument for better institutions to become an argument against that generosity.

But we should examine the weight we place on it.

The successful relative may become a source of school fees, emergency assistance, employment introductions and business finance. Each request can be reasonable in isolation. Together, they may exceed what one person can sustain.

Meanwhile, a person who receives assistance may find it difficult to separate gratitude from obligation. What does he owe in return? Can he choose a different profession? Disagree with his benefactor? Decline a request?

Support does not inevitably become control. But unclear expectations can make independence difficult to negotiate.

The institutional question concerns both sides of this relationship: how can we expand opportunity for the person seeking help while reducing the demand that one relative carry so many other lives?

The institutional work hidden inside a relationship

The rich uncle is not literally a bank, an employment service, a landlord, an insurer and a mentor. A family relationship has its own meaning and cannot be reproduced completely through formal arrangements.

Still, describing what he provides helps us identify work that could also be organised elsewhere.

A transparent apprenticeship can provide access to learning. A fair recruitment process can make vacancies visible. A modest paid first assignment can allow a newcomer to establish credibility. Clear financing terms can make the obligations of assistance understandable.

These arrangements also need scrutiny. A loan can become an unbearable burden. A placement can become unpaid exploitation. A programme with an impressive name can offer little useful learning.

Creating an institution is not enough. We must ask whether its everyday operation supplies the support people actually need.

The standard is practical: can a capable person without a sponsor find the opportunity, understand the terms, meet reasonable requirements and receive what was promised?

Capability after the introduction

There is an uncomfortable question on the other side of this argument.

If someone opened the door tomorrow, would I be ready to do the work?

The absence of support can be real while gaps in preparation are also real. We should be able to investigate both without using one to dismiss the other.

Someone may need an introduction and better skills. A business may need financing and a more credible account of its customers. A professional may need visibility and a stronger record of delivery.

The point of examining unequal support is to improve our explanation of outcomes. It should also improve our choices.

What can I prepare now? What requires assistance? What kind of assistance would be useful? What evidence could I offer someone who has no personal reason to trust me?

Specific answers move us beyond waiting for an unspecified person to change everything.

Beyond the fortunate family

For NaijaOS, the rich-uncle question offers a way to investigate opportunity in concrete terms.

Choose a task: learning a trade, securing a first client, obtaining equipment or moving to take up work. Then identify the support required at each stage.

Which part can the individual reasonably supply? Which part is being supplied through relatives? What happens when the relatives cannot help? Could an accessible arrangement perform some of that work, and how would we know it was working?

This also gives those of us building enterprises a responsibility.

We may not be able to establish a large institution immediately. But we can make a vacancy visible, explain how someone will be selected, define a paid assignment clearly and provide a reference when the work deserves one.

Each is a small decision about whether access depends on knowing us personally.

The existence of a generous uncle should remain a good thing. The absence of one should not settle the extent of a person’s possibilities.

When someone says “I have nobody,” I want us to hear a question we can investigate: what, exactly, does this person need someone to provide?

An answer precise enough to act on may reveal something we can build.
