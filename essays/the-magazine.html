The Magazine That Opened My World

Success Digest and the boundaries of a newly discovered world.

By Paul A. Magaji · 7 min read · How a world becomes visible

Personal essay

Download Word document

A friend gave me a magazine called Success Digest, and I began to see a way of making a living that I had not previously understood.

Someone could learn something useful, organise it, and sell the resulting information to people who needed it. The product did not have to begin as a shop full of goods. It could begin with a problem and the knowledge required to address it.

I followed the magazine closely. I remember becoming absorbed in that world: the possibility of creating a product, making an offer, and earning through something I had assembled myself.

For someone who had once worried that life without a government job might be terribly difficult, this was a consequential discovery. It gave initiative a form I could recognise.

Years later, I began to understand how much remained outside the world I had entered.

***

I do not want to diminish what the magazine did for me by judging it only through what I discovered afterwards.

It helped make enterprise imaginable. That is a substantial contribution.

Before a person attempts something, he needs some account of how an attempt might work. He needs to see a connection between what he can do and something another person might value. Useful information supplied one such connection for me.

I could read, learn, explain, and organise. Here was a way those activities might become commercially useful.

I was particularly interested in the relationship between information and its setting. Knowledge that solved a specific problem for a particular person could have a value that general knowledge did not. Someone trying to complete a task might pay for clarity that saved time or helped him proceed.

That possibility drew me in. It suggested that attention itself could become productive: notice a difficulty, understand it properly, and prepare something useful.

The attraction was also practical. I could imagine creating a product over a weekend and then trying to sell it.

Whether any particular offer would succeed was another question. What mattered at the beginning was that I could now imagine making one.

***

Yet the world I followed became so absorbing that I did not adequately see another one developing.

I later discovered startups and software companies working on problems I had scarcely considered as business opportunities. There were people building companies, attracting attention, and creating professional needs beyond the information enterprise I had been following.

I was already a lawyer.

That made the discovery uncomfortable. Some of this activity should have been relevant to the direction of my professional development. I might have investigated what these companies were doing, what difficulties they encountered, and where legal understanding could help.

I had not done enough of that. I did not even know enough about the field to recognise many of the questions I should have been asking.

It would be inaccurate to say the magazine prevented me from discovering it. My reading choices, relationships, circumstances, and habits of attention all belonged to the explanation.

But I had become accustomed to looking for opportunity within a particular account of opportunity.

What it illuminated occupied my attention. What it did not illuminate was easier to miss.

***

This is a difficult thing to recognise while it is happening.

A source can be useful enough to earn your trust and familiar enough to organise your expectations. You return to it because it has helped before. Over time, the questions it regularly addresses become the questions you expect to matter.

You can be learning continually and still be overlooking a whole field.

I know this because that is how I understand part of my own experience. The problem was not that I had stopped being curious. My curiosity had developed habits.

I was interested in what could be learned, packaged, and sold. I had not given comparable attention to how software businesses were created or how my profession might connect with them.

The distinction mattered. A legal qualification gave me a professional identity. It did not automatically tell me where new needs for legal work were emerging.

To discover those needs, I would have had to look beyond the conversations that already felt relevant to me.

***

My later interest in programming brought this into sharper focus.

I started with Ruby and went on to HTML, CSS, JavaScript, and Python. I did not begin with a complete technical plan. I encountered languages, examples, and explanations and tried to understand what they made possible.

Learning them introduced further questions. What did each part do? What else would I need? How did something written on a computer become a service people used?

I was gradually discovering that the visible product rested on a larger arrangement of knowledge and work.

That experience also changed how I thought about information products. Explaining a task and making a tool that helps perform it can serve related needs, but they require different capabilities. A reader might benefit from a guide. Another person might need a working service, a reliable process, or professional assistance.

Understanding the problem has to come before deciding which form the answer should take.

I had first encountered a form of enterprise that fitted abilities I could already recognise in myself. Learning about other forms required me to accept how much I did not yet know.

***

I now see a responsibility here for anyone who publishes useful information, including me.

A publication necessarily selects. It cannot cover every field, place, profession, or possibility. Selection gives it coherence and allows a reader to know why to return.

The difficulty arises when that selected view begins to look complete.

For NaijaOS, I want to keep the scope of an explanation visible. An account of how one market works should leave room for other markets to work differently. A story about one entrepreneur should not be made to carry the experience of everyone trying to earn a living.

The practical task is to help a reader understand something specific while also showing where further inquiry is needed.

That requires more than collecting success stories. I would want to know how someone found the opportunity, what knowledge was missing at the beginning, who helped, what the work required, and what failed along the way.

These details can make an unfamiliar activity intelligible. They also give a reader something to investigate beyond admiration for the result.

***

The same discipline applies to my use of AI.

When I began asking different systems what Nigerians would buy, I was again looking for access to knowledge I did not possess. These tools helped me approach the question from several directions.

But a persuasive answer could still give me a narrow picture. Agreement between answers did not establish that I had understood the market.

I needed to ask what the answer assumed, which people it described, and what evidence could test it. I also needed contact with the people whose choices would determine whether an offer was useful.

I could recognise a familiar temptation: to stay inside an information environment because it continued to produce things worth thinking about.

The remedy I can practise is to give some of those thoughts a task outside the conversation. Speak to a prospective customer. Examine an unfamiliar company. Learn how a workshop actually operates. Try a modest offer and pay attention to the response.

Each encounter may reveal something the information source could not supply.

***

I remain grateful for the magazine my friend brought me.

It helped me see that knowledge could become useful work and that I could take an initiative of my own. I would not erase that discovery simply because it was incomplete.

What I want to change is my expectation of completeness.

A publication can help me enter a field. A profession can give me a way to contribute. A tool can help me ask questions. I still have to notice where their reach ends and where another inquiry should begin.

This is one of the habits I want to carry into NaijaOS: follow useful knowledge closely, then look deliberately beyond the places where I have learned to expect it.

Success Digest opened a world for me.

Discovering that there were other worlds became another part of my education.
