# Earning from Nigeria by Creating Value for the World

By Paul A. Magaji

Editorial context: No international earnings, paid customers or active affiliate partnership are claimed.

I want an international presence because useful work should have the opportunity to travel. Knowledge, judgment or a practical skill may address a need beyond my immediate environment.

The challenge is making that value visible, understandable and purchasable.

I am exploring the commercial possibility. This is a direction I want to pursue, rather than a claim that international income has already followed.

## Begin with a recognised problem

My interests provide subjects. A buyer needs a reason to spend money.

What are they trying to accomplish? Where are they struggling? What would a useful result look like?

An article can investigate the question. A practical resource can demonstrate an approach. A conversation with a potential buyer can reveal whether the difficulty is important enough to justify payment.

I want an offer to emerge from that understanding.

## Make the deliverable clear

“Insight” is difficult to assess before purchase. A defined research brief, workshop or resource gives someone a more concrete basis for consideration.

The description should explain what they receive, what information it depends on and what requires their participation. Outcomes outside my control need clear limits.

My legal background makes me attentive to those boundaries. They help both sides understand the engagement.

## Demonstrate capability publicly

A website allows someone to examine my thinking before making an enquiry. An essay can show care with evidence. A project can show how I approach a problem. A worksheet can let someone try part of a method.

These examples do not guarantee a sale. They give a buyer a basis for considering one.

My phrase “global-rate value” means work that can justify a fair price in a wider market. Visibility alone does not establish that price. Scope, costs, expertise, alternatives and dependable delivery all matter.

## Consider the different ways useful work can earn

Affiliate income is one possibility I am exploring. There are others, and I want to understand what each would require.

A website can make several kinds of commercial relationship possible. What matters is identifying what someone values enough to pay for.

Someone might pay for my time and judgment. That could involve a research assignment, an advisory engagement or help developing a particular project. The work would need an agreed scope and a result I could realistically deliver.

Another person might need a resource they can use independently. A practical guide, worksheet, template or research report could turn part of my knowledge into a product. Its usefulness would depend on how well it helps the buyer perform a task.

Others might want to learn. A workshop or course could help people understand a subject and apply it to their own circumstances. That would require more than assembling information. I would need to organise the learning and give participants a way to practise.

Some needs continue over time. A publication, membership or subscription could provide regularly updated research, resources or discussion. A continuing fee would create a continuing obligation to provide something worthwhile.

An organisation might also pay for permission to use work I have already developed. It could license a training resource, commission an adaptation or arrange to reproduce a publication. That possibility makes ownership, permission and the terms of reuse important.

There may also be income associated with the audience a website attracts. Advertising, sponsorship and affiliate referrals work differently, but each can connect a business with people interested in a subject. I would need to make commercial relationships clear and preserve my independence in deciding what deserves publication.

Public work could lead to invitations to speak, teach or contribute to a larger project. In those cases, the website helps someone discover capabilities they need elsewhere.

These possibilities encourage me to think more expansively about what I am building.

An article might help a reader immediately while revealing a question that deserves a fuller guide. A guide might lead to a workshop. A workshop might uncover a problem an organisation wants help solving.

None of those steps is automatic. Each involves a different buyer, a different promise and a different delivery burden.

I do not need to pursue every route at once. I need to recognise the possibilities, choose a manageable starting point and learn where my work proves useful enough for someone to purchase.

The ambition is to become better at seeing the different forms value can take—and then doing the work each form requires.

## Deliver what was agreed

Attracting a buyer creates an obligation. Deadlines, revisions and support need clear expectations. The offer should fit what I can realistically provide.

The transaction also needs an understandable route: identity, scope, price, payment and what happens afterwards. The particular arrangements and obligations require checking for the actual engagement.

An initial delivery can teach me whether the offer was clear, useful and sustainable to repeat.

## A first offer brief

Write down the buyer, problem, deliverable, scope limits, delivery time, required inputs and full cost to fulfil. Then discuss the problem with a potential buyer before building a large commercial system around it.
