Why Can’t We Build a TSMC?

How investment becomes lasting industrial capability.

Why could Nigeria not have built something like TSMC?

The question came to me while thinking about the industries we might have developed. We could commit public resources to an undertaking such as Ajaokuta. Why could we not have committed resources to semiconductor manufacturing?

I am not presenting a cost comparison between the two projects. I have not established that their requirements, historical opportunities or likely outcomes were equivalent. My question begins with a refusal to treat the industrial achievements of other societies as naturally beyond us.

But that refusal needs discipline. Money spent on an ambition does not automatically become the capability the ambition requires.

To take the question seriously, we have to investigate what building a TSMC actually means.

Which part of TSMC do we mean

TSMC stands for Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Company. Its defining business model is the dedicated foundry: manufacturing chips designed by customers, rather than building its own competing branded chip products. TSMC describes this focus as central to the model it pioneered in 1987. [1]

Already, the question has become more specific.

Are we asking whether Nigeria could develop businesses that design chips? Whether we could manufacture wafers? Package and test chips? Or build electronic products using chips manufactured elsewhere?

These activities belong to related parts of an industry, but they are not interchangeable.

A country can acquire valuable engineering capabilities without beginning with the most technically demanding fabrication processes. Equally, assembling an electronic device from imported components does not establish domestic semiconductor fabrication.

Precision protects ambition from being satisfied too easily.

The history before the famous company

TSMC did not appear in an empty institutional landscape.

ITRI, Taiwan’s Industrial Technology Research Institute, records a technology-transfer and licensing agreement with RCA in 1976. Its account of TSMC’s formation describes an earlier demonstration factory and the transfer of facilities, equipment, technology and 98 professionals to the company. TSMC’s own historical filings identify government, Philips and other private investors as participants in its founding. [2–4]

These facts do not supply a complete explanation of Taiwan’s success. They establish something narrower and important: the company inherited organised learning and technical assets.

The public contribution included preparing people and capabilities that an enterprise could use.

That changes how I understand the comparison. If we look only at the eventual company, we may miss the years in which a society was learning how to make that company possible.

A government can announce a factory in a day. Training a group of people to diagnose difficult production failures takes longer.

Purchasing equipment and mastering production

Consider the difference between possessing a machine and being able to obtain a dependable result from it.

The machine must be installed correctly. Inputs must meet requirements. People must understand the process well enough to recognise when something has changed and determine why.

In semiconductor manufacturing, a central practical concern is yield: the proportion of manufactured chips that meet the required specifications.

A facility that can produce some working chips has crossed a technical threshold. A business that must deliver acceptable quantities, quality and costs faces further demands.

TSMC’s 2025 annual report discusses manufacturing yield and identifies exposure to interruptions in electricity and water, alongside dependencies involving suppliers and specialised resources. [5]

The relevant capability therefore extends beyond the factory building. It includes reliable services, technical support and routines that preserve quality over time.

Money can buy parts of that arrangement. It must also support the people who learn to operate and improve it.

What Ajaokuta can and cannot prove

My original comparison with Ajaokuta expresses frustration about national choices. It does not prove an alternative history.

To argue that Nigeria would have built a successful semiconductor industry by spending differently, we would need evidence about the period concerned: available partners, technical skills, infrastructure, market access and the capacity to sustain the undertaking.

We would also need to examine the uses of the money we imagine redirecting. An announced sum, an actual expenditure and a recoverable alternative investment are different things.

I can still ask why we did not develop particular capabilities without claiming certainty about what a different allocation would have achieved.

The harder question is whether our institutions could have protected a sustained learning effort, recognised failure early and changed direction intelligently.

An expensive industrial project can fail. A cheaper project can fail too. Neither its size nor its prestige establishes that it was well chosen.

State support and commercial discipline

TSMC’s history gives little support to the idea that public action and private enterprise must be considered separately.

But public involvement alone does not explain success. We still have to ask what an intervention accomplished and whether the resulting business could meet demanding customers’ needs.

For a proposed Nigerian programme, I would want to know who is responsible for technical decisions, how performance will be assessed and what happens when a milestone is missed.

Patient funding should allow time for learning. It should also require evidence that learning is occurring.

That evidence might include a working design, independently assessed test results, an improvement in manufacturing performance or a customer willing to pay for the output.

The exact measure depends on the activity. The principle is that political commitment must remain answerable to technical and commercial results.

An institution becomes fragile when reporting a problem threatens the programme more than leaving the problem unresolved.

A starting point we could investigate

A credible beginning requires an assessment of what already exists.

Which Nigerian universities, laboratories, firms and individual engineers have relevant capabilities? Which Nigerian professionals abroad would participate in defined projects? What equipment is available, and what work is it actually being used to perform?

Without that investigation, even an enthusiastic proposal risks overlooking existing work or duplicating a poorly used facility.

One possible starting point to examine is a focused design and verification programme connected to external fabrication partners. A team could work towards a small, clearly specified chip design, assess it and, if fabrication access were secured, test the manufactured result.

Open resources can assist learning. The SkyWater SKY130 process design kit, for example, makes process-related design information available. Its documentation explicitly describes the open release as experimental and unsuitable for production use at its stated status. Access to files does not secure fabrication, qualification or a viable product. [6]

This is an example of a learning route, not a finding that it is Nigeria’s best commercial opportunity.

Other activities, including packaging, testing or specialised services, would need their own assessment. They should not be described as easy simply because they differ from leading-edge wafer fabrication.

Let the customer enter the argument

A national programme needs a reason for someone to use its output.

Suppose a proposal targets electricity metering, agricultural sensing or industrial monitoring. These are candidate problem areas to investigate, not demonstrated markets for a new Nigerian chip.

Would a custom design solve a problem that existing components cannot solve economically? How many units would be required? Who would purchase them? What reliability, certification and servicing obligations would follow?

Sometimes the investigation may show that the useful business lies in system design, firmware or dependable maintenance, using existing chips.

That would be a meaningful result. A serious inquiry must permit the evidence to change the proposed product.

Customer requirements should influence the technical programme early enough to prevent us building something whose main achievement is its inauguration.

What would count as progress

We should be able to describe progress before spending heavily.

A training programme should demonstrate what participants can design and verify. A prototype project should record what worked and what failed. A commercial programme should establish whether customers receive sufficient value to keep purchasing.

The knowledge should also survive personnel changes. Documentation, maintained equipment and people capable of teaching successors are part of the asset being created.

A promising experiment that reveals an unviable market can still save resources if its findings are recorded and acted upon. Repeating the same experiment under a new name does not provide the same value.

These standards are demanding because the ambition deserves demanding standards.

Returning to why we cannot

I do not know that Nigeria could have reproduced TSMC’s historical path. The evidence considered here cannot settle that counterfactual.

I do know that pointing to the company’s present achievement is insufficient. We have to examine the preceding work and the relationships that sustained it.

The question I want NaijaOS to preserve is whether we can organise a sequence of efforts through which capability accumulates.

That means selecting a task, preparing people, securing the required inputs and allowing results to determine the next investment. It means asking who will maintain the work after the first enthusiasts leave.

“Why can’t we build a TSMC?” remains a useful provocation.

The next question is more exacting: what could we build, test and improve now that would leave us more capable of undertaking the next difficult thing?
