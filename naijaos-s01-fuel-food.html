Why food prices keep rising — the fuel-to-food transmission, decoded.

A working diagnostic of how a fuel price move becomes a food price move in Nigeria. The four-stage transmission, the lag at each stage, and the March 2026 inflation re-acceleration as the cleanest case study the system has produced. Read this if you want to know what your kitchen will look like in eight weeks.

01The frame

Food does not become expensive for one reason. It becomes expensive for one mechanism.

Most discussion of Nigerian food inflation explains it as if each new price move had a fresh cause — a flood, a bad harvest, a hoarding scandal, a closed border. Sometimes one of these is real. Always, underneath, the same mechanism is running.

The pillar briefing — how money actually works in Nigeria — names the fuel transmission as one of the three forces moving income outcomes in 2026. This post unfolds it. How does a price move at the petrol pump become a price move on a market shelf, and why does the second move arrive predictably weeks after the first? The answer is structural to how Nigerian food moves through the country. Understand the structure once and the news headlines about food prices stop being individual surprises and become readable cycles.

Food prices in Nigeria are geographic. The food has to travel.
02The mechanism

Why fuel sets the floor for the food basket.

The transmission depends on three structural facts about the Nigerian economy that are obvious once stated and rarely stated together.

F · 01

Most Nigerian food travels far before it is eaten.

Tomatoes consumed in Lagos are typically grown in the north. Rice eaten in Port Harcourt is often milled in Ebonyi or imported through Lagos. Yam in Kano frequently comes from Benue or Taraba. Pepper in Abuja moves from Kaduna or Kebbi. The journey from farm to plate is rarely a single short trip; it is multiple long trips through several middlemen. Each leg of the journey runs on diesel.

F · 02

The journey runs almost entirely on roads.

Nigerian rail freight handles a small share of agricultural goods relative to road freight. Inland water transport is limited. Aviation is irrelevant for most food categories. Functionally, the country moves its food on trucks fuelled by diesel and petrol, and the cost of that fuel feeds directly into the cost of every kilogram that reaches a market.

F · 03

Food is the largest single line in most household budgets.

The share of household spending devoted to food in Nigeria is high — among the highest in any large economy. Food has the heaviest weight in the inflation basket, which is why headline inflation in Nigeria moves so closely with food inflation. What happens to food prices is what happens to most Nigerians' cost of living. Fuel is the variable that sets that floor.

03The transmission

Four stages, four lags.

The transmission from a fuel price move to a food price move is not a single step. It is a four-stage cascade, each stage with its own lag. The diagram below maps the typical time it takes for a fuel move to reach each subsequent stage.

Three observations about the diagram itself.

One — the cascade is not uniform. Each stage runs on a different lag because each stage operates on a different supply chain length. Transport re-prices fast because truckers update fares between trips. Staples lag because middlemen sell from existing inventory before re-pricing the next lot. Proteins lag the most because cold-chain logistics, breeding cycles, and feed pricing all interpose between fuel and final price.

Two — the diagram describes the move up. When fuel rises, this is the timeline. When fuel falls, the same diagram applies in principle but the lags are longer at every stage. The reverse cascade is what produces the asymmetry the post returns to in § 06.

Three — the lag is what makes the transmission feel mysterious. Most Nigerians experience food inflation without knowing what triggered it because the trigger fired weeks earlier and has already been absorbed into the news cycle. By the time tomatoes are dearer at the market, the fuel move that drove the increase is no longer the headline. The diagram makes the link visible by making the timeline visible.

04The historical record

Three years, one mechanism.

The transmission is not a 2026 phenomenon. It has been running, observably, for the entire post-subsidy period. The figures below show the same pattern firing in three distinct cycles — a fuel move at the start of each window, a transport response within weeks, a food response within months.

The pattern in the table is the same pattern in the diagram, observed across three cycles. 2024 is the cascade firing fully — fuel up, transport up, food up, headline inflation up. The 2025 window is the cascade unwinding — fuel range-bound, transport stabilising, food disinflating, headline inflation falling for eleven straight months. March 2026 is the cascade re-firing. The first month-on-month re-acceleration since early 2025 arrived with food, transport, and restaurants as the largest contributors — exactly the categories the diagram predicts will move first.

This is what the framework means when the pillar calls the fuel transmission a structural force. Structural means it does not stop. It pauses. It reverses partially. It re-fires. The mechanism does not turn off because policymakers want it to or because a single quarter of disinflation suggested it had.

CASE STUDY · MARCH 2026

The cleanest worked example the system has produced.

Eleven months of disinflation gave the framework an unusually clean baseline. When the cascade re-fired in March 2026, every stage was visible at once. Read this as a pattern recognition exercise, not a one-time event.

For most of 2025, the headline inflation chart looked like steady progress — a long descent from a March 2025 peak above 33 percent down to 15.06 percent by February 2026, the lowest reading since November 2020. Then the chart bent. March 2026 produced a 0.32 percentage point re-acceleration to 15.38 percent — modest in size, decisive in meaning.

M · 01

What moved first.

Pump prices for PMS had drifted upward through the first quarter of 2026, driven by a combination of global oil-price firmness and intermittent supply tightness in domestic refining. The move was small per-litre but consistent over weeks. By mid-February, transport rates in the major commercial corridors had visibly re-priced — bus and intercity fares in Lagos, Abuja, Kaduna, and the Eastern routes were running materially above their late-2025 levels.

M · 02

What moved next.

Late February and early March saw the staples adjustment. Tomatoes rose first — they always do; tomatoes have the shortest shelf life and the highest transport sensitivity in the basket. Rice and beans followed in the second week of March. Garri moved with them. By the time the NBS published its March CPI release in April, food inflation had risen from 13.57 percent in February to 14.31 percent — a 0.74 percentage point monthly move, much larger than the headline figure suggested at first reading.

M · 03

What is moving now.

By the publication date of this post, the protein response is underway but not fully visible in the data. Frozen fish, chicken, and beef typically lag the staple cycle by 4 to 12 weeks. Operators in cold-chain retail are already reporting wholesale price increases that will reach household-level pricing in the coming weeks. The cascade is not finished. The March 2026 release captured stages two and three. Stage four is still in transit.

M · 04

What this means for the next print.

Two outcomes are possible. The cascade can complete on its standard timeline, in which case April and May releases will show food inflation continuing to rise modestly while the protein adjustment surfaces, before the system stabilises in the second half of the year. Alternatively, an upstream input — fuel falling back, harvest seasonality stabilising staples, or a base effect from 2025 highs — can dampen the cascade midway. The framework's position is the first scenario unless evidence forces a re-read.

06The asymmetry

Why prices fall slower than they rise.

The transmission is not symmetric. A fuel move up reaches food prices on the timeline the diagram describes. A fuel move down reaches food prices much more slowly — and sometimes barely at all.

The structural reasons prices unwind slower than they rise.

One — sellers pass on increases faster than decreases. This is a behavioural feature of nearly all retail markets, not a Nigerian peculiarity. A trader who was forced to pay more last month does not voluntarily accept less margin this month, even when input costs ease. Margin recovery is treated as compensation for the period when margin was compressed.

Two — cumulative rises compound; reversing them requires every link to give back margin. A fuel move passes through transport, wholesale, and retail. Each link absorbed part of the rise in margin compression and added part to the consumer price. Reversing this requires every link, simultaneously, to give back the margin it took. This rarely happens in coordination, so the unwind is partial and slow.

Three — consumers normalise higher prices within weeks. A market shopper who paid ₦400 for a tomato cup last month and ₦450 this month will, within a few cycles, treat ₦450 as the baseline. The pressure on traders to revert to ₦400 fades. The new price becomes the new floor, and the next cycle begins from there.

This is why headline food inflation in Nigeria can come down — as it did across most of 2025 — while individual staple prices, in absolute terms, do not return to their pre-cycle levels. Disinflation is a slowing of the rate of increase. It is not a reversal of the level. Most Nigerians experience the asymmetry as a sense that prices never come down, even when the news reports lower inflation. They are reading the data correctly. The data is describing the rate of change, not the level.

07What this predicts

Reading food inflation before it arrives.

The reader who finishes this post should be able to do something most readers of Nigerian financial coverage cannot. They should be able to look at a fuel price move today and approximately predict the food price move that will arrive eight to sixteen weeks from now. Not in precise figures — the framework cannot do that, and any source that claims to is offering speculation. But in direction, in approximate magnitude, and in which categories will move first.

This predictive ability is not academic. For households, it is the difference between being surprised by a price rise and budgeting ahead of one. For businesses, it is the difference between absorbing a cost spike and pricing it in. For operators in any of the paths the cluster maps — POS, food vending, the workable-tier businesses in S/04 — it is the foundation of the buffer planning that the system's later siblings unfold.

The mechanism does not surprise the reader who has read it once. The mechanism only surprises the reader who never knew it was running.

A pump-price headline today is a market-shelf headline in eight weeks. Reading the first one well is reading the second one early.
08FAQ

Common questions this post keeps getting asked.

Q · 01

Why does fuel price affect food in Nigeria?

Because Nigerian food moves by road. Most staples are produced hundreds of kilometres from where they are consumed, and almost all of that distance is covered by diesel-powered trucks. When fuel prices rise, transport rates rise within days; when transport rates rise, food prices follow within weeks. The transmission is structural to the country's logistics geography, not a temporary feature of any one cycle.

Q · 02

How long does it take for fuel price changes to reach food prices?

The lag varies by category. Transport rates respond within days. Staples like rice, beans, garri, and tomatoes typically respond in two to six weeks. Proteins — frozen fish, chicken, beef — respond more slowly because cold-chain logistics absorb shocks differently, often producing visible price moves in two to four months. The cascade is fast on the way up and meaningfully slower on the way down.

Q · 03

What caused inflation to rise again in March 2026?

Headline inflation rose from 15.06 percent in February 2026 to 15.38 percent in March, the first re-acceleration after eleven months of disinflation. The National Bureau of Statistics named food, restaurants, and transport as the largest contributors. The pattern matches a fuel-driven transmission cycle that began earlier in the quarter and worked its way through the system on the standard lag — the cascade firing again, on schedule, after the disinflation pause.

Q · 04

Why do food prices fall slower than they rise?

Three reasons. Sellers are quicker to pass on increases than to pass on decreases — a behavioural feature of most retail markets. Cumulative rises along the supply chain compound, so reversing them requires every link to give back margin simultaneously, which rarely happens. And consumers normalise higher prices as a new baseline within weeks, removing pressure on sellers to lower them. The asymmetry is structural, not a market failure.

Q · 05

Is the fuel-to-food transmission unique to Nigeria?

The mechanism exists in most economies that move agricultural goods over long distances by road. What is distinctive about the Nigerian case is the strength of the transmission — a function of the country's logistics geography, the share of household budgets spent on food, and the limited rail and inland-water alternatives to road transport. The same fuel move that produces a 1 percent food inflation effect in a rail-heavy economy can produce a 4 to 5 percent effect in Nigeria.

Q · 06

What can a household do to protect itself from this cycle?

Three architectural moves. Buy storable staples ahead of expected cycles when fuel has visibly moved upward — rice, beans, garri, dry pepper. Diversify protein sources between fast-cycle (fish, chicken) and slow-cycle (eggs, dried fish) categories so the household budget is not all exposed to the same lag. And maintain the household-level equivalent of the buffer the system's playbooks call for — a cash reserve that absorbs the eight-to-sixteen-week price adjustment without forcing the family into borrowing or unhealthy substitution.
