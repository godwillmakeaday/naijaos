Inflation in Nigeria, March 2026 — what the re-acceleration means.

A focused decomposition of one CPI release. Headline at 15.38 percent, the eleven months of disinflation that preceded it, and the food and transport contributions that explain why the chart bent. Read this if you want to know whether to revise your assumptions about what the next print will say.

01The frame

A single number, read in context.

The figure most readers will encounter is 15.38 percent. The reading depends on what came before it.

The pillar briefing — how money actually works in Nigeria — names the numbers as one of the three things every reader should look at first. This post takes the most recent NBS CPI release and decomposes it: where the increase came from, what categories drove it, what it suggests about the next several months, and what it does not tell you. One CPI print is not a trend. But every CPI print is read against whatever the framework expected, and the gap between the expected and the actual is the only useful signal in the release.

An inflation print is a verdict on the trend. The trend is the story. The print is the punctuation.
02The trajectory

Eleven months down, then one up.

From a peak above 33 percent in March 2025 — itself the high point of the post-2023 dislocation — Nigerian headline inflation fell for eleven consecutive months, reaching 15.06 percent in February 2026. That February figure was the lowest reading since November 2020. Then March produced a 0.32 percentage point re-acceleration to 15.38 percent. Two observations the chart makes clearer than prose can.

One — the descent was real and substantial. Eighteen percentage points of disinflation across eleven months is not a statistical artefact. It reflects monetary tightening, FX stabilisation, base effects from the 2024 peaks, and the cooling of the food-cycle this cluster's S/01 sibling documents. The chart shows a structural improvement, not a cosmetic one.

Two — the uptick is small but visible. A 0.32 percentage point move is well within the noise band of any single CPI series, and on its own would be unremarkable. What makes it readable is that it broke an eleven-month consecutive run. The trend was unambiguous; the break, in context, is news. The size of the move is small. The shape of the chart is what changed.

Three things in the decomposition matter most.

One — food rose by more than headline. Food inflation moved 0.74 points while headline moved 0.32. Because food carries the heaviest weight in the basket, the disproportionate food move is what dragged the headline figure up. Without the food contribution, headline would have been close to flat or even slightly lower. This is the same pattern S/01 names: when the cascade re-fires, food is the fastest-visible signal in the data, even before transport has fully passed through.

Two — core inflation re-asserted itself. Core had been suppressed during the disinflation period by the combination of tight monetary policy and FX stability. The March 2026 figure shows core moving back toward the underlying price-pressure level the system carries beneath the headline. This is the most important secondary signal in the release: the disinflation was holding food and energy down, but the structural pressure underneath was always going to surface when those tailwinds eased.

Three — restaurants and transport contributed alongside food. The NBS release names these explicitly, which is the framework's clearest cross-validation. Restaurants are food-plus-service; their price moves combine the food cascade with wage and rent passthrough. Transport is the pillar's fastest channel — when it appears in the named drivers of a CPI release, the cascade is visibly active rather than hypothetical.

04What it means

One print does not make a trend. But it can break one.

The eleven months of disinflation were a structural improvement. The March 2026 release does not erase that improvement. It does, however, shift the framework's reading of where the system is heading.

M · 01

Disinflation has paused, not necessarily reversed.

A single 0.32 percentage point uptick after eleven consecutive declines is consistent with several different stories: a one-month noise event that the next print will reverse; the start of a shallow re-acceleration cycle that runs for two to four months before stabilising; or the early signal of a structural regime change driven by renewed fuel and FX pressure. The framework's reading is the second scenario as the central case, with scenarios one and three as the bracketing tails.

M · 02

The fuel-to-food cascade is operative.

The S/01 sibling shows the cascade structure: fuel moves first, transport responds within days, staples within weeks, proteins within months. The March 2026 release shows transport and food moving together, with restaurants passing through. This is the cascade's stage two and three, on schedule. Stage four — the protein adjustment — should surface in the April or May releases.

M · 03

Monetary policy faces a harder choice.

The disinflation gave the Central Bank room to consider easing the tight monetary stance that helped produce it. A re-acceleration delays that consideration. Whether the next MPR decision tilts toward holding, easing modestly, or tightening further depends on whether the April and May prints continue the March direction or reverse it. The framework does not predict the decision; it names the indicators the decision will be read against.

M · 04

The household and operator implications are immediate.

The conditions that the pillar's § 04 names as exposing households and operators are still in place. A re-acceleration tightens the squeeze on thin-margin paths, raises the importance of the buffer rule the system's S/06 sibling unfolds, and confirms — for any reader who needed confirmation — that the structural mechanisms documented across the cluster did not disappear during the disinflation window. They were dormant. They are now operating.

05How to read the next print

Three indicators worth more than the headline.

The framework's contribution is not to tell the reader what the next inflation figure will be — no honest source can. The contribution is to give the reader the analytical kit to read it well when it arrives. Three indicators matter more than the headline number itself.

The food sub-index trajectory. If food inflation continues to rise faster than headline in April and May, the cascade is fully operative and stage four (proteins) is feeding through. If food stabilises while headline edges higher, the move is being driven by core categories — wages, rent, services — and the diagnostic is structurally different.

The core inflation level. Core moving above 16 percent and staying there is the framework's clearest signal that the disinflation was tactical rather than structural — that the underlying price pressure was always going to surface once the food and energy tailwinds eased. Core falling back below 14 percent in subsequent prints would be the framework's clearest signal that the March move was an outlier.

The transport contribution. If the NBS continues to name transport as a material driver in successive releases, the fuel-to-food cascade is firing on its standard timeline. If transport drops out of the named contributors, the cascade is winding down and the next several prints should reflect the unwind.

The reader who tracks these three lines for the next two prints will know more about the actual state of Nigerian inflation than ninety percent of the commentary that will be published between now and then.

A headline figure tells you one thing. The shape of three sub-indices tells you what is happening.
06FAQ

Common questions this post keeps getting asked.

Q · 01

What is the current inflation rate in Nigeria?

Headline inflation rose to 15.38 percent in March 2026, according to the National Bureau of Statistics — up from 15.06 percent in February 2026, which had been the lowest reading since November 2020. Food inflation was 14.31 percent and core inflation 16.21 percent in the same release. The figure is volatile; the most recent NBS CPI report is always the right reference.

Q · 02

Why did inflation rise in March 2026?

The National Bureau of Statistics named food, restaurants and accommodation, and transport as the largest contributors to the March 2026 increase. The pattern matches a fuel-driven transmission cycle that began earlier in the quarter and worked its way through the system on the standard lag — the cascade that S/01 documents in detail, firing again after the disinflation pause.

Q · 03

Is the disinflation trend over?

One re-acceleration does not establish a new trend. The eleven months of disinflation preceding March 2026 were a real structural improvement, driven by tight monetary policy, FX stabilisation, and base effects from the 2024 peaks. A single 0.32 percentage point uptick is consistent with either continued disinflation or a regime change. The April and May 2026 prints will tell the story; until then, the framework's position is that the trend is paused, not broken.

Q · 04

What is the difference between headline, food, and core inflation?

Headline inflation is the all-items consumer price index — the figure most often cited in news coverage. Food inflation tracks the food sub-basket alone, which carries the heaviest weight in the Nigerian consumer basket. Core inflation strips out food and energy to show the underlying price pressure in everything else. Each tells a different part of the story; reading only headline misses the structural movement underneath.

Q · 05

Should I expect inflation to keep rising in 2026?

The framework does not predict the next print, and any source that does is offering speculation rather than analysis. What the framework can say is that the structural transmission mechanism — fuel to transport to food — is intact and currently active, and that the next several monthly releases will show whether March 2026 was a single-month re-acceleration or the start of a broader cycle. Watch the food sub-basket and the transport contribution; those are the indicators that move first.

Q · 06

How does inflation affect my savings in Nigeria?

Real returns are nominal returns minus inflation. A naira savings instrument paying 18 percent gross delivers about 2.6 percent in real terms at March 2026's headline figure — positive, but thin. The same instrument at the 27 percent inflation peak of 2024 was meaningfully negative. The savings architecture the system's S/07 sibling unfolds treats this question in instrument-level detail, with attention to the choice between naira and dollar holdings as a function of the operator's actual obligations.
