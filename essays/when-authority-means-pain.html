When Authority Means Pain

School punishment and the questions carried into adulthood.

By Paul A. Magaji · 7 min read · Who gets room to attempt

Personal essay

Download Word document

In secondary school, I was beaten in the name of discipline.

I remember the pain. I also remember what it demonstrated: somebody had the power to impose it on me.

When I return to that experience now, I find questions extending beyond the punishment itself. What was I expected to learn? What did I actually learn? How did being subject to force enter my understanding of authority?

I cannot reconstruct every occasion or claim that this experience explains everything I later became. I can say that it belongs to the history of my questions about power.

Before I had a developed language for those questions, I had experienced them physically.

***

“Discipline” was the explanation given to what happened.

But naming the intention does not settle the meaning of the experience. The person administering punishment may describe a lesson in conduct. The person receiving it has to make sense of being hurt by someone entitled, or assumed to be entitled, to act.

I want to preserve the distinction between those accounts.

What did the adult think was being taught? What did the pupil understand about the conduct at issue? Could the pupil ask a question, offer an explanation, or challenge an error? What happened when the authority was mistaken?

I have not investigated enough individual experiences to answer these questions for everyone. They are the questions I bring to my own recollection.

The pain was immediate. Understanding the lesson requires more examination.

***

One distinction matters especially to me: compliance and understanding are different achievements.

A person can stop an action because he understands why it is wrong. He can also stop because he expects pain. From the outside, the immediate behaviour may look similar.

The difference becomes clearer when the threat is absent, when circumstances change, or when judgment is required rather than obedience to an instruction.

If I want someone to exercise responsibility, I need to ask what kind of learning prepares him for that task. Can he explain the reason for a rule? Can he recognise when a situation differs from the familiar example? Can he give an account of his decision?

These questions do not eliminate the need for standards or consequences. They require me to be precise about what the standards and consequences are meant to accomplish.

Pain cannot answer that question on its own.

***

As I grew older, I became interested in stories about pressure and control.

I watched 24, with Kiefer Sutherland as Jack Bauer, and The Blacklist. Part of what drew my attention was the struggle over what could make a person act.

What could be used against someone? What did he need to protect? What happened when somebody else gained control of it?

I was watching fiction. A television story is arranged for dramatic effect; it cannot establish how people or institutions generally behave. But it can reveal the questions a viewer brings to it.

I brought an interest in leverage.

The physical imposition of pain was one form of pressure. Control over access, possessions, or something a person depended upon presented other possibilities. I wanted to understand the relationship between those pressures and the choices someone could actually make.

***

That interest connected with my thinking about a livelihood.

As a boy, I had feared the difficulty of life without a government job. I wanted the dependability of a salary from which to meet responsibilities.

Later, I became interested in enterprise and productive independence. Yet wealth did not seem entirely secure to me either. I worried about who might interfere with what a person had built.

These were fears I carried. They were not findings established through research. Still, they shaped the questions I asked.

I wanted to know how someone could provide for himself and retain meaningful control over his life. What arrangements would make that possible? What would happen when a relationship failed or someone more powerful made a demand?

Remembering school punishment does not answer those questions. It helps explain why I have wanted to ask them so persistently.

***

I must be careful here.

It would be too easy to treat every authority as a version of the person who could hurt me at school. That would turn one kind of experience into an explanation of things it cannot adequately describe.

Authority can involve teaching, coordination, protection, and the resolution of disputes. People can have legitimate responsibilities for decisions that affect others.

The question is how those responsibilities are exercised and examined.

I want to know the scope of the person's power, the reasons for a decision, and what happens when someone affected believes it is wrong. I also want to understand whether the institution can recognise and correct its own mistakes.

These questions allow distinctions that fear alone cannot supply.

They help me ask about a particular exercise of authority rather than assume I already know its meaning.

***

There is also a question for the person who acquires authority.

If I object to being made powerless, what happens when I occupy a position from which I can make another person feel that way?

My own recollection does not guarantee my fairness. I would still have to examine how I respond to disagreement, delay, error, or an explanation I do not want to hear.

Do I want the person to understand the task, or merely to show submission? Am I willing to explain my judgment? Could I admit that I had misunderstood?

These are uncomfortable questions because they return the inquiry to conduct I can influence.

An account of power that scrutinises only what others do to me would remain incomplete. I also need standards for what I do when someone else depends upon my decision.

***

This is part of what I want NaijaOS to make discussable.

When we investigate how a process works, we should ask what the person passing through it can understand and do. Does he know what is required? Can he find out why a decision was made? Is there a meaningful way to raise an error?

Those are questions to investigate in a particular setting. I should not assume that the answers are identical in a school, workplace, public office, or private association.

The differences are precisely what an inquiry should reveal.

I would also want to hear how the people exercising authority understand their responsibilities. What constraints do they face? What do they believe counts as a good outcome? How do they distinguish an explanation from an excuse?

Listening to that account does not require accepting every justification. It gives the inquiry something more concrete to examine.

***

I have begun to think that a useful test of authority is what it enables a person to become capable of doing.

After an encounter with it, does someone better understand his responsibilities? Can he perform the task more competently? Does he know how to correct a mistake?

Or has he chiefly learned to anticipate the authority's reaction?

I offer these as questions, not a complete measure of every institution. Some responsibilities involve urgent action; some require consequences for serious misconduct. Context matters.

But context should make the explanation more specific. It should not make explanation unnecessary.

If the stated purpose is learning, I want to examine what is learned. If the stated purpose is responsibility, I want to examine how responsibility is developed.

***

I cannot now speak for the boy in every detail. I can remember that he was hurt, and I can recognise the questions that remain.

I do not need to turn the experience into an account in which nothing changed afterwards. I discovered other ways to think about usefulness, work, and independence. I encountered people and ideas that widened what seemed possible.

But examining those beginnings is part of understanding the direction of my inquiry.

I want to understand power well enough to distinguish its forms, question its claims, and judge my own conduct when I exercise it.

At school, pain made authority impossible to ignore.

As an adult, I want authority to be possible to question.
