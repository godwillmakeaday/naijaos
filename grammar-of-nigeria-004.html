The Grammar of Nigeria / Essay 004

Republic and Democracy: Why Elections Are Not the Whole of Democracy

If a people choose their rulers, have they also secured the right to govern themselves?

Paul MagajiLong-form civic education3,272 words

On election morning, a citizen may travel across a town, stand in the sun, wait in a queue, present identification, and cast a vote. For that moment, the citizen participates in an extraordinary political act: helping determine who shall exercise public authority.

But consider what happens the next morning. The ballot has been cast. A result is eventually declared. Officials assume office. Laws are proposed, budgets are prepared, contracts are awarded, public institutions make decisions, and people live with the consequences.

Where, in all of this, is democracy? Was it present only at the polling unit, or must it also be present in the long interval before the next election?

This is not a rhetorical dismissal of elections. Elections matter enormously. The peaceful possibility of replacing a government without violence is one of the most valuable political achievements of constitutional democracy. Yet something becomes distorted when a society treats the electoral event as the complete meaning of democratic life.

An election may answer who is authorised to govern. It does not, by itself, answer how power ought to be exercised, limited, questioned, and corrected.

To understand Nigeria, we must distinguish two words that are often spoken together: republic and democracy. Then we must ask what constitutional government demands after the votes are counted.

1. A republic is not the same thing as a democracy

The word republic comes from the Latin res publica—roughly, the public affair or commonwealth. Its meaning has developed over centuries, but in modern constitutional usage a republic commonly denotes a political order in which the head of state is not a hereditary monarch and public offices are constituted under law.

That is not precisely the same question answered by democracy. Democracy concerns the source, exercise, distribution, and control of public power, especially the political standing and participation of the people.

It is possible to have a republic that is not genuinely democratic. An unelected elite may dominate public life even while the country has a president rather than a king. It is also possible for a constitutional monarchy to be democratic, if governing power is accountable to the people through representative institutions and constitutional rules.

Therefore, the absence of a monarch does not establish the presence of popular control. A republic is not made democratic merely by calling its chief executive a president.

Nigeria’s constitutional name is the Federal Republic of Nigeria. That name tells us something important about its political form, but not everything about its democratic performance. To understand that performance, we must look beyond the title to the institutions, freedoms, and practices by which authority is exercised.

2. Democracy is more than a moment of authorisation

Modern democratic theory offers several approaches to the meaning of democracy. Some focus on electoral competition and the procedures through which citizens choose leaders. Others place greater emphasis on equal political standing, rights, public reasoning, participation, and the ability to hold rulers accountable.

These approaches are not always rivals. They illuminate different dimensions of the same political undertaking. A useful starting point, adopted by International IDEA, is that democracy involves both popular control over public decision-making and political equality among those entitled to exercise that control.

Elections are a principal method of authorisation. They create opportunities to choose among alternatives and, where procedures are credible, to withdraw political support. But their democratic value depends on the circumstances under which they take place: genuine competition, adequate information, meaningful political rights, fair procedures, and the possibility of an uncertain outcome.

Even a highly credible election does not guarantee that every subsequent public decision will be lawful, wise, fair, or responsive. Those are different inquiries. Citizens may have selected an officeholder without consenting in advance to every act that officeholder later undertakes.

The distinction is simple but transformative: electoral success confers a mandate to exercise an office within the law, not unrestricted discretion to do whatever an officeholder wishes.

3. What does the Nigerian Constitution actually establish?

The Constitution gives us a disciplined way to examine these ideas. Section 1 declares the supremacy of the Constitution, binds public authorities and persons to it, and forbids the taking of governmental control except in accordance with its provisions. Section 14(1) describes the Federal Republic of Nigeria as a state based on the principles of democracy and social justice.

Section 14(2)(a) declares that sovereignty belongs to the people of Nigeria, from whom government derives its powers and authority through the Constitution. Section 14(2)(b) identifies the security and welfare of the people as the primary purpose of government. Section 14(2)(c) speaks of participation by the people in their government in accordance with the Constitution.

These provisions answer related but distinguishable questions. The sovereignty clause concerns the source of legitimate political authority. Constitutional supremacy concerns the controlling legal framework. The principles of security, welfare, and participation describe the purposes and democratic aspirations of governance.

A legal qualification is necessary. Section 14 belongs to Chapter II, on Fundamental Objectives and Directive Principles of State Policy. Section 6(6)(c) generally restricts judicial inquiry into conformity with Chapter II except as otherwise provided by the Constitution. Accordingly, not every political failure to meet a Chapter II objective automatically gives an individual a directly enforceable courtroom claim. Some objectives may acquire legal effect through other constitutional or legislative provisions.

It would therefore be a mistake to confuse a constitutional ideal with proof of its implementation, or to assume that every declaration of principle operates identically to a justiciable right. Yet it would be an equal mistake to dismiss these objectives as politically meaningless. They supply important standards for evaluating the constitutional direction of government.

4. A majority cannot own the republic

Suppose a political party wins a clear majority of votes and parliamentary seats. What follows? Its candidates may assume the offices to which the law entitles them, and it may have the votes required to pursue a legislative programme. But does it thereby acquire ownership of the state, the treasury, the courts, the civil service, or the rights of those who voted against it?

It does not. Majority decision-making helps resolve a problem that every large political community faces: people disagree, and public decisions cannot always await unanimity. But democratic majorities operate within constitutional arrangements that protect political competition and constrain the means by which authority is exercised.

If a victorious majority could silence its critics, suppress lawful opposition, control every independent institution, and make the next election meaningless, it would be able to destroy the conditions under which its own democratic authority was justified.

This is the paradox of unlimited majoritarianism: power obtained through an election can be used to undermine the possibility of meaningful future elections.

Constitutional rights, institutional independence, judicial review, and opposition freedoms are therefore not merely obstacles to elected leadership. They can be essential safeguards for continuing democratic government.

The question for Nigerians is not whether majority decisions should matter. Of course they should. It is whether a majority should be understood as the temporary holder of public authority or as the permanent proprietor of the political community. Those are radically different ideas.

5. Three clocks of democratic government

Imagine democracy operating through three clocks. Each measures a different kind of political time.

The electoral clock: Who may govern?

This clock measures contests, choices, election timetables, results, challenges, and lawful transitions. It prevents a government from claiming an indefinite entitlement to power simply because it once won public support.

The governing clock: How is power being used today?

This clock measures what happens between elections: implementation, service delivery, spending, appointments, legislative oversight, public complaints, and responses to changing conditions. Citizens experience this clock more continuously than they experience polling day.

The constitutional clock: What may not be done even with public support?

This clock measures the continuing authority of constitutional rules, rights, procedures, and institutional restraints. An election takes place at a particular moment; constitutional obligations do not sleep until the next election.

The clocks must work together. Elections without accountability between contests are thin democracy. Everyday administrative performance without electoral choice may produce efficient government but not necessarily popular self-government. Constitutional rules without political participation may become formal promises without sufficient public life to sustain them.

6. The constitutional machine: Dividing power so that it can be questioned

Nigeria’s Constitution does not place every function of government in a single office. Section 4 deals with legislative powers, Section 5 with executive powers, and Section 6 with judicial powers. Their functions interact, and the precise boundaries can be legally complex, but the structure invites a basic question: who should be able to question whom?

A legislature does more than pass the proposals of the executive. It debates and enacts law, considers public finance, and exercises constitutionally assigned oversight. An executive must do more than announce popular aspirations: it administers public authority under the law. Courts have a responsibility to decide properly brought disputes within their jurisdiction, including questions concerning legal limits on power.

The existence of separate institutions is not proof that they are functioning independently. Nor is every disagreement between branches of government evidence that a system is failing. Some disagreements show that power is being examined rather than silently accepted.

The better question is whether institutions can perform their assigned functions with legality, competence, impartiality, and sufficient independence. How do we know whether legislative oversight is serious? Whether public accounts are intelligible? Whether the courts can resolve disputes without improper pressure? Whether officials provide lawful explanations for consequential decisions?

These are democratic questions even when no election is underway.

7. The citizen between elections

A citizen who votes and then remains politically powerless for four years has exercised one important democratic freedom, but not the whole range of freedoms needed for effective citizenship.

Public reasoning, peaceful association, lawful criticism, access to information, participation in local discussions, petitions, civic organisations, professional bodies, community meetings, and scrutiny of budgets can all contribute to political accountability. The Constitution’s protections for expression and peaceful assembly and association, in Sections 39 and 40, are relevant here, subject to their constitutional limits.

Yet citizens also need analytical discipline. An allegation against a public official is not automatically evidence. A project announcement is not proof of completed work. A budgetary allocation is not necessarily the same as a cash release, and a cash release is not proof of delivery. A failure of service may involve national, state, local, or shared responsibilities.

The democratic citizen must therefore learn to distinguish anger from investigation and suspicion from demonstrated fact. Political loyalty can produce selective blindness; political hostility can do the same.

Meaningful participation should widen our capacity to ask accurate questions, not merely increase the volume of public accusation.

8. Why political opposition is not an interruption of democracy

In ordinary competition, a winner would naturally prefer that the loser cease to interfere. But democratic politics is different. Today’s opposition may become tomorrow’s government; today’s majority may become tomorrow’s minority.

For this reason, the continuing existence of lawful opposition is part of the architecture of democratic choice. It gives citizens alternatives, exposes arguments to criticism, and helps make future electoral contests meaningful.

This principle does not mean that an opposition politician is always correct, that every protest is justified, or that every criticism is offered in good faith. Opposition must also meet standards of truthfulness, legality, and responsibility.

But the right to challenge public authority cannot depend on whether the government regards the challenger as agreeable. Otherwise, the government becomes the judge of the legitimacy of criticism directed against itself.

Democracy cannot require unanimity. The relevant aspiration is a political order in which disagreement is handled without making dissenters strangers to the republic.

9. The rules of elections can change; the civic questions remain

Election law is not static. Nigeria enacted the Electoral Act, 2026, on 18 February 2026, repealing and replacing the Electoral Act, 2022. Rules for registration, political parties, voting, results management, offences, and election petitions must therefore be checked against the current law and authoritative electoral guidance rather than inferred from an older statute.

That legal change offers a useful lesson for civic reasoning. It is possible to support an electoral reform and still ask whether its operational safeguards are adequate. It is also possible to criticise a reform without assuming that every participant supporting it acts in bad faith.

A serious democratic assessment asks: What precisely does the law require? Which institution must implement it? How will compliance be verified? What remedies exist for breach? And what evidence would show that the new rule improved electoral integrity?

These questions direct us beyond slogans about election technology or election fairness toward rules, institutions, evidence, and outcomes.

10. A Nigerian example: The school that was promised

Consider a fictional constituency where an elected official promises to renovate a public secondary school. Two years later, the roof still leaks. Supporters insist that the official has done everything possible. Critics declare that the official has stolen the money. The community is divided before anyone has established the facts.

A more rigorous inquiry begins with jurisdiction. Which government level owns or operates the school? Which office has legal or administrative responsibility for the project? Was there a documented appropriation, contract, allocation, or merely a campaign promise? If funding was approved, was it released? If released, to whom? Were procurement and project records published? What work was actually performed?

The answers may reveal a broken promise, an administrative delay, a procurement problem, a funding gap, a mistaken attribution of responsibility, or genuine misconduct. Each possibility calls for different evidence and a different remedy.

At the next election, voters can judge the performance of the relevant officeholders. But they need not wait until then to investigate public records, seek explanations, use lawful complaint channels, or ask representatives to exercise oversight.

This is democracy translated into a practical method of inquiry. It neither excuses the official nor convicts the official before the evidence is gathered.

11. A seven-question democratic audit

A Nigerian who wishes to examine a government, public institution, or political controversy can begin with a simple framework:

  • 1. Authorisation: By what lawful process did this person or institution obtain authority?  2. Choice: Were citizens given genuine, intelligible alternatives and meaningful opportunities to participate?  3. Law: What constitutional or statutory limits govern the action being examined?  4. Voice: Can affected citizens criticise, organise, and present their concerns without unlawful interference?  5. Answerability: Who must explain the decision, publish relevant information, or account for resources?  6. Evidence: What verifiable facts demonstrate performance, non-performance, or misconduct?  7. Correction: Which lawful process can review, reverse, sanction, or improve a mistaken decision?

These questions do not guarantee agreement. They allow citizens who disagree to test claims against common categories, identify missing evidence, and distinguish political judgments from factual conclusions. That is why they are suitable for an interactive instrument within NaijaOS.

12. The limits of democracy—and why those limits matter

Democracy is not a promise that every public decision will be popular, that every representative will be wise, or that every collective choice will be morally correct. A democratic government may make a necessary but unpopular decision. A majority may support an unjust measure. A court may issue a lawful decision that many citizens dislike.

It would be naïve to suggest that voting converts preference into truth. The majority can determine who wins an election, but the majority cannot determine a scientific fact simply by counting ballots. Nor can the popularity of an official replace the need for legal authority.

The strength of constitutional democracy lies partly in admitting that popular will needs institutions, deliberation, rights, evidence, and legal restraints. Its aspiration is not perfect rulers, but a political arrangement in which authority can be conferred, examined, contested, and peacefully transferred.

The republican principle adds a further challenge: public power should be exercised as a public trust, not as private property. A public officeholder may lead, but should not mistake the office for a personal possession or the state for an extension of a party.

In this sense, the most instructive test of a political movement may not be what it says about democracy before taking power. It may be how it treats criticism, institutions, legal restraints, and future competition once it has acquired power.

Conclusion: The ballot begins a responsibility it does not finish

Nigeria needs elections that citizens can meaningfully participate in and whose rules can be trusted. But it also needs citizens capable of examining how public power is used between elections, what the Constitution permits, and whether institutions make government answerable.

A republic reminds us that public authority is not a hereditary private estate. Democracy reminds us that those who are governed must have a meaningful place in authorising and controlling that authority. Constitutionalism reminds us that power remains subject to law even when its holders are popular.

These ideas overlap, but they are not interchangeable. An election is a method of selecting and changing officeholders; it is not a ceremonial transfer of ownership over the republic.

A society has not completed the work of democracy when it has counted the votes. It has begun the continuing work of keeping power public.

The practical task is therefore not to teach Nigerians to applaud elections or despise them. It is to give Nigerians the intellectual tools to judge elections, officeholders, institutions, public claims, and the uses of power with greater precision.

The next time we ask whether Nigeria is democratic, we should not begin and end with the question of who won. We should also ask how the winner governs, whether the loser remains free to compete, whether citizens can question decisions, and whether mistakes can be corrected under law.

That is where the republic and democracy meet: not in the silence after an election, but in the continued public accountability of those who govern.

Questions for the reader

1. Can a government be lawfully elected and still behave undemocratically? What evidence would establish your answer?

2. If an elected majority seeks to weaken the institutions that could restrain it, is resistance to that action anti-democratic?

3. What should remain beyond the control of a temporary electoral majority?

4. Which Nigerian institution, if any, can be meaningfully evaluated without examining what it does between elections?

5. What makes political opposition legitimate, and what responsibilities should opposition parties themselves bear?

6. How would you distinguish an unfulfilled campaign promise from an illegal act or proven corruption?

7. If you could investigate only one public decision in your community, which documents would you need before reaching a conclusion?

8. Which matters more for democracy: the ability to replace a government or the ability to hold it accountable while it governs? Can the two be separated?
