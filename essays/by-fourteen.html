By Fourteen I Had a Theory of Nigeria

How childhood expectations shape the possibilities we notice.

By Paul A. Magaji · 6 min read · How a world becomes visible

Personal essay

Download Word document

I was perhaps thirteen or fourteen when I began to think that life could become terribly difficult if I did not get a government job.

I did not understand the economy. I had not investigated the range of livelihoods available to me. But I already had a conclusion about the kind of future that might be safe.

A salary would arrive. There would be something from which to feed children and meet responsibilities. Without it, I imagined uncertainty.

I cannot now reconstruct every influence that produced this belief. I cannot name one person as its author. What I remember is the conviction itself, formed while I was still young enough for adults to regard much of my thinking as unfinished.

It was unfinished. It was also consequential.

***

When we discuss a child's future, we often speak as though the important choices lie entirely ahead. The child will grow, finish school, discover an interest, and decide what to become.

But what happens before the decision?

By the time someone considers a profession, he may already have assumptions about which lives are respectable, which incomes are dependable, and which ambitions invite trouble. He may have learned what counts as success without ever having been asked what he thinks success should mean.

In my own case, I had begun to connect security with a particular source of employment. That connection narrowed the question. Instead of asking how I might create a dependable livelihood, I was anxious about whether I would secure the kind I already recognised.

This is what I mean when I say that, by fourteen, I had a theory of Nigeria.

It was a child's working explanation of where safety could be found. I carried it into a future much larger than the experience from which I had assembled it.

***

I want to be careful about how I judge that explanation now.

The adults around a child may have serious reasons for recommending caution. They have obligations, histories, disappointments, and knowledge the child does not possess. A recommendation to seek stable employment can be an expression of care.

Nor is there anything inadequate about public service as an ambition. The problem I am examining is the fear that one familiar route is the only route through which a responsible life becomes possible.

Once that fear takes hold, other possibilities may appear irresponsible before they have even been considered.

An enterprise may become visible chiefly as a chance to lose money. Learning an unfamiliar skill may look like a distraction from the recognised path. Someone building slowly may seem to be doing nothing because the work has not yet produced a familiar title or salary.

I recognise this as a danger in my own thinking. I do not need to claim that every family behaves this way to take it seriously.

***

My early understanding of authority also included punishment.

In secondary school, I was beaten in the name of discipline. I remember how force could be used against me and how little room there was, in that moment, to negotiate with it.

I would not turn that experience into a complete explanation of my adult character. But when I examine the questions that later interested me, I cannot simply leave it out.

I became curious about how people are compelled to act. Pain was one means. The control of something a person needed was another. Dependence could give someone leverage even without physical force.

Those questions were part of what drew my attention in stories such as 24 and The Blacklist. I watched people discover what could be used against them and struggle to recover the ability to choose.

Looking back, I can see why a dependable livelihood mattered to me in ways that exceeded income. I wanted to understand how a person could provide for himself while retaining some power over his own life.

***

Yet a child's theory need not remain his adult conclusion.

A friend helped me begin thinking more deliberately about productivity and usefulness. I worked on the farm. Later, the same friend introduced me to Success Digest.

The magazine showed me that someone could assemble useful information and sell it. Learning could lead to a product. An initiative could begin with a problem that another person needed help solving.

This gave me a different kind of question to ask. What could I learn well enough to make useful?

I became absorbed in that world. Later still, I discovered software businesses and a startup economy that had developed beyond much of my attention. I began learning programming and finding further possibilities I had not previously understood.

There was no single moment in which every old belief disappeared. I was encountering examples that my earlier picture could not adequately explain.

That is an important distinction for me. A new idea can sound attractive while leaving an old fear intact. Actual encounters give the fear something more specific to answer.

***

I have also become wary of replacing one certainty with another.

It would be easy to look back on my childhood anxiety and conclude that the answer is simply to become more adventurous. But an unfamiliar venture still has costs. An obligation does not disappear because an ambition is exciting.

The adult task is to investigate more carefully than the child could.

If I believe a course of action is impossible, what exactly prevents it? Is the obstacle money, knowledge, access, time, permission, or demand? What evidence supports my judgment? Could I learn something through an attempt small enough for me to afford?

Those questions do not guarantee success. They make a conclusion more accountable.

They also allow a different response to caution. Someone warning me about an idea may identify a real weakness. I can use that information to improve the attempt. But a warning should eventually be examined in relation to the particular thing I am proposing.

Otherwise, “be realistic” can become an instruction to stop looking.

***

This is one of the reasons I want NaijaOS to examine how Nigerians understand possibility.

An account of institutions is incomplete if it never asks how people learn to approach them. What does someone expect before entering an office, proposing an enterprise, seeking a customer, or learning a new field? What experiences produced those expectations?

My own story gives me a place to begin. It does not entitle me to assume that everyone else has lived it.

I would want to hear from people whose childhood expectations were different: someone who grew up around workshops, someone whose family understood trade, someone who saw professional work closely, and someone who had to discover all of these possibilities much later.

What could each person see? What did each take for granted? What had to happen before another kind of life became imaginable?

These are questions worth asking alongside questions about skills and resources.

***

I still understand the boy who wanted a government job because he feared the alternative.

He was thinking about responsibility. He was trying to find a way to become an adult who could provide. There is dignity in that concern.

What he lacked was enough knowledge to see the full range of arrangements through which people might build a dependable life. I am still acquiring that knowledge.

I cannot go back and give him everything I have since encountered. I can examine the conclusions he left me. Some may deserve to remain. Others need to change as the evidence changes.

And I can remember, when speaking to a young person, that a theory may already be forming behind the questions he asks.

By fourteen, I had a theory of Nigeria.

Much of my adult learning has involved finding out where it was incomplete.
