A Factory Can Fit in One Room. An Industrial System Cannot.

Aba and the productive relationships beyond the workshop walls.

By Paul A. Magaji · 7 min read · Building productive capability

During a conversation with a client from Abia, I described my interest in the idea that a productive business could begin in a single room.

She told me that this was already familiar in Aba. In her account, a small space could contain the work of making shoes for sale. What I was discussing as a possibility elsewhere was something she recognised at home.

Her comparison was striking: Aba was like China.

I take that comparison as an invitation to investigate, rather than a literal equivalence. Neither a Nigerian city nor a country as large and varied as China can be explained by one image. But the image of the room stayed with me.

What makes a small room capable of supporting production?

The tempting answer is the determination of the person inside it. That matters. Yet it cannot explain everything the person needs to obtain, learn, use and sell.

A workshop has walls. Its productive relationships extend beyond them.

What the room leaves out

Imagine a small shoemaking workshop. This is an illustration of the questions to investigate, rather than a description of a particular business I have inspected.

The producer needs suitable materials and tools. Some operations may require equipment he does not own. He needs someone who can repair a machine, customers who will accept the product and a way to move finished goods to them.

He also needs knowledge. How should the materials be cut? Which adhesive suits the task? Why did a sole separate? How can a pair be reproduced consistently?

Some of those answers can be written down. Others are learned by doing the work beside someone who already knows.

If all these resources are accessible nearby, a small workshop may draw on capabilities much larger than its own premises. If they are absent, the owner must spend time and money assembling them separately.

The room may look the same in both places. The practical difficulty of producing inside it will be different.

What the research establishes

The March 2018 Made in Aba Cluster Mapping Report examined garment and leather-goods production through research, observation, surveys and interviews. Its findings describe a concentration of small producers, with apprenticeship serving as an important route through which knowledge and innovation spread. It also identified constraints involving power, transport, finance and standardisation. [1]

The date matters. This is evidence about the period studied, rather than a measurement of Aba’s present conditions. The report also acknowledged weaknesses in production measurement and sector data. It cannot support every large claim made about the city.

It does, however, help us ask a better question.

Instead of wondering only how an exceptional individual manages to produce in a small space, we can examine how a concentration of businesses makes particular kinds of production possible.

This shifts attention towards the connections between enterprises: who teaches whom, who supplies what, where particular tasks are performed and how customers reach the producers.

The entrepreneur and the surrounding system

There is a way of praising enterprise that makes its supporting conditions disappear.

We see the finished product and admire the person who made it. We may overlook the people who trained him, sold him materials, repaired his tools or brought the buyer.

Recognising these contributions does not reduce his achievement. It gives us a more accurate account of what the achievement required.

This is particularly important when we want to reproduce success somewhere else.

Telling a young person in another town to be as enterprising as an Aba producer may leave him with a task that advice alone cannot solve. Where does he obtain materials? Who can correct his mistakes? What happens when his equipment fails? Who will purchase the first order?

The useful lesson may involve organising access to those things.

We should investigate the enterprise and its surroundings together.

Small beginnings and the conditions for growth

A small workshop can be an appropriate beginning. It may allow a producer to learn, test demand and avoid commitments he cannot yet sustain.

But remaining small can also reflect a constraint.

A business may be unable to accept a larger order because working capital is insufficient. Its products may vary because it lacks suitable equipment or a reliable process. It may have customers but struggle to deliver at an agreed time.

These are possible explanations to test, not conditions we should assume apply to every workshop.

The distinction matters because an observer can mistake endurance for preference. A producer who works around a problem may still want the problem removed.

We should ask what the business owner wants to become and what is preventing the next useful step. More floor space might help. In another case, access to one specialised machine, dependable materials or better scheduling might matter more.

Growth should be assessed through useful results: consistent quality, reliable delivery, safer work and the ability to meet obligations. A larger building does not establish those results by itself.

The promise and difficulty of shared facilities

The Nigerian Export Promotion Council describes a Common Facility Centre for leather and leather products in Aba, established through a joint programme involving NEPC, UNIDO, the federal trade ministry and the Abia State Government. Its stated benefits include skills acquisition and access to modern technology. [2]

That is an institutional response to a practical problem: some capabilities may be more accessible when several enterprises can use them.

The existence of a centre, however, is only the beginning of an assessment. Its current operation and results would require separate verification.

Can producers obtain appointments when needed? Are the machines maintained? Are fees understandable? Who pays for repairs? Does the service improve the work enough to justify the cost of using it?

These questions belong at the centre of the project.

A shared facility is an ongoing service. Someone must organise access, keep equipment usable, account for revenue and resolve competing demands. Funding the equipment without organising that work leaves a substantial part of the undertaking unfinished.

This is one place where governance becomes visible in the quality and timing of an ordinary commercial transaction.

Learning to make and learning to originate

My client’s account also concerned the ability to recreate products.

Reproduction can demonstrate practical skill. But the ability to make an object, the right to use a particular design or brand, and the ability to develop an original product are distinct questions.

The next ambition should therefore be examined carefully. What would help producers develop designs of their own? How would they learn what customers value? Could they test durability, improve consistency and earn recognition under identities they control?

Those questions should not erase existing craftsmanship. They ask how craftsmanship can acquire more commercial independence.

Nor should we assume that every enterprise must perform every function itself. Design, production, distribution and customer service can be organised across different businesses. What matters is whether those relationships allow the participants to improve their work and receive a sustainable return.

What NaijaOS should investigate

The one-room workshop offers NaijaOS a concrete way to study possibility.

Begin with a product. Trace what is required to make and sell it. Identify which capabilities sit inside the enterprise and which are obtained elsewhere. Then ask where the process becomes expensive, uncertain or unreliable.

This can reveal an opportunity for a producer. It can also reveal work for someone who never manufactures the final object: supplying materials, maintaining tools, organising transport or providing a dependable specialist service.

It also puts national pride on firmer ground.

Calling Aba “our China” expresses admiration. Understanding how a producer there actually completes an order gives us something we can learn from, improve and perhaps adapt elsewhere.

My client’s remark began with a room. The investigation has to follow the work beyond its walls.

A factory can fit in one room. The question is what must exist around that room for the person inside it to keep producing well.
