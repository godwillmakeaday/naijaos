Why We Celebrate Wealth and Discourage the Attempt

Why uncertain beginnings can attract less support than finished success.

By Paul A. Magaji · 7 min read · Who gets room to attempt

Personal essay

Download Word document

There is a contradiction I have struggled to understand.

I have encountered people who admire someone who has prospered, yet resist an unfamiliar attempt by someone close to them. The established success receives recognition. The uncertain beginning attracts warnings.

I felt this tension while trying to move beyond the expectations I had grown up with. I wanted to become productive and independent. I also wanted the security that earning through useful work might provide.

Yet departing from a familiar route could itself become a source of concern.

I found myself asking: if we admire what a successful person has built, how do we respond to someone whose work has not yet become a success?

***

I should begin by limiting the claim.

This is an observation from my experience, not a finding about every Nigerian family or community. Nor have I established that everyone who warned me was motivated by the same concern.

The apparent contradiction may sometimes be less mysterious than it feels.

A person admiring an established business can see an outcome. Someone listening to a proposed business has to assess a possibility. The first has survived at least some of the difficulties that the second has yet to encounter.

That difference can justify different judgments.

It cannot, by itself, justify dismissing every unfamiliar attempt. If the only enterprise we can approve is one that has already succeeded, we have left very little room for a beginning.

The difficulty lies in deciding what deserves a chance before the outcome is known.

***

My own history made the question personal.

As a boy, I thought life might be terribly difficult without a government job. I associated a salary with the ability to feed children and meet responsibilities. Dependability mattered before I had enough knowledge to examine the different ways a livelihood could be built.

Later, I became interested in enterprise. A friend helped me think about productivity and usefulness. Success Digest introduced me to the possibility of creating and selling information. Discovering software businesses opened further questions.

These encounters widened my view, but they did not remove the obligations that had made security important to me.

I could understand why an uncertain venture might worry someone. I also knew that remaining within the limits of my earlier understanding would carry consequences of its own.

There was a judgment to make between risks, including the risk of never investigating a possibility properly.

***

One possibility I want to examine is that familiar success is easier to admire than unfamiliar work is to understand.

A prosperous person may have a business whose result is visible while its beginnings remain obscure. We see what the enterprise has become. We may know little about the skills, relationships, revisions, and failed attempts that preceded it.

Then someone nearby proposes work for which there is no familiar description.

What exactly is he doing? Who will pay? Why has it not produced income yet? These can be reasonable questions. But if the listener cannot picture the work at all, uncertainty about understanding it can become certainty that it will fail.

I recognise the danger in myself as well. Before I understood much about startups, I could not adequately assess where my own legal skills might fit. My lack of a map did not establish that no route existed.

The appropriate response was further inquiry.

***

There is another distinction I need to make: admiration for prosperity does not necessarily reveal what is being admired.

Is it the quality of the work? The independence it provides? The recognition surrounding the person? His capacity to assist others?

I cannot infer the answer simply from the welcome a wealthy person receives.

If I want to investigate a community's attitude towards enterprise, I need to look more closely at how it responds to the work itself. Does anyone ask how the product is made? Do people help identify customers? Is there interest in how another person could learn the skill?

These questions would reveal something different from a public celebration of the result.

They also direct attention towards the less conspicuous forms of support that can make an attempt better: an introduction, an honest assessment, access to experience, or the time to explain an unfamiliar process.

Money is valuable, but it is not the only contribution worth recognising.

***

I must be equally careful about the person making the attempt.

It is tempting, when discouraged, to treat resistance as proof that one is doing something important. But opposition cannot establish the merit of an idea. Neither can my sincerity.

A critic may have identified a weakness I would rather avoid.

Have I understood the customer's problem? What evidence suggests that the proposed product would help? What will the attempt cost? Who would bear the loss? Which responsibilities could it affect?

These questions deserve answers even when they arrive in an unwelcome tone.

In particular, I cannot claim complete freedom to experiment while quietly expecting someone else to absorb the consequences. The person who may have to support me if the attempt fails has a stake that enthusiasm does not erase.

A serious account of enterprise must make that stake visible.

***

This suggests a more useful way to distinguish caution from discouragement.

A warning becomes useful when it identifies something that can be examined. Perhaps the costs are incomplete, the customer unclear, the necessary skill missing, or the proposed schedule implausible.

I can investigate those concerns. I may have to revise the attempt substantially or decide against it.

A general verdict that I should stop because the path is unfamiliar gives me less to work with. It leaves no clear account of what evidence could change the judgment.

I can ask myself the same question about confidence. What would make me reconsider? If nothing could, then my ambition is becoming as unaccountable as the discouragement I object to.

The standard should apply to both of us.

***

I would therefore like to change the conversation around an uncertain beginning.

Instead of asking for unconditional belief, I can explain a bounded attempt. This is the problem I want to understand. This is what I propose to make or test. This is what I can afford to commit. This is what I will examine before deciding whether to continue.

The point is to make the work assessable.

An experiment small enough to undertake responsibly may teach me that the idea needs improvement. It may also show that the opportunity is weaker than I thought. Either outcome can be useful if I pay attention.

Someone concerned about my future can participate in that examination without having to promise that I will succeed.

That is the kind of support I would want to give another person as well.

***

For NaijaOS, this becomes a question about the conditions surrounding an attempt.

We should investigate how people acquire the room to learn through doing. What support was available? Which obligations had to be protected? What mistakes could the person afford? What happened when the first version did not work?

These questions could make stories of enterprise more instructive than a simple celebration of wealth.

They could also expose my own assumptions. Perhaps a warning I experienced as rejection contained information I had not yet understood. Perhaps another reflected an expectation that needed to be challenged. I need enough detail to distinguish them.

The inquiry should preserve that openness.

***

I still feel the force of the contradiction that began this essay.

When an established success is celebrated and an emerging attempt is dismissed, I want to ask how the distance between them is supposed to be crossed.

But I also want to make my own attempt worthy of a serious response. That means explaining the work, listening carefully, protecting responsibilities, and allowing evidence to alter the plan.

We do not have to know in advance who will become wealthy to take someone's effort seriously.

We can begin by asking what they are trying to do, what it requires, and how they will find out whether it works.
