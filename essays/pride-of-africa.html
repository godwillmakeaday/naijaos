Pride of Africa: What Would We Have to Build?

What would we have to build for pride to be an everyday experience?

By Paul A. Magaji · 8 min read · Building productive capability

I like the phrase “Pride of Africa.”

When I use it, I am thinking about something we could create. There may be a distance between the words and the conditions we inhabit. I want to understand what it would take to fill that distance.

It is possible to become so familiar with disappointment that an ambitious description sounds embarrassing. We anticipate the objection before we finish speaking. Someone will point to the electricity, the roads, the unemployment or an institution that failed them.

Those objections deserve answers. But the existence of unfinished work cannot settle what we should be willing to attempt.

The question is what kind of pride could help us undertake that work honestly.

Pride before the evidence

There is a dignity that should never depend on national performance.

A person does not become less worthy because the hospital near him lacks equipment. A child does not acquire greater human value because she was born in a wealthy country. No one should have to produce an industrial achievement before being treated with respect.

Pride in an institution or a public accomplishment asks a different question. What has been done, how well does it work, and who benefits?

Confusing these questions causes trouble. We can defend people’s dignity while examining the failures of institutions that serve them. We can also admire an achievement without treating its creators as more human than anyone else.

For me, “Pride of Africa” must leave room for both convictions.

I do not need evidence before affirming that our people deserve respect. I do need evidence before claiming that a particular system deserves their confidence.

What would people experience

Suppose we wanted to build something that would give Nigerians reason for pride.

We might begin by picturing a large structure, a famous company or a technological breakthrough. Such achievements can matter. But I also want to begin with the experience of the person who needs the service.

Can a patient obtain dependable care? Can a producer complete an order? Can a student acquire a skill and demonstrate it? Can someone understand a public procedure without finding an influential person to explain the unofficial route?

These questions direct attention towards what an institution enables.

A building may be impressive on the day it opens. Its value becomes clearer through the ordinary days that follow: whether people can enter, whether equipment works, whether staff can perform their roles and whether a problem can be reported and resolved.

The pride I want would survive those ordinary days.

It would give people something more substantial than the memory of a ceremony.

The achievement beyond the exceptional person

I admire people who accomplish difficult things under difficult conditions. Their work can expand our understanding of what is possible.

Yet an exceptional person cannot be the full measure of a society’s opportunity.

If one student succeeds through extraordinary personal sacrifice, I want to understand the achievement. I also want to ask what would make a useful education accessible to the next student.

If one entrepreneur succeeds because a relative provides accommodation, finance and introductions, his work still deserves attention. So does the question of how someone without that support could begin.

This is where pride becomes connected to institution-building.

An achievement becomes more widely useful when some part of the route can be travelled by others. The knowledge can be taught. The application process can be understood. The equipment can be maintained. A person who does not know the founder can still obtain the service on fair terms.

We should be able to celebrate the pioneer and improve the path behind him.

Pride that can accept a complaint

One test of an institution is how it treats an accurate account of its shortcomings.

Imagine a service that has earned public admiration. A user then reports a serious failure. Does the organisation investigate, or does it treat the complaint as an attack on something everyone is supposed to celebrate?

If admiration makes correction difficult, success has begun to obstruct improvement.

I want a pride that can hear disappointing evidence without demanding silence from the person who brings it. A hospital should be able to acknowledge a preventable error. A business should be able to admit that a product failed. A public office should be able to correct a procedure that imposes unnecessary hardship.

The willingness to correct does not diminish the achievement. It helps preserve its usefulness.

This also requires care from critics. A specific failure should be investigated precisely. It need not become a verdict that nobody here can ever build anything worthwhile.

Praise and criticism should both help us see the work more clearly.

Who receives the benefit

A national success can be real while its benefits remain narrowly distributed.

That possibility should make us ask who can use what has been built. What does participation cost? Which places are served? Who supplies the labour? What risks do workers and surrounding communities carry?

A useful institution cannot answer every social problem. It should still be judged on the effects for which it is responsible.

For an enterprise, dependable payment to suppliers and decent treatment of workers belong in the account of success. For a training programme, what participants can actually do afterwards matters more than the number photographed at graduation.

For a public project, people outside the circle of its sponsors should be able to explain how it improves their lives.

These are practical questions about the quality of an achievement. They give pride something concrete to rest on.

The years after the founder

There is a further question I find increasingly important: what happens when the person who began the work is absent?

Some undertakings function because one individual remembers every agreement, approves every payment and repairs every relationship. His dedication may be extraordinary. It may also conceal how vulnerable the organisation remains.

A lasting achievement requires other people to learn how it works and acquire the authority to keep it working.

That means records, trained successors, clear responsibilities and resources for maintenance. It means making some decisions in ways that can be explained without referring everything back to the founder’s intentions.

A society accumulates capability when one generation’s work becomes a usable starting point for another.

If every successor must reconstruct the organisation from fragments, we repeatedly spend energy recovering what we once knew.

I would find reason for pride in a modest institution that survives this transition well.

Africa without a contest of worth

The phrase “Pride of Africa” carries a continental imagination, even when my immediate project concerns Nigeria.

I do not want it to become a competition in which our worth depends on diminishing another African society. If people elsewhere on the continent build something useful, their achievement gives us an opportunity to learn.

We should ask how they organised it, what conditions made it possible and what would need to change before a similar approach could work here.

Learning requires attention to differences. A policy cannot be transferred simply because its outcome is attractive. Its financing, personnel and surrounding institutions need examination.

The same applies when we look beyond Africa.

We can learn from Taiwan’s industrial development without pretending Nigeria could reproduce its history by announcing a semiconductor company. We can investigate a workshop in Aba without reducing a city’s productive life to a slogan about ingenuity.

Respect becomes more useful when it makes us curious about the work.

Where we could begin

The scale of the phrase should not prevent a small beginning.

We can choose one recurring difficulty and investigate it closely. Who experiences it? What currently happens? Which part could we realistically improve? What would count as a result that the user could recognise?

For someone building an enterprise, that may mean making a service dependable before expanding it. For a professional, it may mean developing a process that another person can follow competently. For a public institution, it may mean making one procedure clear and applying it consistently.

Small beginnings still need standards. We should keep records of the initial conditions, the change attempted and the result. We should be willing to discover that our proposed answer did not work.

The aim is to leave behind something useful: a better service, a tested method or knowledge that prevents the next person from repeating a mistake.

That is a contribution we can examine and build upon.

What NaijaOS should hold us to

NaijaOS began for me with questions about how Nigerians make money and what they might buy. Those questions opened into others about security, access, family support and the conditions of productive independence.

“Pride of Africa” belongs within that inquiry.

It asks what we could build that would give people greater confidence in their ability to live, work and create here. It also asks us to become precise enough to distinguish a promise from an achievement.

I would want NaijaOS to document useful work without exaggerating it, investigate failure without turning it into a theory of permanent incapacity, and make the conditions behind an achievement easier to understand.

There is room for imagination in that task. We need to be able to picture arrangements we have not yet experienced.

There must also be room for the people who will use them to tell us whether they work.

I still like the phrase “Pride of Africa.” Its unfinished quality gives me a responsibility.

What would we have to build for someone to experience those words as true in the course of an ordinary day?
